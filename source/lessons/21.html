Why fairness is a machine-learning problem

Imagine a bank that replaces its loan officers with a model trained on ten years of past decisions. The model is 88% accurate, fast and consistent. Is it fair? Accuracy alone can't tell you. If past officers turned down qualified applicants from one neighbourhood, the model has learned to do the same — automatically, at scale, and with an air of objectivity that makes it harder to challenge.

Machine learning finds patterns in data, and data records the world as it was, including its unfairness. A model doesn't need to be told anyone's ethnicity or gender to discriminate: other features — a postcode, a first name, a gap in employment — can act as proxies. This lesson gives you the vocabulary, the numbers and the habits to notice and reduce these problems.

The core idea

A model can be accurate on average while making very different kinds of mistakes for different groups of people. Fairness work means measuring those differences, deciding which ones matter for the situation, and acting on them — a job for people, informed by numbers.

What can go wrong: kinds of harm

  • Allocation harms: a resource or opportunity — a loan, a job interview, a medical referral — is withheld from some groups more than others.
  • Quality-of-service harms: the system simply works worse for some people, like face recognition or speech recognition with higher error rates for some groups.
  • Representation harms: outputs reinforce stereotypes or erase groups, such as image search or text generation that reproduces biased associations.
  • Privacy and autonomy harms: people are profiled, re-identified or manipulated using data they never agreed to share for that purpose.

Where bias comes from

Bias can enter at every step of the workflow you learned in Lesson 5:

  1. Historical bias: the world itself was unequal, so even a perfect record of it teaches the model those patterns (the Amazon hiring model, Lesson 1).
  2. Representation (sampling) bias: some groups are under-represented in the training data, so the model is less accurate for them.
  3. Measurement and label bias: the label is a proxy for what you care about. Using healthcare cost to stand for health need under-served Black patients (Obermeyer et al., 2019; Lesson 5).
  4. Aggregation bias: one model for everyone ignores real differences between groups — for example, a medical threshold that suits one population but not another.
  5. Evaluation bias: the test set or benchmark doesn't reflect the people the model will actually be used on.
  6. Deployment and feedback loops: the model's decisions change the future data. Police sent where a model predicts crime record more crime there, which "confirms" the prediction.

Measuring fairness with group metrics

Split your test set by group and compute the confusion matrix (Lesson 9) for each group separately. From those counts come the standard fairness measures:

MeasureFormula (per group)"Fair" means equal…Name of the criterion
Selection rate$\frac{TP + FP}{\text{all}}$share of people approvedDemographic parity
True-positive rate (recall)$\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$chance a qualified person is approvedEqual opportunity
TPR and false-positive rate$\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$ and $\frac{FP}{FP+TN}$both kinds of errorEqualised odds
Precision (PPV)$\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$how reliable an approval isPredictive parity

A common summary is the demographic-parity ratio: the lower selection rate divided by the higher one. In US employment law the "four-fifths rule" of thumb treats a ratio below 0.8 as evidence of possible adverse impact that needs explaining.

A bank tests its model on 100 applicants from each of two groups. In group A, 50 would repay; in group B, 20 would.

Try it: one threshold or two?

Two groups, A (half would repay) and B (one in five would repay), each get a score from the same model. Move the thresholds and watch the per-group rates. Try to make the selection rates equal; then try to make the true-positive rates equal; then notice what happens to precision and accuracy.

You can't have everything: the impossibility result

Why not just make every rate equal? Because, when the groups' base rates differ (here 50% vs 20%), it is mathematically impossible except for a perfect classifier. For any group, the four quantities are tied together by an identity (Chouldechova, 2017):

$$\text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot\text{TPR},$$

where $p$ is the group's base rate. If two groups have the same PPV and the same TPR but different $p$, their FPRs must differ.

Worked example — checking the identity

Group A: $p = 0.5$, PPV $= 40/45$, TPR $= 0.8$: $\ \frac{0.5}{0.5}\cdot\frac{5/45}{40/45}\cdot 0.8 = 1\times0.125\times0.8 = 0.10$ ✓ (the FPR we computed).

Group B: $p = 0.2$, PPV $= 0.75$, TPR $= 0.6$: $\ \frac{0.2}{0.8}\cdot\frac{0.25}{0.75}\cdot0.6 = 0.25\times0.333\times0.6 = 0.05$ ✓.

Now suppose we tuned group B to match A's PPV (0.889) and TPR (0.8). The identity forces B's FPR to be $0.25\times0.125\times0.8 = 0.025$ — no longer equal to A's 0.10. Equal precision and equal opportunity together rule out equal false-positive rates.

So choosing a fairness criterion is a value judgement. In lending, a bank may care most that qualified applicants are treated alike (equal opportunity). In criminal justice, critics of the COMPAS risk score (ProPublica, 2016) focused on unequal false-positive rates, while its makers pointed to equal predictive value — and both were right about their own measure. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2016) proved a closely related result for calibrated scores.

What you can do about it

  • Fix the data (before training): collect more data for under-represented groups, check that labels measure what you care about, remove or rethink proxy features, re-weight examples so groups count fairly.
  • Constrain the model (during training): add a fairness penalty or constraint to the loss, just as regularisation adds a penalty (Lesson 10). Libraries such as Fairlearn and AIF360 implement several methods.
  • Adjust decisions (after training): choose thresholds per group to equalise a chosen rate, as in the demo. This is effective but can be legally restricted, because it treats groups differently explicitly — get legal advice.
  • Change the system, not just the model: keep a human in the loop for consequential decisions, give people an explanation and a way to appeal, monitor outcomes after deployment, and be willing not to deploy.
"Fairness through unawareness" doesn't work

Deleting the protected attribute does not remove bias when other features are correlated with it — and it makes the bias harder to measure, because you can no longer compute group metrics. Most practitioners keep the attribute out of the model's inputs but use it for auditing.

Beyond fairness: privacy, transparency and accountability

  • Privacy: collect only the data you need, with consent. Models can leak training data (membership inference, Lesson 10); differential privacy adds carefully calibrated random noise during training so that no single person's record changes the result much.
  • Transparency: prefer interpretable models for high-stakes decisions, and explain outputs to the people affected. Document data with datasheets (Gebru et al.) and models with model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019).
  • Accountability: someone must be responsible for a model's decisions, and there must be a route to contest them. Laws such as the EU AI Act (see Recent developments) now require risk management, documentation and human oversight for high-risk uses such as hiring and credit.
  • Wider impact: energy use, effects on workers, and misuse (deepfakes, surveillance) are part of the responsibility too.

A practical checklist before you deploy

  1. Purpose: who is affected, what decision does the model inform, and is ML appropriate at all?
  2. Data: who is missing or under-represented? Is the label a fair proxy for the real goal?
  3. Metrics: compute the confusion matrix, selection rate, TPR, FPR and PPV for every relevant group — and the uncertainty when groups are small.
  4. Choose: decide with stakeholders which errors matter most here, and which fairness criterion you will hold the model to.
  5. Document: write a model card: intended use, evaluation by group, known limitations.
  6. Monitor: after deployment, track group metrics over time and watch for feedback loops; provide explanations and appeals.

In Python: a fairness report from scratch

Everything above is just the confusion matrix computed per group. Here is a small report on a synthetic loan dataset in which group B has a lower base rate:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# A synthetic loan dataset: two groups with different base rates of repayment
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 2000
group = rng.choice(["A", "B"], size=n, p=[0.6, 0.4])
base = np.where(group == "A", 0.5, 0.2)              # share who would repay
repay = (rng.random(n) < base).astype(int)
score = np.clip(0.5 + 0.25 * (repay - 0.5) * 2 + rng.normal(0, 0.18, n), 0, 1)  # model's score
df = pd.DataFrame({"group": group, "repay": repay, "score": score})

def group_report(df, threshold):
    d = df.assign(approve=(df["score"] >= threshold).astype(int))
    rows = {}
    for g, s in d.groupby("group"):
        tp = ((s.approve == 1) & (s.repay == 1)).sum(); fp = ((s.approve == 1) & (s.repay == 0)).sum()
        fn = ((s.approve == 0) & (s.repay == 1)).sum(); tn = ((s.approve == 0) & (s.repay == 0)).sum()
        rows[g] = {"selection rate": (tp + fp) / len(s), "TPR": tp / (tp + fn), "FPR": fp / (fp + tn), "PPV": tp / (tp + fp)}
    return pd.DataFrame(rows).T.round(3)

r = group_report(df, threshold=0.6)
print(r)
print("demographic-parity ratio:", round(r.loc["B", "selection rate"] / r.loc["A", "selection rate"], 3))
   selection rate    TPR    FPR    PPV
A           0.430  0.799  0.039  0.956
B           0.178  0.801  0.025  0.887
demographic-parity ratio: 0.414

Read it like a fairness auditor: with one shared threshold, qualified applicants are treated alike — the true-positive rates are both 0.80 (equal opportunity holds). But group B is approved far less often (ratio 0.41, well below 0.8) and an approval is less reliable for B (PPV 0.89 vs 0.96) — both consequences of B's lower base rate, exactly as the impossibility result predicts.

Key takeaways

  • Models learn the unfairness in their data; proxies let them discriminate without seeing protected attributes.
  • Bias can enter through history, sampling, labels, aggregation, evaluation and feedback loops.
  • Fairness is measured by comparing per-group rates: selection rate (demographic parity), TPR (equal opportunity), TPR and FPR (equalised odds), precision (predictive parity).
  • When base rates differ, these criteria cannot all hold at once — choosing one is a value judgement to make with the people affected.
  • Mitigation happens in the data, the training, the thresholds and, above all, the surrounding system: documentation, human oversight, appeal and monitoring.

Check your understanding

1. A model never receives applicants' ethnicity. Can it still discriminate by ethnicity?

Features like postcode or school are often correlated with protected attributes, so a model can reproduce group differences without the attribute itself.

2. Group A's selection rate is 0.50 and group B's is 0.30. What is the demographic-parity ratio, and does it pass the four-fifths rule of thumb?

$0.30/0.50 = 0.6$, which is below 0.8, so it would be flagged for possible adverse impact.

3. "Equal opportunity" asks which rate to be the same for every group?

Equal opportunity (Hardt, Price & Srebro, 2016) requires that qualified people — the true positives — are approved at the same rate in every group.

4. Two groups have different base rates. A model has equal precision and equal TPR for both. What must be true?

By the identity $\text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot\text{TPR}$, different base rates $p$ force different FPRs.

5. A hospital model predicts healthcare spending to decide who gets extra care. What kind of bias is the biggest risk?

This is the Obermeyer et al. (2019) case: groups with less access to care spend less at the same level of need, so the label understates their need.
