Why "accuracy" is not enough

You've trained a classifier. Is it good? The obvious answer is accuracy: the fraction of predictions that are correct. But accuracy can be badly misleading.

The accuracy trap

A disease affects 1 in 100 people. A "model" that simply says "healthy" for everyone is 99% accurate — and completely useless, because it never finds a single sick person.

Different mistakes have different costs. Missing a cancer is far worse than an unnecessary follow-up test; flagging a real email as spam is worse than letting one spam through. This lesson gives you the tools to see exactly which mistakes a model makes, and to measure what actually matters for your problem.

The confusion matrix

For a binary classifier, every prediction falls into one of four boxes:

Predicted positivePredicted negative
Actually positiveTP — true positive
correctly caught		FN — false negative
missed ("Type II error")
Actually negativeFP — false positive
false alarm ("Type I error")		TN — true negative

The word after "true/false" is what the model predicted; "true" means that prediction was right. So a false positive is a predicted positive that was wrong.

Worked example — a screening test on 100 patients

10 patients are actually sick, 90 are healthy. The model flags 18 patients: 8 of them are really sick.

We'll compute every metric below from these four numbers.

Accuracy, precision, recall, specificity

Accuracy — what fraction of all predictions were right?

$$\text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} = \frac{8 + 80}{100} = 0.88$$

Precision — of the ones we flagged, how many were really positive? ("When the model says yes, can I trust it?")

$$\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} = \frac{8}{8 + 10} \approx 0.44$$

Recall (also sensitivity or true positive rate) — of the real positives, how many did we catch? ("Does the model find what it should?")

$$\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} = \frac{8}{8 + 2} = 0.80$$

Specificity (true negative rate) — of the real negatives, how many did we correctly leave alone?

$$\text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP} = \frac{80}{90} \approx 0.89$$
Remember them with a fishing net

You cast a net to catch a particular kind of fish. Recall: what fraction of those fish in the lake ended up in your net? Precision: what fraction of the things in your net are those fish (and not old boots)? A huge net has great recall but poor precision; a tiny careful net has great precision but poor recall.

So our screening test catches 80% of sick patients (good recall), but only 44% of its alarms are real (low precision). Whether that's acceptable depends on the situation: for an initial screening followed by a more accurate test, it may be fine.

The F1 score

To summarise precision and recall in one number, use their harmonic mean, the F1 score:

$$F_1 = 2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN}$$

For our example: $F_1 = \frac{2\cdot8}{2\cdot8 + 10 + 2} = \frac{16}{28} \approx 0.57$.

Why the harmonic mean rather than the ordinary average? It's dragged down by whichever value is smaller, so a model can't hide terrible precision behind great recall. A model with precision 1.0 and recall 0.01 has an ordinary average of about 0.5, but an F1 of only about 0.02.

Weighting precision vs recall

The $F_\beta$ score lets you weight recall $\beta$ times as much as precision: $F_\beta = \frac{(1+\beta^2)\,TP}{(1+\beta^2)\,TP + \beta^2 FN + FP}$. $F_2$ favours recall (useful for medical screening); $F_{0.5}$ favours precision (useful for spam filtering).

The threshold trade-off

Most classifiers output a score or probability (Lesson 8). Turning it into a yes/no decision needs a threshold. Moving the threshold trades one kind of mistake for the other:

  • Lower threshold → flag more cases → more TPs and more FPs → recall ↑, precision ↓.
  • Higher threshold → flag fewer cases → fewer FPs but more FNs → precision ↑, recall ↓.

There is no threshold that is "correct" in general; you choose it based on the cost of each type of mistake.

ROC curves and AUC

Instead of picking one threshold, we can look at all of them at once. For each threshold, compute:

$$\text{TPR} = \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \qquad \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN} = 1 - \text{Specificity}$$

Plotting TPR (up) against FPR (across) for every threshold gives the ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic, a name from radar engineering).

  • The bottom-left corner $(0, 0)$ is threshold = 1: flag nothing.
  • The top-right corner $(1, 1)$ is threshold = 0: flag everything.
  • A perfect model reaches the top-left corner $(0, 1)$: all positives caught, no false alarms.
  • Random guessing gives the diagonal line.

The area under the ROC curve (AUC) summarises the whole curve in one number from 0.5 (random) to 1.0 (perfect). It has a lovely interpretation:

What AUC means

AUC is the probability that the model gives a randomly chosen positive example a higher score than a randomly chosen negative example.

Worked example — AUC by counting pairs

Scores for 3 positives: $0.9, 0.7, 0.4$. Scores for 3 negatives: $0.8, 0.3, 0.2$.

There are $3\times3 = 9$ positive–negative pairs. Count the pairs where the positive scores higher:

  • $0.9$ beats $0.8, 0.3, 0.2$ → 3
  • $0.7$ beats $0.3, 0.2$ (not $0.8$) → 2
  • $0.4$ beats $0.3, 0.2$ → 2

AUC $= 7/9 \approx 0.78$. The model ranks a positive above a negative 78% of the time.

AUC is useful because it doesn't depend on any particular threshold. But when positives are very rare, the ROC curve can look flattering, because FPR divides by the huge number of negatives.

Precision–recall curves

When positives are rare (fraud, disease, defects), a precision–recall curve — precision (up) against recall (across) for every threshold — is usually more informative. Its summary is average precision (AP), a weighted average of precision across recall levels. A random model's AP equals the fraction of positives (e.g. 0.01 for 1% fraud), so the baseline is much lower than ROC's 0.5, making differences between models easier to see.

More than two classes

With $K$ classes the confusion matrix is $K\times K$: row = true class, column = predicted class, and the diagonal holds the correct predictions. Off-diagonal cells show which classes get confused (e.g. "3" mistaken for "8"). Precision, recall and F1 are computed per class, then averaged:

  • Macro average: plain average over classes — every class counts equally.
  • Weighted average: weighted by how many examples each class has.

Metrics for regression

For predicting numbers, we measure the size of the errors $e_i = \hat{y}_i - y_i$:

MetricFormulaNotes
MAE — mean absolute error$\frac{1}{n}\sum|e_i|$In the target's units; robust to outliers
MSE — mean squared error$\frac{1}{n}\sum e_i^2$Punishes large errors; squared units
RMSE — root MSE$\sqrt{\text{MSE}}$Back in the target's units
$R^2$$1 - \frac{\sum e_i^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$Fraction of variance explained (Lesson 6)
Worked example

True $y = 3, 5, 2, 7$; predicted $\hat{y} = 2.5, 5, 4, 8$. Errors: $-0.5, 0, 2, 1$.

MAE $= (0.5 + 0 + 2 + 1)/4 = 0.875$.
MSE $= (0.25 + 0 + 4 + 1)/4 = 1.3125$, RMSE $= \sqrt{1.3125} \approx 1.15$.
$\bar{y} = 4.25$, $\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2 = 14.75$, so $R^2 = 1 - 5.25/14.75 \approx 0.64$.

Notice RMSE (1.15) > MAE (0.875): the single error of 2 counts extra in RMSE. If you care a lot about avoiding big misses, use RMSE; if every unit of error costs the same, use MAE.

Choosing the right metric

SituationFocus on
Balanced classes, mistakes cost the sameAccuracy
Missing a positive is very costly (disease, fraud)Recall (with acceptable precision), $F_2$
False alarms are very costly (spam filter, legal flags)Precision, $F_{0.5}$
Need one balanced number for imbalanced dataF1, average precision
Comparing models independent of thresholdROC-AUC (or AP if positives are rare)
Regression, all errors equally badMAE
Regression, big errors especially badRMSE
Always compare to a baseline

Before celebrating a score, compute what a trivial model gets: always predicting the most common class, or always predicting the mean. A 95% accurate model is unimpressive if the baseline is 94%. And always measure on data the model hasn't trained on (Lesson 5).

In Python

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, classification_report, roc_auc_score

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
y = 1 - y        # make "malignant" the positive class (1)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(X_train, y_train)
proba = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]          # P(malignant)

for threshold in [0.5, 0.2]:
    pred = (proba >= threshold).astype(int)
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y_test, pred).ravel()
    print(f"threshold {threshold}: TP={tp} FN={fn} FP={fp} TN={tn}  "
          f"precision={tp/(tp+fp):.3f} recall={tp/(tp+fn):.3f}")

print("ROC-AUC:", round(roc_auc_score(y_test, proba), 3))
print(classification_report(y_test, (proba >= 0.5).astype(int), target_names=["benign", "malignant"]))

At the default threshold of 0.5 the model missed 2 malignant tumours. Lowering the threshold to 0.2 missed only 1 (recall 0.96 → 0.98) but raised 9 false alarms (precision 1.00 → 0.85) — exactly the trade-off from the demo. For cancer screening, many doctors would accept those extra follow-up tests. Key takeaways

  • The confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN) shows exactly which mistakes a classifier makes.
  • Precision $= \frac{TP}{TP+FP}$: trust in positive predictions. Recall $= \frac{TP}{TP+FN}$: share of positives found. F1 balances them.
  • Accuracy misleads on imbalanced data — always compare with a trivial baseline.
  • The threshold trades precision against recall; ROC-AUC and average precision summarise all thresholds.
  • Regression: MAE, RMSE (punishes big errors) and $R^2$.

Check your understanding

1. TP = 30, FP = 10, FN = 20. What is the precision?

Precision $= 30/(30 + 10) = 0.75$. (Recall would be $30/(30+20) = 0.60$.)

2. A fraud detector must miss as few frauds as possible. Which metric matters most?

Missing a fraud is a false negative; recall $= TP/(TP+FN)$ measures how many frauds are caught.

3. You lower the classification threshold from 0.5 to 0.3. What usually happens?

More examples get flagged positive: more true positives are caught (recall ↑) but more false alarms too (precision ↓).

4. A model has ROC-AUC = 0.5. What does that suggest?

AUC 0.5 means a random positive is scored above a random negative only half the time — like a coin flip.

5. Errors are 1, −1, 1, −5. Which is larger, MAE or RMSE?

MAE $= 8/4 = 2$. MSE $= (1+1+1+25)/4 = 7$, RMSE $\approx 2.65$. Squaring makes the big error count more.
