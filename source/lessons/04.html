Why probability and statistics?

Real data is messy. Two houses of the same size sell for different prices; the same symptoms can come from different illnesses. ML models live with this uncertainty, so they speak the language of probability: a spam filter doesn't say "spam", it says "97% likely spam". Statistics gives us tools to summarise data — averages, spread — that we'll use in almost every lesson.

Probability basics

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how likely an event is: 0 = impossible, 1 = certain, 0.5 = as likely as not. We write $P(A)$ for "the probability of event $A$".

When all outcomes are equally likely:

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes where } A \text{ happens}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$
Worked example — a fair die

$P(\text{roll a 6}) = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$. $P(\text{roll an even number}) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5$ (outcomes 2, 4, 6).

Three rules do most of the work:

  • Complement: $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$. Probability of not rolling a 6 is $5/6$.
  • Addition (for events that can't both happen): $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$. Rolling a 1 or a 2: $1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3$.
  • Multiplication (for independent events — one doesn't affect the other): $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. Two coin flips both heads: $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$.

Conditional probability

Often we know something already, and that changes the odds. $P(A \mid B)$ — "probability of $A$ given $B$" — is the probability of $A$ when we only consider situations where $B$ happened:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}$$
Worked example — emails

Out of 100 emails, 30 are spam. 20 emails contain the word "free", and 15 of those are spam.

$P(\text{spam}) = 30/100 = 0.30$.
$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"}) = 15/20 = 0.75$.

Knowing the email contains "free" raised the spam probability from 30% to 75%. This is the core idea behind Naive Bayes spam filters (Lesson 12): features change our beliefs about the label.

Careful: $P(A \mid B) \neq P(B \mid A)$

$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"}) = 15/20 = 0.75$, but $P(\text{"free"} \mid \text{spam}) = 15/30 = 0.5$. Mixing these up is one of the most common reasoning errors — and the next section shows how to convert between them.

Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem flips a conditional probability around:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}$$

In ML language, with $A$ = "a hypothesis" (e.g. has the disease) and $B$ = "the evidence" (e.g. tested positive):

  • $P(A)$ — the prior: what we believed before seeing evidence.
  • $P(B \mid A)$ — the likelihood: how probable the evidence is if the hypothesis is true.
  • $P(A \mid B)$ — the posterior: our updated belief after seeing the evidence.
Worked example — the surprising medical test

A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches it 90% of the time when present (sensitivity), but also gives a positive result to 5% of healthy people (false positives). You test positive. What's the chance you have the disease?

Most people guess around 90%. Let's compute.

$P(D) = 0.01$, $P(+ \mid D) = 0.90$, $P(+ \mid \text{healthy}) = 0.05$.

First, the overall chance of a positive test (sick-and-positive plus healthy-and-positive):

$$P(+) = 0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585$$

Then Bayes:

$$P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.90 \times 0.01}{0.0585} \approx 0.154$$

Only about 15%! Because the disease is rare, the false positives from the 99% of healthy people outnumber the true positives.

The same thing with counts (often easier): in 1,000 people, 10 are sick and 9 of them test positive. Of the 990 healthy people, about 49.5 test positive. So of roughly 58.5 positives, only 9 are sick: $9/58.5 \approx 15\%$.

Try it: Bayes with 1,000 people

Each square is a person. Change the disease rate and the test's accuracy, and see how many of the people who test positive are actually sick.

Random variables and expected value

A random variable is a quantity whose value depends on chance — the result of a die roll, tomorrow's temperature, a house's sale price. Its expected value (mean) is the average you'd get over many repetitions: each possible value weighted by its probability.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x \cdot P(X = x)$$
Worked example — a die

$\mathbb{E}[X] = 1\cdot\tfrac16 + 2\cdot\tfrac16 + \dots + 6\cdot\tfrac16 = \tfrac{21}{6} = 3.5$. You can never roll 3.5, but it's the long-run average.

Describing data: mean, variance, standard deviation

Given $n$ numbers $x_1, \dots, x_n$:

The mean (average) is the centre:

$$\bar{x} = \mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$

The variance measures spread — the average squared distance from the mean:

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2$$

The standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$ is spread in the original units.

Worked example

Exam scores: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12.

Mean: $\mu = (4+6+8+10+12)/5 = 40/5 = 8$.

Distances from the mean: $-4, -2, 0, 2, 4$. Squared: $16, 4, 0, 4, 16$.

Variance: $\sigma^2 = 40/5 = 8$. Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$.

So a typical score is about 2.8 points away from the average of 8.

Population vs sample

Dividing by $n$ gives the population variance. When estimating from a sample, statisticians often divide by $n-1$ instead (the "sample variance"), which corrects a small bias. NumPy's np.var divides by $n$ by default; pandas' .var() divides by $n-1$. For large datasets the difference is negligible.

Also useful: the median (middle value when sorted) is robust to outliers. Incomes of 30k, 35k, 40k, 45k and 1M have a mean of 230k but a median of 40k — the median describes the "typical" person far better.

Standardisation (z-scores)

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

A score of 12 above: $z = (12 - 8)/2.83 \approx 1.41$. Converting every feature to z-scores — mean 0, standard deviation 1 — is called standardisation. It's one of the most common preprocessing steps in ML (scikit-learn's StandardScaler), because many algorithms work badly when features have wildly different scales (Lessons 11 and 20).

Probability distributions

A distribution describes all the values a random variable can take and how likely each is.

Bernoulli: a single yes/no

One trial with success probability $p$: $P(1) = p$, $P(0) = 1-p$. A coin flip is Bernoulli with $p=0.5$. A classifier's "is this spam?" output is a Bernoulli probability.

Normal (Gaussian): the bell curve

Many natural quantities — heights, measurement errors — follow the bell-shaped normal distribution, described by its mean $\mu$ (centre) and standard deviation $\sigma$ (width). Its density is:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

You don't need to memorise the formula — just its shape and this rule of thumb:

  • about 68% of values lie within 1 $\sigma$ of the mean,
  • about 95% within 2 $\sigma$,
  • about 99.7% within 3 $\sigma$.
Worked example

Adult heights in a population: $\mu = 170$ cm, $\sigma = 10$ cm. About 95% of people are between $170 - 20 = 150$ and $170 + 20 = 190$ cm. Someone 200 cm tall is 3 $\sigma$ above the mean — rarer than 1 in 300 (only 0.3% fall outside $\pm 3\sigma$, half of them above).

Try it: sampling from a normal distribution

Each click draws random samples from a normal distribution and adds them to the histogram. With few samples the shape is ragged; with many, it settles into the bell curve. Adjust $\mu$ and $\sigma$ to see the centre move and the width change.

How this connects to machine learning

  • Classifiers output probabilities: logistic regression (Lesson 8) outputs $P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$.
  • Naive Bayes (Lesson 12) is Bayes' theorem applied to features.
  • Noise: we model data as "true pattern + random noise", often assumed normal. That's why the squared error loss is so natural.
  • Evaluation (Lesson 9) reports means and standard deviations of scores across experiments.
  • Preprocessing uses means and standard deviations to standardise features.

In Python

import numpy as np

scores = np.array([4, 6, 8, 10, 12])
print(scores.mean())           # 8.0
print(scores.var())            # 8.0   (divides by n)
print(scores.std())            # 2.828...
print(np.median([30, 35, 40, 45, 1000]))   # 40.0

z = (scores - scores.mean()) / scores.std()
print(z.round(2))              # [-1.41 -0.71  0.    0.71  1.41]

# Bayes: the medical test
p_d, sens, fpr = 0.01, 0.90, 0.05
p_pos = sens * p_d + fpr * (1 - p_d)
print(round(sens * p_d / p_pos, 3))        # 0.154

# Sample 100,000 heights and check the 68-95-99.7 rule
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
h = rng.normal(170, 10, size=100_000)
print(np.mean(np.abs(h - 170) < 20))       # ≈ 0.95

Key takeaways

  • Probabilities run from 0 to 1. Key rules: complement, addition, multiplication for independent events.
  • Conditional probability $P(A\mid B)$ updates odds given information; $P(A\mid B) \ne P(B\mid A)$.
  • Bayes' theorem: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Rare events stay fairly unlikely even after positive evidence.
  • Mean = centre, variance/standard deviation = spread; z-scores standardise features.
  • The normal distribution: 68% / 95% / 99.7% within 1 / 2 / 3 standard deviations.

Check your understanding

1. You flip a fair coin 3 times. What is the probability of 3 heads?

Independent flips multiply: $0.5^3 = 1/8$.

2. Data: 2, 4, 6. What is the variance (dividing by $n$)?

Mean is 4. Squared distances: 4, 0, 4. Average: $8/3 \approx 2.67$.

3. In Bayes' theorem, $P(A)$ is called the…

The prior is your belief before seeing the evidence. The posterior $P(A\mid B)$ is after.

4. A value has z-score −2. What does that mean?

$z = (x - \mu)/\sigma = -2$ means $x$ is two standard deviations below the mean.

5. A normal distribution has $\mu = 50$, $\sigma = 5$. Roughly what fraction of values fall between 45 and 55?

45–55 is within 1 standard deviation of the mean: about 68%.
