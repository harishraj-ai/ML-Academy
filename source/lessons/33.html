Not "whether", but "when"

Many questions are about time until something happens: how long until a customer cancels, a machine fails, a loan defaults, a patient recovers, an employee leaves. Survival analysis (also called time-to-event analysis) models these times. Its special challenge is censoring: for many people the event hasn't happened yet when the data is collected. A customer who joined three months ago and is still subscribed hasn't survived "three months"; they've survived at least three months.

Treat those customers as if they'd churned at three months and you underestimate how long people stay; drop them and you lose information and bias the data towards short times. This lesson covers the tools built for this: Kaplan–Meier curves, the log-rank test, the Cox proportional hazards model, and the concordance index for evaluation.

The core idea

At each moment, look only at the people still "at risk" (not yet churned and still observed), and ask what fraction of them churn right then. Chaining those fractions together gives the survival curve, and censored people contribute for exactly as long as they were watched.

Censored data

Each row has a time and an event flag: churned = 1 means the customer cancelled at that time; churned = 0 means they were still subscribed when the study ended (right-censored). Here are 600 simulated subscribers on monthly or annual plans, with the number of support tickets they raised in their first month:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n = 600
plan = rng.integers(0, 2, n)                                  # 0 monthly, 1 annual
support = rng.poisson(2, n)                                   # support tickets in the first month
rate = np.exp(-2.0 - 0.8 * plan + 0.25 * support)             # monthly hazard of cancelling
churn_time = rng.exponential(1 / rate)
study_end = rng.uniform(6, 24, n)                             # customers joined at different times
df = pd.DataFrame({"plan": plan, "support": support,
                   "months": np.minimum(churn_time, study_end).round(2),
                   "churned": (churn_time <= study_end).astype(int)})
print(df.head(4).to_string(index=False))
print("customers who churned during the study:", df.churned.sum(), "of", n)
print("naive average of 'months' (ignoring censoring):", round(df.months.mean(), 2), "  true mean time to churn:", round(churn_time.mean(), 2))

Output:

 plan  support  months  churned
    1        3    3.71        1
    1        3    7.30        1
    1        4    2.44        1
    1        0    5.78        1
customers who churned during the study: 494 of 600
naive average of 'months' (ignoring censoring): 6.02   true mean time to churn: 7.86

106 of the 600 are censored. Averaging the observed times gives 6.02 months, well below the true average of 7.86, because censored customers are counted at the time the study stopped watching them instead of when they eventually leave.

The Kaplan–Meier estimator

The survival function $S(t)$ is the probability of still being a customer after time $t$. The Kaplan–Meier estimator builds it step by step. At each time $t_k$ when someone churns, with $n_k$ customers still at risk and $d_k$ churning:

$$\hat S(t) = \prod_{t_k \le t} \left(1 - \frac{d_k}{n_k}\right)$$

Worked example — Kaplan–Meier by hand

Five customers: churned at 2 months, censored at 3, churned at 4, churned at 4, censored at 6.

At $t = 2$: 5 at risk, 1 churns, so $S = 1 - 1/5 = 0.8$. At $t = 3$ one is censored: no churn, so no step, but the risk set falls to 3. At $t = 4$: 3 at risk, 2 churn, so $S = 0.8 \times (1 - 2/3) \approx 0.267$. After that, one customer remains, censored at 6, so the curve stays at 0.267.

Treating the censored customer at 3 months as a churner would instead give $S(4) = 1/5 = 0.2$: too pessimistic.

def kaplan_meier(t, e):
    order = np.argsort(t); t, e = t[order], e[order]
    times, surv, s, at_risk = [0.0], [1.0], 1.0, len(t)
    for u in np.unique(t):
        d = e[t == u].sum()                                     # churned at exactly this time
        if d: s *= 1 - d / at_risk; times.append(u); surv.append(s)
        at_risk -= (t == u).sum()                               # churned or censored leave the risk set
    return np.array(times), np.array(surv)
def median_survival(times, surv):
    below = np.where(surv <= 0.5)[0]
    return times[below[0]] if len(below) else np.inf
for p, name in [(0, "monthly"), (1, "annual")]:
    g = df[df.plan == p]
    tt, ss = kaplan_meier(g.months.to_numpy(), g.churned.to_numpy())
    s12 = ss[tt <= 12][-1]
    print(f"{name:8s}: still subscribed after 12 months {s12:.3f}, median survival {median_survival(tt, ss):.1f} months")

Output:

monthly : still subscribed after 12 months 0.112, median survival 3.2 months
annual  : still subscribed after 12 months 0.297, median survival 6.9 months

After 12 months, 29.7% of annual-plan customers are still subscribed, against 11.2% of monthly ones. The median survival, when the curve crosses 0.5, is 6.9 months for annual and 3.2 for monthly. Medians are the usual summary, because the mean depends on the far tail, which censoring hides.

Comparing groups: the log-rank test

Is the difference between the curves real or noise? The log-rank test compares, at each churn time, how many churns occurred in one group with how many would be expected if both groups had the same risk, given who was at risk. It sums the differences and converts them to a chi-squared statistic with 1 degree of freedom:

from scipy import stats
def logrank(t, e, g):
    O_minus_E, V = 0.0, 0.0
    for u in np.unique(t[e == 1]):
        at = t >= u; n_all, n1 = at.sum(), (at & (g == 1)).sum()
        d_all, d1 = ((t == u) & (e == 1)).sum(), ((t == u) & (e == 1) & (g == 1)).sum()
        O_minus_E += d1 - d_all * n1 / n_all
        if n_all > 1: V += d_all * (n1 / n_all) * (1 - n1 / n_all) * (n_all - d_all) / (n_all - 1)
    chi2 = O_minus_E ** 2 / V
    return chi2, stats.chi2.sf(chi2, 1)
chi2, p = logrank(df.months.to_numpy(), df.churned.to_numpy(), df.plan.to_numpy())
print(f"log-rank test, annual vs monthly: chi-squared {chi2:.1f}, p-value {p:.2g}")

Output:

log-rank test, annual vs monthly: chi-squared 55.0, p-value 1.2e-13

A chi-squared of 55.0 and a p-value around $10^{-13}$: annual customers really do stay longer. Like any test (Lesson 4's "Going deeper"), it says the difference is unlikely to be chance, not how big it is or why. Annual customers may differ in other ways, so this isn't proof that switching someone's plan would make them stay (Lesson 29).

The Cox proportional hazards model

To use several features at once, model the hazard $h(t \mid x)$: the instantaneous rate of churning at time $t$ among those still at risk. The Cox model assumes

$$h(t \mid x) = h_0(t)\,\exp(\beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_p x_p)$$

The baseline hazard $h_0(t)$ can have any shape and is never estimated directly; the features multiply it by a constant factor. $e^{\beta_j}$ is the hazard ratio for a one-unit increase in $x_j$. The coefficients are fitted by maximising the partial likelihood: at each churn, the probability that it was this customer, among everyone at risk at that moment, who churned:

from scipy.optimize import minimize
Xc = df[["plan", "support"]].to_numpy(float); T, E = df.months.to_numpy(), df.churned.to_numpy()
def neg_partial_loglik(beta):
    eta = Xc @ beta; total = 0.0
    for i in np.where(E == 1)[0]:
        total += eta[i] - np.log(np.exp(eta[T >= T[i]]).sum())   # the risk set at that moment
    return -total
fit = minimize(neg_partial_loglik, np.zeros(2), method="BFGS")
for name, b in zip(["annual plan", "support tickets"], fit.x):
    print(f"{name:15s} coefficient {b:+.3f}   hazard ratio {np.exp(b):.2f}")
print("true coefficients: -0.8 and +0.25")

Output:

annual plan     coefficient -0.698   hazard ratio 0.50
support tickets coefficient +0.258   hazard ratio 1.29
true coefficients: -0.8 and +0.25

The annual plan has a hazard ratio of 0.50: at any moment, annual customers churn at about half the rate of otherwise similar monthly ones. Each support ticket multiplies the churn rate by 1.29, about 29% higher. Both are close to the true values used in the simulation. The model's key assumption is proportional hazards: the ratio stays constant over time. Check it, for example by fitting separate models for early and late periods; if an effect fades over time, a time-varying coefficient is needed.

Evaluating survival models

Censoring makes ordinary metrics unusable: you don't know the true time for censored people. The standard metric is the concordance index (C-index): among all pairs where we know who churned first, the share in which the model gave the earlier churner a higher risk score. 0.5 is random and 1 is a perfect ordering; it's the survival version of ROC-AUC (Lesson 9):

def concordance(T, E, risk):
    ok = tot = 0
    for i in np.where(E == 1)[0]:
        later = T > T[i]                                           # pairs we can order: i churned first
        tot += later.sum(); ok += (risk[i] > risk[later]).sum() + 0.5 * (risk[i] == risk[later]).sum()
    return ok / tot
print("concordance index, Cox model:", round(concordance(T, E, Xc @ fit.x), 3))
print("concordance index, support tickets only:", round(concordance(T, E, Xc[:, 1]), 3))
print("concordance index, random scores:", round(concordance(T, E, rng.random(len(T))), 3))

Output:

concordance index, Cox model: 0.639
concordance index, support tickets only: 0.59
concordance index, random scores: 0.517

The Cox model reaches 0.639, support tickets alone 0.59, and random scores 0.517. Churn timing is noisy, so values of 0.6 to 0.75 are typical in practice. Other tools: the Brier score over time (calibration at each horizon), and machine-learning models built for censoring, such as random survival forests and gradient boosting with a Cox loss. The lifelines and scikit-survival libraries implement all of these; this lesson builds the core from NumPy and SciPy.

Survival analysis in practice

  • Define time zero carefully: sign-up date, installation date, diagnosis date. Everyone's clock must start at a comparable moment.
  • Censoring must be non-informative: people mustn't drop out of observation because they were about to churn. A customer who closed their account through a different channel isn't censored; they churned.
  • Don't use features from the future: a feature measured after time zero (such as support tickets in month six) leaks information about survival. Use values known at time zero, or time-varying models.
  • Competing risks: if several events are possible (cancelled, or moved to a partner), treating one as censoring for the other biases the estimates; use competing-risk methods.

In Python: survival from a subscription table

From raw sign-up and cancellation dates to a Kaplan–Meier table, computing the time and censoring flag yourself, as you would from a real database:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

subs = pd.DataFrame({
    "signup":   pd.to_datetime(["2025-01-05", "2025-02-10", "2025-02-20", "2025-03-01", "2025-04-15", "2025-05-01", "2025-06-10", "2025-07-01"]),
    "cancelled": pd.to_datetime(["2025-04-01", None, "2025-05-20", "2025-03-30", None, "2025-09-15", None, "2025-08-01"])})
today = pd.Timestamp("2025-12-31")
subs["event"] = subs.cancelled.notna().astype(int)
subs["months"] = ((subs.cancelled.fillna(today) - subs.signup).dt.days / 30.44).round(1)
rows, s, at_risk = [], 1.0, len(subs)
for t, g in subs.sort_values("months").groupby("months"):
    d = g.event.sum()
    if d:
        s *= 1 - d / at_risk
        rows.append((t, at_risk, d, round(s, 3)))
    at_risk -= len(g)
print(subs[["months", "event"]].to_string(index=False))
print(pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["month", "at risk", "churned", "survival"]).to_string(index=False))

Customers still subscribed on the cut-off date get event 0 and their time so far. The survival column falls only when someone churns, and each step's size depends on how many were still at risk, exactly as in the worked example.

Key takeaways

  • Survival analysis models time until an event, handling censored observations properly.
  • Kaplan–Meier multiplies the share surviving each event time among those at risk; report medians.
  • The log-rank test compares survival curves between groups.
  • The Cox model gives hazard ratios for features, assuming proportional hazards.
  • Evaluate with the concordance index; define time zero carefully and avoid future features.

$1 - 4/40 = 0.9$.

3. A Cox coefficient of 0.4 for "missed payments" means…

The hazard ratio is $e^\beta$.

4. Why is the median survival time usually reported instead of the mean?

The median only needs the curve to cross 0.5.

5. A survival model has a concordance index of 0.5. That means…

0.5 is random ordering; 1 is perfect.
