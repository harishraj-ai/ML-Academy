k-nearest neighbours

1The simplest idea in ML

Picture it
New in town and wondering if a restaurant is good? Ask the three people who live closest. "Tell me who your neighbours are, and I'll tell you who you are."

To classify a new example, find the $k$ most similar training examples and let them vote. No equations, no weights, and yet it can draw curved boundaries linear models can't. It teaches two big lessons: feature scaling and the curse of dimensionality.

2The algorithm

Training: just store the data (a lazy learner: the work happens at prediction time).

Predicting for a point $\mathbf{q}$:

  1. Distance from $\mathbf{q}$ to every training example.
  2. Take the $k$ smallest.
  3. Classification: majority vote. Regression: average of their $y$.

kNN is non-parametric: the training data itself is the model.

3Measuring distance

  • Euclidean $\sqrt{\sum(a_j-b_j)^2}$: straight line (default).
  • Manhattan $\sum|a_j-b_j|$: city blocks; less dominated by one big difference.
  • Minkowski $(\sum|a_j-b_j|^p)^{1/p}$: $p=1$ Manhattan, $p=2$ Euclidean (scikit-learn's default).
  • Cosine: the angle between vectors; popular for text.
Example
Query (2, 2): red A (1, 1) at 1.414, red B (2, 1) at 1.000, blue C (4, 3) at 2.236 … With k = 3, count the votes of the three closest.
(1, 2) to (4, 6): $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=$ 5 (Manhattan: 7).

4Choosing k

  • Small k: follows every point, even mislabelled ones → islands → high variance. With $k=1$ training accuracy is always 100% (each point is its own neighbour), which means nothing.
  • Large k: smooth, but too large blurs real structure and ends up predicting the majority class → high bias.
  • Pick k by cross-validation; odd values avoid ties with two classes.

Distance-weighted voting (weights="distance", weight $1/d$): closer neighbours count more, and the exact k matters less.

6Feature scaling is essential

Example: age vs income
P: 30 years, 50,000. Q: 60, 51,000. R: 31, 70,000.
$d(P,Q)\approx1000.4$, $d(P,R)\approx20000$. So Q looks "much closer", even though Q is twice P's age. Income (thousands) swamps age (tens); age is ignored.

After standardising (mean 0, sd 1), each gap is measured relative to how much that feature normally varies, so both count.

7kNN for regression

$\hat y(\mathbf{q})=\dfrac1k\sum_{i\in N_k(\mathbf{q})}y_i$

Nearest houses sold for 300k, 320k, 340k → predict 320k. With distance weighting it's a weighted average.

8The curse of dimensionality

A cube-shaped neighbourhood holding 10% of evenly spread data needs side length $0.1^{1/d}$:

Example from the lesson
d = 1: 0.10 · d = 2: 0.32 · d = 3: 0.46 · d = 10: 0.79 · d = 100: 0.98.
In 10 dimensions the "neighbourhood" spans 79% of each feature's range: the nearest neighbours aren't near at all.

kNN works best with fewer, relevant features.

9Strengths and weaknesses

+ simple to explain
+ no training time
+ curved boundaries
+ classification and regression
− slow predictions (compares to every point)
− stores the whole training set
− needs scaling
− struggles with many / irrelevant features

Speed-ups: KD-trees and ball trees skip whole regions that can't hold a nearer point (algorithm="auto" chooses for you).

★Going deeper: distances, dimensions and fast neighbour search

The curse of dimensionality, measured

In a small room, some people are close to you and some far away, so "nearest" is meaningful. In a space with hundreds of directions, every person differs from you a little in every direction, and those small differences add up to almost the same total for everyone. Everyone becomes roughly equally far away.

Reading the ratio
2 dimensions: farthest/nearest $=1.33/0.01\approx136$. "Nearest" is very special.
100 dimensions: $4.65/3.35\approx1.39$.
1,000 dimensions: $13.55/12.17\approx1.11$. The nearest point is only 11% closer than the farthest, so it tells you little.

Choosing the distance and the weighting

Three ways to measure how far apart two places are: as the crow flies (Euclidean), along a city's grid of streets (Manhattan), or simply whether they lie in the same direction from home (cosine).

Cosine ignores length
Document A has word counts (1, 2); document B, twice as long, has (2, 4).
Euclidean distance: $\sqrt{1^2+2^2}\approx2.24$, "far apart".
Same direction, so $\cos\theta=1$ and cosine distance $=0$: "identical topic".

Finding neighbours fast

Finding the closest café: checking every café in the country is slow. A map split into regions lets you check only your area (a tree). Asking a well-connected local who knows nearby places, and hopping from friend to friend, is even faster and almost always right (approximate search).

How much a tree saves
1,000,000 points. Brute force: 1,000,000 distance checks per query.
A balanced tree halves the candidates at each level: about $\log_2(10^6)\approx20$ levels.
So a few dozen checks instead of a million (in low dimensions).

How good can kNN get?

With unlimited data, the nearest neighbour of any new point is practically the same point, so copying its label is almost as good as knowing the true odds. The only mistakes left come from the problem's own randomness.

What "twice" means
A problem where even the perfect model is wrong 5% of the time (Bayes error 5%).
With enough data, 1-NN is wrong at most 10% of the time.
The catch: in many dimensions, "enough data" can be astronomically large.
In one breath
In high dimensions all distances look alike, so reduce dimensions or use good embeddings; scale features and tune $k$ before worrying about the metric (use cosine for text); use trees or approximate search for speed; and with enough data kNN gets close to the best possible.
✎ in one line: vote among the k most similar examples; scale first, pick k by CV, keep the features few and relevant.
