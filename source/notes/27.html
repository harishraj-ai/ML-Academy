Recommender systems

1What recommenders do

Every streaming service, shop and feed picks, for each person, a few items out of millions. A large share of what people watch and buy online comes from recommendations. The data: a huge, mostly empty users × items table; the job: fill in the blanks and show each user their best unseen items.

2Explicit vs implicit feedback

  • Explicit: stars, thumbs. Clear but scarce.
  • Implicit: clicks, plays, purchases, watch time. Plentiful but noisy, and no negatives: an unclicked item may be disliked or simply never seen.
The lesson's data
300 users × 60 films, built from 3 hidden tastes: 2,747 ratings fill 15.3% of 18,000 cells. Real systems are often well under 1%.

3Baselines: averages and biases

Example (RMSE on one held-out rating per user)
Global average 1.077 · each film's average 1.024 · film average + naive user bias 1.057 (worse!).

With only ~9 ratings per user the user bias is noisy and double-counts the film effect (someone who happened to rate good films looks generous). Biases must be learned jointly with regularization, as matrix factorisation does.

Always beat popularity
Compare with "most popular items", not with random recommendations.

4Collaborative filtering

"People like you also liked…". User-based: from users similar to you. Item-based: from films similar to this one (rated alike by the same users). No item information needed.

Similarity (cosine or Pearson) on centred ratings, so a harsh and a generous rater with the same taste look alike:

$\hat r_{ui}=\bar r_u+\dfrac{\sum_{j\in N(i)}\text{sim}(i,j)(r_{uj}-\bar r_u)}{\sum_{j\in N(i)}|\text{sim}(i,j)|}$

Example
Item-item CF: RMSE 1.012, a little better than the baselines (similarities are noisy with ~46 ratings per film).

5The demo

Rate a few films; your ratings are compared with every other user's, and the users who resemble you predict the rest. Rate sci-fi high and romance low, then the reverse.

6Matrix factorisation

Give every user a vector $\mathbf{p}_u$ and every item a vector $\mathbf{q}_i$ of $k$ hidden factors (tastes), plus biases:

$\hat r_{ui}=\mu+b_u+b_i+\mathbf{p}_u\cdot\mathbf{q}_i$

Learned by minimising squared error on the known ratings, with L2 regularization:

$\min\sum_{\text{known}}(r_{ui}-\hat r_{ui})^2+\lambda(\|\mathbf{p}_u\|^2+\|\mathbf{q}_i\|^2+b_u^2+b_i^2)$

It's the low-rank (SVD) idea from Lesson 2, trained by SGD or ALS. The same dot products fill in the blanks.

7Evaluating: rankings, not ratings

Users see a short list, so measure the top of it:

  • Precision@k: share of the top $k$ that are relevant.
  • Recall@k / hit rate@k.
  • NDCG, MAP: also reward putting good items higher.
Example: top-10 unseen films per user
Random films liked 44.7% of the time; popularity baseline 67.1%; matrix factorisation's personalised lists do better still.
  • Split by time so the model never sees a user's future.
  • Exclude items the user already has.
  • Unobserved ≠ disliked.
  • Final judge: an online A/B test.

8Cold start, content, feedback loops

  • Cold start: a new user or item has no interactions → popularity, a few onboarding questions, or content features (genre, description via TF-IDF or embeddings, price). Hybrids combine both.
  • Two-stage systems: fast retrieval of a few hundred candidates (nearest neighbours of a user embedding), then a ranking model orders them.
  • Feedback loops: users only click what they're shown, so popular items get more exposure and look even better. Add some exploration and watch diversity.

★Going deeper: alternating least squares and popularity bias

Alternating least squares

Solving for two unknowns at once is hard; solving for one while the other is held still is easy. So fix the items and work out every user's tastes, then fix the users and work out every item's, and keep alternating.

One user with one factor
Items rated have factors 1, 2, 3; the user rated them 2, 4, 6. $\lambda=0$.
$Q^\top Q=1+4+9=14$; $Q^\top r=2+8+18=28$.
$p_u=28/14=2$: this user's taste is 2, and $2\times$ each item's factor reproduces the ratings exactly.

Measuring popularity bias

A bookshop that only puts bestsellers in the window. Sales look fine, but most of the books are never shown, niche readers are ignored, and new authors never get a chance.

Reading the rows
Popularity baseline: reaches only 30% of the catalogue; recommended items are liked by 38.7 users on average vs 29.2 overall.
Matrix factorisation: 85% coverage, popularity 30.4, close to average.
In one breath
ALS fits matrix factorisation by alternating simple ridge regressions for users and items, which parallelises well; and always check coverage and popularity of recommendations, not just accuracy.
