Graphical models & HMMs

1Structure in probability

Many uncertain things affect each other: a disease causes symptoms, hidden wear causes noisy sensor readings. One giant table for every combination is hopeless: 30 yes/no variables need over a billion entries. Graphical models use a graph of who directly influences whom and break the joint distribution into small, understandable pieces. (Naive Bayes was one: class → each feature.)

2Bayesian networks

A directed graph without cycles, with one conditional table per node:

$P(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_iP(x_i\mid\text{parents}(x_i))$

Rain makes the sprinkler less likely; both make the grass wet. Three small tables replace one of $2^3$ combinations, and any question is answered by summing over the unknowns (inference by enumeration).

Example from the lesson
Before anything: P(rain) = 0.2. See wet grass → 0.358. Learn the sprinkler was on → 0.007.
Explaining away
One cause (the sprinkler) explains the effect, so the other cause (rain) becomes less likely.

Arrows show the model's structure; don't read them as proven causes without a reason.

3Hidden Markov models

Hidden states $z_1,z_2,\dots$ follow a Markov chain; each emits an observation $x_t$. Three sets of numbers:

  • Initial $\pi_i=P(z_1=i)$.
  • Transition $T_{ij}=P(z_{t+1}=j\mid z_t=i)$.
  • Emission $E_{ik}=P(x_t=k\mid z_t=i)$.

Example: a friend's weather is hidden (Sunny/Rainy); you only hear what they did (walk, shop, clean). Sunny days tend to follow sunny days; on rainy days they tend to clean. The forward algorithm gives the probability of a whole observed sequence.

Don't mix up the transition and emission matrices. And use logs: many small probabilities multiplied underflow to 0.

4Decoding: Viterbi

The single most likely sequence of hidden states: the forward algorithm with the sum replaced by a max, plus back-pointers (dynamic programming, in logs).

Example from the lesson
Shopping on day 3 is ambiguous alone (sunny 0.3, rainy 0.4), but days 4–5 involve cleaning and weather persists, so Viterbi decides the rain started on day 3. Day 6 (shopping) keeps the rain; day 7 (walking) brings the sun back.

The sequence context is what a day-by-day classifier misses.

5The decoding demo

True hidden state, a noisy signal of it, and Viterbi's decoding. Make the state stickier or the signal noisier and compare accuracies.

6Learning the parameters

  • Labelled states: just count (share of sunny days followed by rainy → $T$; share of rainy days with cleaning → $E$). From 5,000 labelled days the counts are within 0.01 of the truth.
  • Unlabelled: Baum–Welch (EM). Finds states only up to their names (it doesn't know which is "Sunny") and can hit local optima, so run it from several starts.

7Why the sequence matters

Example
2,000 new days: Viterbi right 75.2% of the time vs 70.3% for day-by-day guesses (the gain grows with stickier states).
Long-run share of sunny days (the stationary distribution, $T$ raised to a high power): 60%, matching the sample's 59.5%.

HMMs ran speech recognition and part-of-speech tagging for decades; still used for gene finding, activity recognition, regime detection.

8In practice

  • Naive Bayes: class → features. Text, baselines.
  • Bayesian network: any DAG. Diagnosis, risk, causal diagrams.
  • HMM: a hidden chain. Speech, tagging, sensors, regimes.
  • Markov random field / CRF: undirected. Image segmentation, sequence labelling.
  • Kalman filter: an HMM with continuous normal states. Tracking, navigation.

Exact inference is easy for chains and trees, hard for general graphs (use MCMC or variational inference). Libraries: pgmpy, hmmlearn.

★Going deeper: forward–backward and Baum–Welch

Each day's probabilities: forward–backward

To guess whether it rained on Wednesday, use what happened before Wednesday and after it: if Thursday was a cleaning day too, rain on Wednesday becomes more likely. Forward–backward combines evidence from both directions for every day.

Combining both directions for one day
Forward (past) evidence says: rainy 0.4, sunny 0.6.
Backward (future) evidence: the future is twice as likely if today is rainy (0.2 vs 0.1).
Multiply: rainy $0.4\times0.2=0.08$, sunny $0.6\times0.1=0.06$.
Normalise: rainy $0.08/0.14\approx0.57$. The future tipped the balance.

Learning from observations alone: Baum–Welch

Like EM for clustering: guess which hidden state each day was in, count up how often states followed each other and what each state led your friend to do, refit the numbers from those counts, and repeat until they settle.

One M-step count
Forward–backward says: over all days, about 120 "rainy → rainy" transitions and 40 "rainy → sunny" are expected.
New P(rainy → rainy) $=120/(120+40)=0.75$.
Repeat with the new parameters; each round increases the likelihood.
In one breath
Forward–backward combines evidence from before and after each day to give every day's state probabilities; Baum–Welch uses those to learn the model's numbers from unlabelled sequences, from several starting guesses.
✎ in one line: draw who influences whom, multiply small local tables, use Viterbi for the best hidden path and forward–backward for each day's odds.
