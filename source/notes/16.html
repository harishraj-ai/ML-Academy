Clustering

1Learning without labels

Picture it
A shop owner notices groups: families buying in bulk, students buying snacks, office workers buying lunch. Nobody labelled them; the groups emerged from the data.

Most of the world's data has no labels. Clustering finds natural groups: similar inside, different from other groups. Uses: customer segments, grouping news by topic, organising photos, spotting unusual activity, compressing an image to a few colours.

2k-means

Split the data into exactly $k$ clusters, each represented by its centroid (the average of its points). Goal: small inertia:

$\text{inertia}=\sum_i\min_j\|\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol\mu_j\|^2$

Lloyd's algorithm:

  1. Pick $k$ starting centroids.
  2. Assign each point to its nearest centroid.
  3. Update each centroid to the mean of its points.
  4. Repeat until nothing changes.

Each step can only lower the inertia, so it always converges.

3The demo

Step through assign/update and watch the centroids move. On the "moons" data k-means makes round clusters and cuts the two curved shapes in the wrong place.

4Initialisation: k-means++

Converging isn't the same as finding the best: two centroids can start inside one natural group and split it (a local minimum). Fixes:

  • k-means++: each new start centroid is picked with probability ∝ squared distance to the nearest chosen one → spread out (scikit-learn's default).
  • Restarts (n_init): run several times, keep the lowest inertia.

5Choosing k

Inertia always drops as $k$ grows ($k=n$ → 0), so don't just minimise it.

  • Elbow: plot inertia vs $k$; pick where the steep drop flattens.
  • Silhouette: $a$ = average distance to my own cluster, $b$ = to the nearest other cluster.

$s=\dfrac{b-a}{\max(a,b)}$

Near 1 = clearly placed; near 0 = on a border; negative = probably the wrong cluster. Choose the $k$ with the highest average.

Example
$a=2$, $b=6$ → $(6-2)/6\approx$ 0.67.

6Limitations of k-means

  • You must choose $k$ in advance.
  • Assumes round, similar-sized clusters; fails on long, curved or nested shapes.
  • Sensitive to feature scales (standardise) and outliers.
  • Every point is forced into some cluster.

7Hierarchical clustering

Agglomerative: every point starts alone; merge the two closest clusters; repeat until one remains. The merge history is a tree, the dendrogram; cut it at any height for any number of clusters.

"Closest" depends on the linkage: single (closest pair; follows chains and curves), complete (farthest pair; compact), average, Ward (smallest increase in variance; k-means-like).

8DBSCAN: dense regions

Clusters = regions where points are packed closely, separated by sparse regions. Two settings: eps (neighbourhood radius) and min_samples.

  • Core point: at least min_samples points within eps.
  • Border point: not core, but within eps of a core point.
  • Noise: neither → labelled −1.

Finds clusters of any shape, works out how many there are, and flags outliers. Great for crescents + outliers.

9Is the clustering good?

  • Internal scores (silhouette): tightness and separation, but favour round clusters.
  • External scores if some true labels exist: adjusted Rand index (1 = identical, ~0 = random).
  • Usefulness: do the clusters make sense to experts and help the task?

★Going deeper: mixtures, density, hierarchy

Gaussian mixtures and EM

Each point comes from cluster $k$ (probability $\pi_k$), drawn from a normal with mean $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ and covariance $\Sigma_k$, so clusters can be stretched and tilted. Fitted by EM:

  • E-step: for every point, the probability ("responsibility") each cluster produced it (Bayes).
  • M-step: re-estimate $\pi_k,\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k$ weighted by responsibilities.

Never lowers the likelihood; can hit local optima, so restart. k-means = EM with round equal clusters and hard 0/1 responsibilities.

Example from the lesson
Three stretched, tilted blobs: k-means ARI 0.58, Gaussian mixture 1.0. Bonus: soft assignments show which points are genuinely ambiguous.

Any shape: DBSCAN and linkage

Example: two half-moons
DBSCAN 1.000, single linkage 1.000 (follow dense regions). k-means 0.253, Gaussian mixture 0.495, Ward 0.559 (assume compact groups, cut each moon in half).
  • Single linkage risks chaining: a thin bridge of noise merges two clusters.
  • DBSCAN is sensitive to eps and struggles with very different densities → HDBSCAN tries many densities.
  • Dendrograms help when the right level of detail is unknown (segments, taxonomies).

Choosing the number of clusters

  • Silhouette: higher is better.
  • BIC (mixtures): $-2\log L+p\log n$; penalises extra clusters; lower is better.
Example
4 true clusters: silhouette peaks at k = 4 (0.811), BIC is lowest at k = 4 (5133). On real data signals are weaker: also ask whether each segment is useful.

Which method?

  • k-means: round, similar blobs; needs k; scale first.
  • Gaussian mixture: elliptical, soft membership; choose k by BIC.
  • DBSCAN / HDBSCAN: any shape, marks noise; tune eps / min_samples.
  • Agglomerative: a hierarchy; ~$O(n^2)$ memory; chaining with single linkage.
✎ in one line: k-means for round groups, mixtures for stretched ones, DBSCAN for odd shapes and outliers, and check that the groups are useful.
