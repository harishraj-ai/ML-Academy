Linear regression

1The idea

The "hello world" of ML, and still one of the most used models in industry. It predicts a number assuming the label changes in a straight-line way with each feature. Worth learning well: logistic regression, regularized models, SVMs and every neuron in a neural network reuse the same pattern, weights × features + bias.

2The model

$\hat{y}=wx+b\qquad\text{or}\qquad\hat{y}=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b$

$w$ (weight / slope) = change in $\hat{y}$ when $x$ goes up by 1. $b$ (bias / intercept) = the prediction at $x=0$. Two features → a flat plane; more → a "hyperplane".

3What does "best" mean? Least squares

Each residual $r_i=y_i-\hat{y}_i$ is a vertical gap. Ordinary least squares (OLS) minimises the mean of their squares:

$L(w,b)=\frac1n\sum\big(y_i-(wx_i+b)\big)^2$

  • Squaring makes every error positive, so ups and downs don't cancel.
  • Big errors cost much more (an error of 4 costs 16, an error of 1 costs 1).
  • The loss is a smooth bowl with one bottom, so calculus finds it.
Picture it
Each squared residual is the area of a little square. Least squares finds the line with the smallest total area.

4Solving with calculus

At the bottom of the bowl both partial derivatives are zero.

  • From $\partial L/\partial b=0$: $b=\bar{y}-w\bar{x}$, so the best line always passes through the average point $(\bar{x},\bar{y})$.
  • From $\partial L/\partial w=0$: the slope formula below.

$w=\dfrac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}$

5Worked example by hand

Example from the lesson
$x=1,2,3,4,5$; $y=2,4,5,4,5$ → $\bar{x}=3$, $\bar{y}=4$.
Top sum = 6, bottom sum = 10 → $w=$ 0.6, $b=4-0.6\times3=$ 2.2.
Line $\hat{y}=0.6x+2.2$; residuals −0.8, 0.6, 1.0, −0.6, −0.2 → squares sum to 2.4 → MSE = 0.48. Predict $x=6$: 5.8.

Read it as revision hours vs test score: each extra hour goes with about 0.6 more marks.

7How good is the fit? R²

MSE depends on units. $R^2$ compares the model with the laziest baseline, "always predict the mean":

$R^2=1-\dfrac{\text{SS}_{\text{res}}}{\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}}=1-\dfrac{\sum(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2}$

  • 1 = perfect · 0 = no better than the mean · below 0 = worse than the mean (possible on test data).
Example
SSres = 2.4, SStot = 6 → $R^2=1-2.4/6=$ 0.6: the line explains 60% of the variation in scores.

8Many features: the normal equation

Add a column of 1s to $X$ so the bias is just another weight; then $\hat{\mathbf{y}}=X\mathbf{w}$. Setting the gradient $\frac2nX^\top(X\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y})$ to zero gives:

$\mathbf{w}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$

The best weights can be calculated directly. For huge problems, gradient descent (Lesson 7) is faster.

9Reading the weights

price = 2.5·size + 12·bedrooms − 4·distance + 40 (in thousands) → holding the rest fixed, +1 m² ≈ +2.5k, +1 bedroom ≈ +12k, +1 km ≈ −4k.

Two cautions
• Units matter: standardise features first if you want to compare weights.
• Correlation ≠ causation: splitting a room into two won't necessarily add 12k.

10Limitations and fixes

  • Curved relationship → add features like $x^2$, $x^3$ (still linear in the weights).
  • Outliers pull the line a long way.
  • Correlated features make single weights unstable → regularization (Lesson 10).
  • Residuals should look like random noise. A curved pattern means something is missing.

★Going deeper: the statistics behind linear regression

The four assumptions, and what each one buys you

A line always fits; the question is whether its numbers can be trusted. The four assumptions are like a car's safety check: the car may still drive if one fails, but you shouldn't trust the speedometer.

Why squared error? Two classic answers

If errors follow a bell curve, the most believable line is the one with the smallest total squared miss. Squared error isn't an arbitrary choice; it falls out of the bell curve.

How sure are we about each weight? Standard errors, p-values and intervals

Ask ten different samples of patients and you'd get ten slightly different weights. The standard error says how much they would typically differ; a weight many standard errors from zero is very unlikely to be luck.

Bmi's row by hand
Weight about 520 with SE about 66.6.
$t\approx520/66.6\approx7.81$: almost eight wobbles from zero.
Interval $\approx520\pm1.97\times66.6$: about 389 to 651, nowhere near 0.

When features overlap: multicollinearity and the VIF

Two friends always arrive at parties together. If the party gets louder, you can't tell which one caused it. s1 and s2 (two cholesterol measures) are like that: the model knows their joint effect, not each one's.

VIF by hand
Predict s1 from the other features: suppose $R^2=0.9$. VIF $=1/(1-0.9)=10$.
If $R^2=0.98$: VIF $=1/0.02=50$.
s1's actual VIF is 59.2, so its SE is $\sqrt{59.2}\approx7.7$× larger than it would be alone.
Drop s2: SE falls from 416.7 to 114.0, while $R^2$ barely moves (0.5177 → 0.5155).

Adjusted $R^2$, and why cross-validation is better still

Adding a column of random noise is like adding a student who guesses answers to a study group. Training $R^2$ always says the group got "better"; adjusted $R^2$ and cross-validation see through it.

The charge in numbers ($n=442$)
10 features, $R^2=0.518$: $1-0.482\times441/431\approx0.507$.
Suppose 30 features gave $R^2=0.560$: $1-0.440\times441/411\approx0.528$.
A feature must beat chance to raise adjusted $R^2$.

When errors fan out: unequal spread and the log transform

A guess of a junior's salary might be off by ₹1 lakh; a senior's by ₹5 lakh. Errors that grow with size are natural for money. Taking the log turns "± 25%" into one constant error size.

Reading a log weight
Weight for experience: $w=0.0591$.
$e^{0.0591}\approx1.061$, so each extra year means about 6.1% higher salary.
Shortcut for small $w$: $100w\approx$ the percentage, here 5.9%.

Points that bend the line: leverage, Cook's distance and robust regression

A see-saw: a child sitting far from the middle moves it much more than one near the centre. A point far out on the $x$ axis (high leverage) can tilt the whole line, especially if it's also off the line.

What the diagnostics say
With the far point: slope 0.75 instead of about 2.
Its leverage 0.576 is about 9× the median.
Cook's distance 12.73, far above 1; all other points below 0.1.
Huber regression keeps the slope at 1.85, close to the truth.

Categorical features and the dummy-variable trap

If every house is exactly one of Central, Suburb or Outskirts, then knowing two of the yes/no columns tells you the third. Keeping all three plus the bias term says the same thing twice.

Why the three dummies are redundant
Central row: (1, 0, 0). Suburb: (0, 1, 0). Outskirts: (0, 0, 1).
Each row sums to 1, exactly the bias column.
So Central = 1 − Suburb − Outskirts: one column says nothing new.
In one breath
Check the LINE assumptions, read each weight with its standard error and interval, use VIF to spot overlapping features, log money-like targets, hunt for high-leverage points with Cook's distance (or use Huber), drop one dummy per category, and prefer cross-validated $R^2$ over training $R^2$.
✎ in one line: the line that minimises squared gaps, judged by R² against the mean, read carefully and checked for assumptions.
