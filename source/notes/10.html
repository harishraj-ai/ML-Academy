Part 2 · Lesson 10

Overfitting, regularization & bias–variance

1The problem

Lesson 5's degree-11 polynomial threaded every training point and failed on new data. Flexible models can fit more patterns, including noise. But real relationships are often complicated, so we don't want to throw flexibility away. Two questions: why does overfitting happen (bias–variance), and how do we stop it (regularization)?

2Bias and variance

Two students
One memorises last year's paper word for word (high variance: lost if the questions change). The other uses one rule of thumb for everything (high bias: wrong in a predictable way).

Imagine training the same kind of model on many different training sets:

  • Bias: error from wrong assumptions. Even on average it misses the true pattern (a line through a curve) → underfitting.
  • Variance: error from sensitivity to the particular training set; each set gives a very different model → overfitting.

expected error = bias² + variance + noise

Noise is the randomness no model can remove.

3The trade-off

More complexity → bias falls, variance rises. Total error is lowest in between, at the bottom of Lesson 5's U-curve.

Diagnose
Train high, test high and similar → high bias → more flexible model, better features, less regularization.
Train low, test much higher → high variance → more data, simpler model, more regularization, fewer features.

More data reduces variance: noise averages out, which is why large datasets let us use very complex models. When you can't get more data, regularize.

4Regularization: penalise complexity

Overfitted models usually have huge weights that cancel each other out to bend sharply through noisy points. So add a penalty for large weights:

loss = data loss (fit the data) + $\lambda\cdot$penalty (keep weights small)

  • $\lambda=0$: plain least squares. Small $\lambda$: gentle push. Large $\lambda$: weights forced to 0, may underfit.

It trades a little bias for a big drop in variance. The bias term $b$ isn't penalised (it only shifts predictions). In scikit-learn $\lambda$ is called alpha.

5Ridge (L2)

$L=\sum(y_i-\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i-b)^2+\lambda\sum w_j^2,\qquad\mathbf{w}=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$

Bonus: adding $\lambda I$ means the matrix always has an inverse, fixing Lesson 6's perfectly correlated features.

Example from the lesson
$x=1,2,3$, $y=2,4,7$ → $w=\frac{31}{14+\lambda}$.
λ = 0: 2.214 · λ = 1: 2.067 · λ = 10: 1.292 · λ = 100: 0.272. The weight shrinks smoothly towards 0.

6Lasso (L1)

$L=\frac{1}{2n}\sum(y_i-\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i-b)^2+\lambda\sum|w_j|$

Lasso drives many weights to exactly zero, switching off unhelpful features: automatic feature selection and simpler, more readable models.

Why exact zeros?
The L2 penalty $w^2$ has slope $2w$, which fades near 0, so weights get small but rarely reach it. The L1 penalty $|w|$ has slope ±1 all the way down: a constant push that can pin a weight at 0.

7The demo

A degree-9 polynomial on 12 noisy points with ridge: slide λ from almost 0 (wild overfitting, huge weights) to very large (an almost flat line that underfits). The sweet spot is in between.

8Always scale first

A feature in millimetres needs a weight 1000× smaller than the same feature in metres. Without scaling, the penalty punishes some features unfairly. Standardise inside a pipeline (fitted on training data only).

9Choosing λ

λ is a hyperparameter. Try values spaced by factors (0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100), score each with cross-validation, pick the best, retrain on all training data. RidgeCV and LassoCV do this for you.

Logistic regression too
L2 by default, controlled by C $=1/\lambda$: smaller C = stronger. penalty="l1" (with "liblinear" or "saga") for lasso-style selection.

10Other ways to fight overfitting

  • More training data (most reliable).
  • A simpler model: fewer features, lower degree, shallower trees.
  • Early stopping (Lesson 7).
  • Ensembles: averaging reduces variance (Lesson 14).
  • Dropout and data augmentation for neural networks (Lesson 19).

★Going deeper: regularization in practice

Measuring bias and variance

Throw darts many times. Bias is how far the average landing spot is from the bullseye. Variance is how scattered the darts are. A model trained on many different training sets is like many darts.

Reading the rows
$\lambda=10^{-6}$ (almost no penalty): bias² 0.001, variance 1.133. Every curve is different and wild.
$\lambda=10$ (strong penalty): variance 0.025, but bias² 0.335. Every curve is flat and wrong.
$\lambda=0.1$: bias² 0.100 + variance 0.050 + noise 0.09 = 0.240, the best total.
This is the U-shaped trade-off from the lesson, with real numbers.

Why lasso gives exact zeros: soft-thresholding

Ridge turns every weight's volume down by the same percentage: loud stays fairly loud, quiet becomes quieter, but nothing goes silent. Lasso subtracts a fixed amount from every weight: anything quieter than that amount becomes completely silent.

The four rows with $\lambda=1$
3.0: lasso $3-1=2.0$; ridge $3/2=1.5$.
0.8: lasso $\max(0.8-1,0)=0$; ridge $0.4$.
−0.3: lasso 0; ridge −0.15.
−2.5: lasso $-(2.5-1)=-1.5$; ridge −1.25.
Ridge never gives exactly 0; lasso does for every $|z|<1$.

Choosing the penalty automatically

Try a whole ladder of penalty strengths, score each with cross-validation, and keep the best. The CV versions of ridge and lasso do this for you, reusing work between steps so it's barely slower than one fit.

Lasso and correlated features: why elastic net exists

Two equally good twins apply for one job. Lasso hires exactly one of them, almost at random, and the choice can flip if you ask again. Elastic net hires both and splits the salary.

Reading the counts
Only a kept: 47 times. Only b kept: 63 times. Both: 90 times.
So "lasso picked a" from one fit is close to a coin flip.
Elastic net gives both 0.89: the credit is shared evenly.

Early stopping is regularization too

Training starts with all weights at zero, and they grow step by step. Stop early and they haven't had time to grow big, just as if a penalty had held them back.

Steps as a penalty
$\eta=0.01$, $k=100$ steps: $\lambda\approx1/(100\times0.01)=1$.
$k=1{,}000$ steps: $\lambda\approx0.1$, a weaker penalty.
So more steps = less regularization.

Double descent: when bigger models get better again

Test error first falls as the model grows, then rises as it starts fitting noise, peaking where it can just barely fit every training point. Keep growing it and, surprisingly, the error falls again.

In one breath
Bias is the average miss and variance the wobble, and you can measure both; lasso subtracts and so zeroes small weights while ridge only shrinks; choose the penalty by cross-validation; use elastic net for correlated features; early stopping acts like a penalty; and very large models can improve again past the interpolation peak.
✎ in one line: balance bias against variance; a penalty on weights trades a little bias for much less variance.
