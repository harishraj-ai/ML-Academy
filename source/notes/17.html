Dimensionality reduction & PCA

1Too many features

The teapot
A photo from its best angle shows almost everything about its shape; a photo straight down the spout shows very little. PCA finds the "best angles" to look at your data.

An 8×8 digit image = 64 numbers; a photo = millions; a customer record = hundreds of columns. Problems: the curse of dimensionality, overfitting, slowness, and we can't see more than 3 dimensions. Luckily real features are often redundant (height in cm and in inches carry the same information).

2Keep the directions with most variance

Points lying roughly along a diagonal differ mainly in one way: how far along the line. Rotate the axes so the first runs along the line: it captures almost all the differences; the perpendicular one is nearly constant (mostly noise) and can be dropped.

PC1 = the direction of greatest spread; PC2 = the greatest spread at right angles to PC1; and so on. Keep only the first few.

3The demo

Rotate a line and watch the variance of the projected points. The best angle (PC1) maximises the kept variance and, at the same time, minimises the squared length of the projection lines (the information lost).

4Covariance

$\text{cov}(x_1,x_2)=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_{i1}-\bar x_1)(x_{i2}-\bar x_2)$

  • Positive: above average together (height and weight).
  • Negative: one up, the other down.
  • About 0: no linear relationship.

A feature's covariance with itself is its variance. All pairs in a table = the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ ($d\times d$, symmetric).

5Eigenvectors: PCA's directions

The principal components are the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$, and the variance along each is its eigenvalue.

$\Sigma\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$

The matrix only stretches $\mathbf{v}$ (by λ), never turns it. For a covariance matrix the eigenvectors are perpendicular and the eigenvalues ≥ 0. Explained variance of component $j$ = $\lambda_j/\sum\lambda$.

6PCA by hand

Example from the lesson
Points (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 6).
Centre (means 3, 4): (−2, −2), (−1, −1), (0, 1), (1, 0), (2, 2).
Covariance: var 2.5 and 2.5, cov 2.25.
Eigenvalues: $2.5\pm2.25$ → 4.75 and 0.25.
PC1 = $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ (the diagonal); check: $\Sigma(1,1)=(4.75,4.75)$ ✓. PC2 = $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$.
PC1 explains $4.75/5=$ 95% of the variance.

7PCA step by step

  1. Centre each feature (and usually standardise if units differ, or the biggest numbers dominate).
  2. Covariance matrix (or the SVD directly).
  3. Eigenvectors and eigenvalues, sorted largest first.
  4. Keep the top $k$ as columns of $W_k$.
  5. Project: $Z=X_cW_k$ ($n\times k$ new features).

Reconstruct approximately: $\hat X=ZW_k^\top+\bar{\mathbf{x}}$.

8How many components?

  • Variance threshold, e.g. 95%: PCA(n_components=0.95).
  • Scree plot: eigenvalues in order, look for an elbow.
  • Feeding a model: choose $k$ by cross-validation.
  • Pictures: $k$ = 2 or 3.
Example
Eigenvalues 6, 3, 1 → first two explain $9/10=$ 90%.

10t-SNE and UMAP

For curved structure (like the moons), a flat projection can squash groups on top of each other. t-SNE places points in 2-D so that neighbours stay neighbours; its perplexity ≈ how many neighbours each point attends to. UMAP is newer and faster.

Reading t-SNE safely
Cluster sizes mean nothing; distances between separated clusters may mean nothing; low perplexity can make pure noise look clustered; different runs differ.

★Going deeper: components and curved maps

How many components? Reconstruction error

Keeping $k$ components has an exact cost: the reconstruction error equals the variance you dropped, and the top $k$ PCs give the smallest error of any $k$-dimensional flat subspace (Eckart–Young).

Example: 8×8 digits (64 pixels)
2 components keep only 28.5%; 29 are needed for 95%. At 30 components 0.959 is kept and the relative error is 0.041 = 1 − 0.959.

Side job: anomaly detection, since points the main components can't reconstruct don't follow the usual pattern.

Kernel PCA

PCA finds only flat directions. Kernel PCA uses the kernel trick to find components in a high-dimensional feature space.

Example: concentric circles
Ordinary PCA's one component: 0.450 (worse than guessing). RBF kernel PCA's first component: 1.000; "distance from the centre" becomes a straight direction.

Needs the $n\times n$ kernel matrix, so it doesn't scale to huge data, and its components are hard to interpret.

t-SNE and UMAP: maps for looking

t-SNE turns distances into neighbour probabilities (a normal curve whose width is set by the perplexity in the original space; a heavier-tailed $t$-distribution on the map) and moves the map points until they match. UMAP keeps more of the global layout.

Example
A digit's 5 nearest map neighbours share its label 97.6% of the time with t-SNE vs 60.3% with a flat PCA projection: hence t-SNE's ten neat islands.
  • Distances between clusters and cluster sizes mean little.
  • Try several perplexities and seeds.
  • For looking, not modelling: no transform for new points, meaningless axes. Use PCA (or UMAP) for features.

Practical notes

  • Fit PCA inside the pipeline on training data only.
  • Standardise first when units differ.
  • Whitening rescales components to variance 1, but amplifies noise in small components.
  • Huge or sparse data: TruncatedSVD (on TF-IDF it's latent semantic analysis), IncrementalPCA, randomized SVD.
  • A linear autoencoder with squared error learns the same subspace as PCA.
