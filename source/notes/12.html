Naive Bayes

1A classifier from Bayes' theorem

Lesson 4 used Bayes to update a belief from evidence. Naive Bayes turns that into a classifier: evidence = the features, hypothesis = the class. The classic spam filters of the early 2000s were Naive Bayes, and it's still useful: trains almost instantly, needs little data, great on text.

2Bayes for classification

$P(y\mid\mathbf{x})=\dfrac{P(y)\,P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)}{P(\mathbf{x})}$

  • $P(y)$ = prior: how common the class is (40% of emails are spam).
  • $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$ = likelihood: how probable these features are for this class.
  • $P(\mathbf{x})$ is the same for every class, so ignore it when comparing (divide by the total at the end for real probabilities).

3The "naive" assumption

$P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$ for a whole combination of thousands of words is impossible to count. So assume the features are independent given the class, and just multiply:

$\hat y=\arg\max_y\;P(y)\prod_jP(x_j\mid y)$

Usually false ("free" and "money" appear together), but the classifier only needs to get the ranking of classes right, not the exact probabilities, so it often works well anyway.

4A spam filter by hand

The lesson's data
10 emails: 4 spam ("win money now", "free money offer", "win a free prize now", "claim your free prize"), 6 ham. Is "free money now" spam?
Priors: P(spam) = 0.4, P(ham) = 0.6.
Counts: spam has 15 words, ham 25; vocabulary 24 words.
Laplace smoothing (+1)
$P(w\mid c)=\dfrac{\text{count}+1}{\text{total words in }c+\text{vocab}}$
Without it, "free" never appearing in ham → P = 0 → the whole ham score is 0. One word would kill the class.
Word probabilities
spam: free (3+1)/39 = 0.1026, money 3/39 = 0.0769, now 3/39 = 0.0769
ham: free 1/49 = 0.0204, money 0.0204, now 2/49 = 0.0408
spam score = 0.4 × 0.1026 × 0.0769 × 0.0769 ≫ ham score → spam.

5Work with logs

Hundreds of numbers like 0.001 multiplied together underflow to exactly 0. Logs turn products into sums and keep the order:

$\ln P(y)+\sum_j\ln P(x_j\mid y)$

Same email in log space
spam: −0.916 − 2.277 − 2.565 − 2.565 = −8.32
ham: −0.511 − 3.892 − 3.892 − 3.199 ≈ −11.49 → spam wins.

6The demo

Type a message: each word adds a bar of length $\ln\frac{P(w\mid\text{spam})}{P(w\mid\text{ham})}$ towards spam or ham. Unseen words are ignored. Try "win a free lunch" or "team meeting now".

7Variants

Same formula; only $P(x_j\mid y)$ changes:

  • Multinomial: word counts → text.
  • Bernoulli: present/absent → short texts, yes/no features.
  • Gaussian: a bell curve per class and feature → measurements.
  • Complement: statistics from all other classes → imbalanced text.
  • Categorical: smoothed frequency of each category.

8Strengths and weaknesses

+ extremely fast (just counting)
+ little data, many features
+ many classes naturally
+ easy to explain
− independence is usually false
− probabilities often over-confident
− can't learn interactions
− Gaussian NB assumes bell shapes
A great baseline
Quick to try, hard to break. If a complex model can't beat it, the complexity isn't earning its keep.

★Going deeper: smoothing, text, generative models

Where smoothing comes from

Example from the lesson
Two messages with words seen in only one class. Almost no smoothing (α = 10⁻¹⁰): answers of exactly 0 and 1, decided by a single word. With α = 1: a sensible 0.184 and 0.735.

$P(\text{word }j\mid c)=\dfrac{\text{count}_{j,c}+\alpha}{\text{total}_c+\alpha V}$

It's not a hack: it's the Bayesian posterior mean with a symmetric Dirichlet prior (the many-category Beta), like having seen every word α extra times. α is a regularization strength: tune by CV (0.01–1 is common for text).

Reading what it learned

Each word adds $\log P(w\mid\text{spam})-\log P(w\mid\text{ham})$ to the spam log-odds every time it appears.

Example
"win", "cash", "now", "prize": +1.07 each. "monday", "meeting": −1.12.

So the log-odds is a weighted sum of word counts: multinomial NB is a linear classifier, like logistic regression, with weights set by counting instead of optimisation.

A realistic text pipeline

  • Bigrams (word pairs), so "not good" isn't "not" + "good".
  • TF-IDF: term frequency × inverse document frequency; down-weights words in every document ("the", "to").
  • Complement NB: more stable with imbalanced classes, often beats standard multinomial on text.

Even on 8 messages it classifies both new ones correctly with 47 features. Also try a linear SVM or logistic regression on the same TF-IDF features; modern alternatives use embeddings or a fine-tuned transformer.

Generative vs discriminative

Generative (NB, LDA, mixtures)
learns how each class produces data, $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$
· good with little data
· handles missing features naturally
· can generate examples
· probabilities often badly calibrated
Discriminative (logistic regression, SVM, neural nets)
learns the boundary $P(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ directly
· needs more data
· usually more accurate with lots of data
· better calibrated

Ng and Jordan (2001): NB reaches its best error with far fewer examples (about logarithmic in the number of features), logistic regression needs more (about linear) but ends lower when independence is wrong. Today's generative AI is the generative idea at enormous scale.
