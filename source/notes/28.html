Bayesian & probabilistic ML

1Uncertain about the model itself

Most models give one answer: one set of weights. But with 10 data points the weights are very uncertain and with 10 million they're not, and a single number hides that. Bayesian ML treats every unknown, parameters included, as uncertain, describes it with distributions, and updates them as data arrives. You get honest error bars, sensible behaviour with little data, a way to include prior knowledge, and risk-aware decisions.

2Bayes for parameters

$p(\theta\mid D)=\dfrac{p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(D)}\;\propto\;\text{likelihood}\times\text{prior}$

  • Max of the likelihood → MLE. Max of the posterior → MAP (= ridge or lasso for normal or Laplace priors).
  • A conjugate prior (Beta for a probability) gives a closed-form posterior.

This lesson uses the whole posterior, not just its peak.

3A Bayesian A/B test

Page A: 120 of 2,400 visitors converted; B: 156 of 2,400 (classical $p=0.026$). Give each rate a flat Beta(1, 1) prior and update:

The posterior by hand
Beta($a$, $b$) + $s$ successes + $f$ failures → Beta($a+s$, $b+f$).
B: Beta(157, 2,245), mean $157/2402\approx$ 0.0654, close to 156/2400, because 2,400 visitors overwhelm a weak prior.

Then sample both posteriors and answer the manager's questions directly: P(B better than A) and the expected loss if we pick B and it's worse.

4The updating demo

A prior centred on 10%; add visitors converting at about 8%. A stronger prior needs more data to move; each 100 visitors narrows the curve.

5Bayesian linear regression

Prior $\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2I)$, noise variance $\sigma^2$ → a normal posterior:

$\Sigma_{\text{post}}=\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}X^\top X+\tfrac{1}{\tau^2}I\Big)^{-1},\qquad\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}=\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\Sigma_{\text{post}}X^\top\mathbf{y}$

The mean is exactly ridge with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$. New: the covariance gives every prediction its own error bar, $\boldsymbol\phi^\top\Sigma_{\text{post}}\boldsymbol\phi+\sigma^2$, which widens away from the data.

6Gaussian processes

A prior directly over functions, controlled by a kernel (how similar nearby outputs are). After data: a mean and a standard deviation everywhere.

Example from the lesson
At x = 2 (data): sd ≈ 0. At x = 4.5 (a gap): 0.45. At x = 11 (far beyond): back to the prior, mean ≈ 0, sd ≈ 1.

Excellent for small, smooth problems (calibration, sensors, experiment design, Bayesian optimisation), but cost grows like $O(n^3)$.

7MCMC: when there's no formula

Metropolis: propose a small random move; if the posterior is higher, accept; if lower, accept with probability = the ratio. The chain visits regions in proportion to their posterior probability, and the awkward $p(D)$ cancels in the ratio.

Example
Centre of 7 measurements, one suspicious (8.5), with a heavy-tailed Cauchy likelihood (robust): about half the proposals accepted (healthy); posterior mean 2.76, 95% interval 2.2 to 3.28.

Stan and PyMC use smarter samplers (HMC/NUTS).

8Thompson sampling

A classic A/B test keeps sending half the visitors to the worse page. A multi-armed bandit learns and earns at once: for each visitor, draw a plausible rate for each design from its posterior, show the highest draw, update.

Example
True rates 4%, 5%, 6.5%: 9,188 of 10,000 visitors saw the best design; 652 conversions vs about 517 for an even split.
Use a bandit when…
…you care about earning while learning (headlines, ads). Use a classic test when you need an unbiased, precise comparison of every option.

★Going deeper: prior sensitivity and checking MCMC

How much does the prior matter?

Three people with different opinions read the same growing pile of evidence. After a page, they still disagree. After a whole book, they agree. If the answer still depends on who's reading, you need more evidence.

Posterior means with 50 visitors (3 converted)
Flat: $(1+3)/(2+50)\approx0.077$.
Sceptical: $(10+3)/(200+50)=0.052$.
Optimistic: $(30+3)/(200+50)=0.132$.
The priors still dominate: 50 real visitors vs 200 imaginary ones.

Has the sampler converged?

Send four hikers from four different corners of a park to map it. If their maps agree, trust them. If each hiker only mapped the area around their own starting point, the maps disagree, and none of them is the whole park.

In one breath
Re-run the analysis with several reasonable priors: if they agree, the data dominates; and run several MCMC chains from different starts, trusting the results only when R-hat is close to 1.
✎ in one line: keep a distribution over the unknowns, update it with data, check the prior's influence, and use the uncertainty to decide.
