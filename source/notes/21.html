Ethics & fairness in ML

1Why it's an ML problem

A bank replaces loan officers with a model trained on ten years of their decisions: 88% accurate, fast, consistent. Fair? Accuracy can't tell you. If past officers turned down qualified applicants from one neighbourhood, the model learned to do the same, automatically, at scale, and with an air of objectivity.

Data records the world as it was, unfairness included. A model doesn't need to see ethnicity or gender to discriminate: proxies like postcode, first name or an employment gap do the job.

2Kinds of harm

  • Allocation: a loan, interview or referral withheld from some groups more than others.
  • Quality of service: the system works worse for some people (face or speech recognition).
  • Representation: outputs reinforce stereotypes or erase groups.
  • Privacy and autonomy: profiling, re-identification, manipulation with data people never agreed to share.

3Where bias comes from

  • Historical: the world was unequal, so even a perfect record teaches it.
  • Representation (sampling): under-represented groups get a less accurate model.
  • Measurement / label: the label is a proxy (healthcare cost standing in for health need under-served Black patients).
  • Aggregation: one model for everyone ignores real group differences.
  • Evaluation: test data that doesn't represent everyone.
  • Feedback loops: decisions shape the next round of data.

4Measuring fairness: compare groups

Compute the confusion matrix per group, then compare:

  • Selection rate $\frac{TP+FP}{\text{all}}$ equal → demographic parity.
  • TPR $\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$ equal → equal opportunity (qualified people approved equally).
  • TPR and FPR equal → equalised odds.
  • Precision $\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$ equal → predictive parity.
Example: the four-fifths rule
Group A approved 0.50, group B 0.30 → demographic-parity ratio $0.30/0.50=$ 0.6, below 0.8 → flagged for possible adverse impact.

5The two-thresholds demo

Group A: half would repay; group B: one in five. Make the selection rates equal, then the TPRs, and watch precision and accuracy move. You can't fix everything at once.

6The impossibility result

$\text{FPR}=\dfrac{p}{1-p}\cdot\dfrac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot\text{TPR}$

($p$ = the group's base rate.) If two groups have the same PPV and the same TPR but different $p$, their FPRs must differ (Chouldechova, 2017). Unless the classifier is perfect.

Example from the lesson
Group A: $p=0.5$, PPV = 40/45, TPR = 0.8 → FPR = $1\times0.125\times0.8=$ 0.10 ✓.
So it's a value judgement
When base rates differ, choose which fairness criterion matters with the people affected.

7What you can do

  • Fix the data: more data for under-represented groups, labels that measure what you care about, rethink proxy features, re-weight.
  • Constrain the model: a fairness penalty in the loss (Fairlearn, AIF360).
  • Adjust decisions: per-group thresholds (effective, but can be legally restricted).
  • Change the system: a human in the loop, explanations, a way to appeal, monitoring.
Deleting the protected column doesn't fix it (proxies). And always split metrics by group, never only overall accuracy.

8Privacy, transparency, accountability

  • Privacy: collect only what you need, with consent. Models can leak training data; differential privacy adds calibrated noise so no single record changes the result much.
  • Transparency: interpretable models for high-stakes decisions; datasheets for data, model cards for models.
  • Accountability: someone is responsible and decisions can be contested. The EU AI Act requires risk management, documentation and human oversight for high-risk uses (hiring, credit).

9Checklist before deploying

  1. Purpose: who's affected; is ML appropriate at all?
  2. Data: who's missing; is the label a fair proxy?
  3. Metrics: selection rate, TPR, FPR, PPV for every group, with uncertainty.
  4. Choose the criterion with stakeholders.
  5. Document: a model card.
  6. Monitor group metrics and feedback loops; provide explanations and appeals.

★Going deeper: the impossibility result, measured

The identity behind the conflict

Imagine three dials joined by one gear: the base rate, precision and the two error rates. Turn one and the others must move. If two groups start with different base rates, you can't set all the other dials equal for both. It isn't a bad model; it's arithmetic.

Same precision and TPR, different base rates
Precision 0.6 and TPR 0.7 in both groups.
Group with $p=0.3$: FPR $=\frac{0.3}{0.7}\cdot\frac{0.4}{0.6}\cdot0.7=0.20$.
Group with $p=0.5$: FPR $=1\cdot\frac{0.4}{0.6}\cdot0.7\approx0.47$.
Equal precision and TPR force very different false-alarm rates.

A perfectly calibrated model can still have unequal error rates

Two honest weather forecasters, one for a dry city and one for a wet city. Both are calibrated: "70% rain" really means rain 70% of the time. Yet with the same "carry an umbrella above 50%" rule, people in the two cities get very different numbers of false alarms and misses.

Group-specific thresholds: what they buy and what they cost

Lowering the bar for one group catches more of its real positives, but also flags more of its people wrongly. Every adjustment trades one kind of fairness or accuracy for another.

What moved
Group A's threshold 0.5 → 0.305: TPR 0.232 → 0.634, matching B's 0.631.
FPRs closer too: 0.320 vs 0.362.
Cost: group A's accuracy 0.736 → 0.667, and equal precision is lost.

Small groups: put error bars on fairness metrics

A survey of 10 people can swing wildly; one of 1,000 barely moves. A fairness gap measured on a small group might be mostly noise.

The wobble of a TPR on 106 positives
TPR 0.226 on 106 people.
Rough wobble: $\sqrt{0.226\times0.774/106}\approx0.041$.
95% range ≈ $0.226\pm2\times0.041$: about 0.14 to 0.31.
In one breath
When base rates differ, equal precision and equal error rates can't all hold, by simple arithmetic; even a perfectly calibrated model has unequal error rates; group thresholds trade one metric for another; small groups need error bars; and the choice of which harm matters most is a human decision made before measuring.
✎ in one line: measure every metric per group with error bars, choose a fairness goal with the people affected, and build checks around the model.
