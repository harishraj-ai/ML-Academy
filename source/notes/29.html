Causal inference

1Prediction is not causation

So far: "what will happen?". Many business questions are: "what will happen if we act?" Will a discount raise spending? Did the new app cause the rise in orders? Who should get the coupon? A model that predicts well can still answer these badly, because of confounding: something that affects both the action and the outcome. If loyal customers get more discounts and spend more anyway, discount-takers spend more even if the discount does nothing.

2Potential outcomes

For each customer: $Y_i(1)$ = spend with a discount, $Y_i(0)$ = without. Individual effect = the difference; the average treatment effect:

$\text{ATE}=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)]$

We never see both for the same person (the "fundamental problem of causal inference"). The naive comparison equals the ATE only if both groups would have spent the same without the discount.

Example from the lesson
True effect +10, but loyalty drives both discounts and spending: the naive difference is 35.7, more than 3× too big.

3The confounder demo

Increase how much loyalty decides who gets a discount: the naive difference grows while the adjusted estimate stays near the true 10. At 0 (random discounts) they agree.

4The gold standard: randomise

If a coin decides, loyalty can't influence the choice, so the groups are alike on average in everything, measured or not, and a simple difference in means is unbiased.

Example
Randomised estimate 10.9, 95% interval 9.3 to 12.6 (contains the true 10).

Randomise whenever you can. When you can't (unethical, too slow, already decided), use the methods below and defend their assumptions.

5Adjusting for confounders

  • Regression adjustment: include the confounders; the treatment's coefficient is the effect holding them fixed.
  • Propensity scores $e(x)=P(T=1\mid x)$: weight treated people by $1/e(x)$ and untreated by $1/(1-e(x))$ (IPW) to build a pseudo-population where treatment is unrelated to the confounders.
Example
A treated person with propensity 0.2 gets weight $1/0.2=$ 5: they stand in for the many similar people who weren't treated.
Two assumptions
No unmeasured confounding (you measured everything that drives both) and overlap (everyone had some chance of either). Trim extreme weights near 0 or 1.

6Difference-in-differences

A change hits a whole group at once (a city launches an app). Before/after mixes the effect with everything else that changed; use a similar untreated group to measure the background change:

$\text{DiD}=(\bar y_{B,\text{after}}-\bar y_{B,\text{before}})-(\bar y_{A,\text{after}}-\bar y_{A,\text{before}})$

Example
City B's orders rose 13.4 after the launch; city A (no app) also rose with the season. Subtracting → 7.5, close to the true 8.

Key assumption: parallel trends, i.e. without the app, B would have moved like A.

7Uplift modelling: who benefits?

An average hides variation: a coupon changes some customers and does nothing for others (they'd buy anyway, or never). Estimate the effect per person, $\tau(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid x]$.

T-learner: on randomised data, one model for treated, one for untreated; uplift = the difference of their predictions.

Example
Top 30% by predicted uplift: the coupon raises buying by 17.9 points; the rest only 9.0. Predictions correlate 0.64 with the true effects.
✗ target by predicted purchase (sends coupons to people who'd buy anyway) · ✓ target by predicted uplift.

8In practice

  • Can randomise → A/B test (no interference between units).
  • Rich observational data → regression / propensity weighting / matching (no unmeasured confounding, overlap).
  • A change at a known time → difference-in-differences (parallel trends).
  • A threshold decides → regression discontinuity.
  • Targeting → uplift / CATE models (T-learner, causal forests).

Draw a causal diagram first: adjust for confounders, but never for things the treatment causes.

★Going deeper: bad controls, discontinuities

Don't adjust for what the treatment causes

A mediator lies on the path from treatment to outcome; adjusting for it removes part of the effect.

Example from the lesson
A randomised discount brings customers back more often, and all its effect on spending works through those visits. Unadjusted: 11.96 (true 12). "Controlling" for visits: about 0, as if the discount did nothing.
  • Confounder → adjust.
  • Mediator → don't (unless you want only the direct effect).
  • Collider (caused by both, e.g. "contacted support") → never; it creates fake associations.

Regression discontinuity

When a threshold decides treatment (credit score ≥ 600), people just above and just below are very similar: almost an experiment. Fit a line on each side near the cut-off; measure the jump.

Example
Naive difference 29.8 (high scorers spend more anyway). Discontinuity within 25 points of 600: 14.7, close to the true 15.

Assumptions: people can't precisely manipulate their score across the line, and nothing else changes exactly at 600. Check for bunching just above the cut-off.

✎ in one line: ask "what if we act?", randomise if you can, otherwise adjust only for true confounders and state your assumptions.
