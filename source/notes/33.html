Survival analysis

1Not "whether" but "when"

How long until a customer cancels, a machine fails, a loan defaults, a patient recovers? Survival (time-to-event) analysis models these times. The special challenge is censoring: for many people the event hasn't happened yet. A customer who joined 3 months ago and is still here hasn't survived "3 months"; they've survived at least 3 months.

Treat them as churned at 3 months → you underestimate how long people stay. Drop them → you lose information and bias towards short times.

2Censored data

Each row: a time and an event flag (1 = churned then; 0 = still subscribed when the study ended: right-censored).

Example from the lesson
600 subscribers, 106 censored. Averaging the observed times gives 6.02 months, well below the true 7.86: censored people are counted when we stopped watching, not when they eventually left.

3Kaplan–Meier

$S(t)$ = probability of still being a customer after time $t$. At each churn time, with $n_k$ still at risk and $d_k$ churning:

$\hat S(t)=\prod_{t_k\le t}\left(1-\dfrac{d_k}{n_k}\right)$

By hand
Churned at 2; censored at 3; churned at 4 and 4; censored at 6.
t = 2: 5 at risk, 1 churns → $S=0.8$.
t = 3: censored, no step, but the risk set drops to 3.
t = 4: 3 at risk, 2 churn → $S=0.8\times(1-2/3)\approx$ 0.267. Then flat.

Report the median survival time (where the curve crosses 0.5): the mean depends on a long tail that censoring hides.

Example
40 at risk, 4 churn → multiply by $1-4/40=$ 0.9.

4The demo

True medians 4.9 and 9.7 months. Increase the share censored: Kaplan–Meier still recovers curves and medians close to the truth.

5Comparing groups: log-rank test

At each churn time, compare the churns in one group with what you'd expect if both had the same risk, given who was at risk; sum into a chi-squared with 1 degree of freedom.

Example
Annual vs monthly plans: chi-squared 55.0, p ≈ $10^{-13}$: annual customers really do stay longer.

That says the difference isn't chance, not how big or why. Annual customers may differ in other ways, so it's no proof that switching plans would make someone stay.

6The Cox model

Model the hazard: the instant churn rate among those still at risk.

$h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)\exp(\beta_1x_1+\dots+\beta_px_p)$

The baseline $h_0(t)$ can have any shape (never estimated directly); features multiply it by a constant. $e^{\beta_j}$ = the hazard ratio per unit of $x_j$ (assumes proportional hazards). Fitted by the partial likelihood: at each churn, the chance it was this customer among everyone at risk.

Examples
Annual plan: hazard ratio 0.50 → at any moment annual customers churn at about half the rate.
$\beta=0.4$ for missed payments → each one multiplies the hazard by $e^{0.4}\approx$ 1.49.

7Evaluating survival models

You don't know true times for censored people, so use the concordance index: among pairs where we know who churned first, the share where the model gave the earlier churner a higher risk. 0.5 random, 1 perfect: survival's ROC-AUC.

Example
Cox 0.639 · support tickets alone 0.59 · random 0.517. Values of 0.6–0.75 are typical (churn timing is noisy).

Also: the Brier score over time, random survival forests, gradient boosting with survival losses.

8In practice

  • Time zero: start everyone's clock at a comparable moment (sign-up, installation, diagnosis).
  • Non-informative censoring: people mustn't drop out of view because they were about to churn.
  • No features from the future: only values known at time zero (or time-varying models).
  • Competing risks (cancelled vs moved to a partner): treating one as censoring for the other biases estimates.

★Going deeper: checking proportional hazards, RMST

When hazards aren't proportional

A Cox model reports one hazard ratio for the whole period. If an advantage fades (a welcome offer that helps only early), that single number misleads.

Example from the lesson
First 6 months: group 1 churns at 0.39× group 0's rate. After that: 1.04×, no advantage. A single Cox ratio would average to about 0.6, true of no period.

Fixes: separate coefficients per period, stratify, or report the curves. Check visually: $\log(-\log\hat S(t))$ lines should be parallel.

Restricted mean survival time (RMST)

The average time survived within a fixed horizon = the area under the Kaplan–Meier curve up to it. No proportional-hazards assumption needed.

Example
First 12 months: group 1 stays 7.71 months on average, group 0 5.45 → 2.3 more months per customer, which converts directly into revenue.

"2.3 more months in the first year" is easier for decision-makers than a hazard ratio.

✎ in one line: model "when", keep censored people in, use Kaplan–Meier and Cox, judge with the C-index, and check the hazards stay proportional.
