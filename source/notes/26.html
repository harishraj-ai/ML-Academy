Time series forecasting

1Why time is different

Umbrellas next week, electricity tomorrow evening, patients on Saturday night: predict future values from past ones. Time changes the rules:

  • Rows aren't independent: today looks like yesterday.
  • The future can differ from the past (trends, holidays, a pandemic).
  • A random split is badly wrong: it trains on the future (Lesson 5's shuffled split reported R² = 0.96 for a model that couldn't forecast at all).

2Trend, seasonality, noise

  • Trend: long-term direction. Seasonality: repeats with a fixed period (weekday, month, hour). Noise: the unpredictable rest.
  • Additive (trend + season + noise) or multiplicative (swings grow with the level, typical of sales; take logs to make it additive).
Example: 3 years of simulated shop sales
Saturday ≈ 383 vs Monday ≈ 254 (weekly pattern); yearly averages 245 → 304 → 361 (trend).

3Baselines first

  • Naive: tomorrow = today.
  • Seasonal naive: tomorrow = the same day last week.
  • Moving average: the average of the last $k$ days.
Example (one day ahead, last 8 weeks)
Seasonal naive MAE 32.6 · 28-day average 45.3 · yesterday 50.0. Seasonal naive gets the weekly pattern for free. Any model must beat 32.6.

5Turning a series into features

  • Lags: the value 1, 7, 14, 28 days earlier.
  • Rolling statistics: mean / max / sd of the previous 7 or 28 days. Shift before rolling so the window excludes the day being predicted.
  • Calendar: weekday (one-hot), month, holidays, and $\sin$/$\cos$ of the day of year (so 31 Dec and 1 Jan are close).
  • Known future inputs: planned promotions, the weather forecast.
MASE: a scaled error
"MAE 26" means nothing alone. MASE = your MAE ÷ the seasonal-naive MAE. Below 1 beats the baseline; 1.3 = 30% worse.

6Backtesting

One test period can be lucky. Repeat "train up to a date, forecast the next block" moving forward (TimeSeriesSplit).

Example
Five consecutive 8-week windows: error from 19.8 to 28.6. Report the average (25.0) and the spread.

Leave a gap if your features need data that won't be available in time, and re-run the backtest whenever features change.

7Several steps ahead

  • Recursive: predict tomorrow, feed it back as a lag, continue. One model; errors can compound.
  • Direct: a separate model per horizon. More models, no compounding.
Example
Recursive: error 28.1 for days 1–7, 24.8 for days 8–14 (didn't grow here: the series is smooth and strongly seasonal).

With shocks, recursive errors usually grow, so always report accuracy by horizon.

8The wider toolkit

  • Seasonal naive, moving averages: baselines, often strong.
  • Exponential smoothing (Holt–Winters, ETS): one series with trend and season; fast, robust.
  • ARIMA / SARIMA: classical statistics with prediction intervals.
  • Gradient boosting on lag features: many related series (thousands of products) plus prices and promotions.
  • Deep learning: very many long series; needs lots of data to win.
Trees can't extrapolate: a tree never predicts above values it has seen. On a rising trend, model the changes or detrend first.

★Going deeper: choosing lags and honest prediction intervals

Autocorrelation: which lags matter?

Shop sales on a Monday look a lot like last Monday's, and less like yesterday's Sunday. Autocorrelation measures this: how much the series resembles itself shifted back by 1 day, 2 days, 7 days, and so on. The peaks tell you which "days ago" to give the model.

Reading the numbers
Lags 7, 14, 21: about 0.6, strongly similar. A weekly cycle.
Lags 1–6: small. Yesterday tells you little once the weekday is known.
Lag 2: −0.32, a typical side effect of differencing, not a real pattern.
So give the model "value 7, 14 and 21 days ago".

Prediction intervals from held-out errors

A weather forecast of "25°C" is less useful than "22 to 28°C". To get a range, look at how wrong your recent forecasts were and assume future misses will be similar.

Building a 90% interval
Calibration errors: 5th percentile −40, 95th percentile +50.
Forecast 1,000 → interval 960 to 1,050.
Later, count how often the truth landed inside: ideally about 90%.
In one breath
Pick lags where the autocorrelation of the changes peaks (here multiples of 7), and build prediction intervals from recent held-out errors, then check their real coverage, especially when the series is trending.
✎ in one line: beat seasonal naive, build features only from the past, backtest in time order, and check your interval coverage.
