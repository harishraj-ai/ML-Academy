Probability & statistics

1Why?

Real data is messy: two same-size houses sell for different prices. Models live with this uncertainty, so they speak probability: a spam filter says "97% likely spam", not just "spam". Statistics gives the summaries (averages, spread) used in nearly every lesson.

2Probability basics

A number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). With equally likely outcomes: $P(A)=\frac{\text{outcomes where }A}{\text{all outcomes}}$.

Example: a die
$P(6)=1/6\approx0.167$; $P(\text{even})=3/6=0.5$. Over 600 rolls expect about 100 sixes. At least one 6 in two tries: $1-(5/6)^2\approx$ 0.31; in six tries about 0.67, not 1.
  • "Not A": $1-P(A)$.
  • Independent events multiply: 3 heads in a row $=0.5^3=1/8$.

3Conditional probability

$P(A\mid B)$ = chance of A among the cases where B happened:

$P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\text{ and }B)}{P(B)}$

Example
100 emails, 30 spam. 20 contain "free", 15 of those are spam. $P(\text{spam})=0.30$, but $P(\text{spam}\mid\text{"free"})=15/20=$ 0.75. The word is strong evidence (×2.5). This is the idea behind Naive Bayes (Lesson 12).
The big trap
$P(A\mid B)$ is not $P(B\mid A)$.

4Bayes' theorem

$P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}$

  • $P(A)$ = prior: belief before the evidence.
  • $P(B\mid A)$ = likelihood: how probable the evidence is if A is true.
  • $P(A\mid B)$ = posterior: updated belief.
The surprising medical test
Disease in 1% of people; test catches 90%, false-positive rate 5%. $P(+)=0.90\times0.01+0.05\times0.99=0.0585$, so $P(\text{sick}\mid+)=0.009/0.0585\approx$ 15%, not 90%!

5The 1,000-people demo

The disease is rare, so false alarms from the huge healthy group swamp the true cases. Always check the base rate.

6Random variables and expected value

A number that depends on chance (a die roll, tomorrow's temperature). Its expected value = the long-run average:

$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_x x\cdot P(X=x)$

Example
A fair die: $21/6=$ 3.5. You can never roll 3.5, but 1,000 rolls pay about 3,500.

7Mean, variance, standard deviation

Two coffee shops
Both average 5 minutes per order. One always takes 4–6 minutes, the other 1 to 15. Same centre, very different experience: you need the spread too.

$\mu=\frac1n\sum x_i,\qquad\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\mu)^2,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}$

Example
Scores 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 → mean 8 → gaps −4, −2, 0, 2, 4 → squared 16, 4, 0, 4, 16 → variance 40/5 = 8 → $\sigma\approx$ 2.83.

Skewed data (incomes)? A few huge values drag the mean; the median is more representative.

8z-scores (standardisation)

$z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$

Score 12 above → $z=(12-8)/2.83\approx1.41$. Turning every feature into z-scores (mean 0, sd 1) is standardisation (StandardScaler), because many algorithms struggle when features have wildly different scales.

9Distributions

  • Bernoulli: one yes/no with probability $p$. A classifier's "spam?" output.
  • Normal (Gaussian): the bell curve, set by its centre $\mu$ and width $\sigma$.

Rule of thumb: 68% / 95% / 99.7% within 1 / 2 / 3 standard deviations.

10Sampling demo

Draw random samples into a histogram: ragged with few samples, settling into the bell curve with many.

11How it connects to ML

  • Classifiers output probabilities ($P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})$ in logistic regression).
  • Naive Bayes is Bayes' theorem on features.
  • Data = true pattern + noise (often assumed normal), which is why squared error is so natural.
  • Evaluation reports means and standard deviations of scores; preprocessing standardises features.

★Going deeper: statistical inference

More distributions worth knowing

  • Binomial($n,p$): number of yeses in $n$ tries (clicks out of 10 visitors). P(8 or more of 10 at $p=0.5$) = 0.0547.
  • Poisson($\lambda$): counts in a fixed time (calls per hour). At 3/hour, an hour with none: $e^{-3}\approx0.0498$.
  • Exponential: waiting time until the next event. Average wait 20 min → over 30 min: $e^{-1.5}\approx0.2231$. Used in survival models.
  • Normal: sums of many small effects → squared-error loss.

Maximum likelihood (MLE)

Pick the parameters that make the observed data least surprising. 7 heads in 10 flips: $\log L(p)=7\log p+3\log(1-p)$; set the derivative to 0 → $p=0.7$.

The same recipe gives least squares (normal noise) and log loss (yes/no outcomes).

Why variance divides by n − 1
The MLE of the variance divides by $n$ and is biased low: with $n=5$ and true variance 4 it averages 3.21 (theory: 3.2). Dividing by $n-1$ fixes it (4.012).

Bayesian estimation and MAP

Combine a prior belief with the data: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. For a probability, a Beta($a,b$) prior = "$a$ imaginary heads, $b$ imaginary tails".

Example from the lesson
Prior Beta(2, 2) + 7 heads, 3 tails → posterior Beta(9, 5). Mean 9/14 ≈ 0.643 (pulled towards 0.5), MAP peak ≈ 0.667, 95% credible interval 0.386 to 0.861. Honest: 10 flips don't tell you much.

MAP = regularization in disguise: a normal prior on weights gives ridge, a Laplace prior gives lasso. Believing "weights are probably small" is the same as penalising big weights.

Central limit theorem and standard error

Average $n$ independent values of anything (mean $\mu$, sd $\sigma$): for large $n$ the average is roughly normal, with

$\text{SE}=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$

Example from the lesson
A very skewed variable: skewness 1.98 for single values, 0.37 for averages of 30, 0.15 for averages of 200. To halve an error bar you need 4× the data.

Confidence intervals and the bootstrap

For a mean: $\bar{x}\pm1.96\,\text{SE}$. For anything without a formula (median, correlation, F1 score): the bootstrap. Resample your data with replacement thousands of times, recompute the statistic each time, and take the middle 95%.

Example from the lesson
60 house prices: median 72.6 lakh, 95% bootstrap interval 58.0 to 78.7. Not symmetric, because prices are skewed; the bootstrap captures that automatically. (Bagging in Lesson 14 uses the same resampling.)

Hypothesis tests and A/B testing

2,400 visitors see old page A, 2,400 see new page B. B converts 6.5%, A 5.0%. Real or luck?

  1. Null hypothesis $H_0$: the pages convert equally.
  2. Test statistic: the difference in standard-error units, $z=\frac{\hat p_B-\hat p_A}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)(1/n_A+1/n_B)}}$.
  3. p-value: chance of a difference at least this big if $H_0$ were true. Below α (usually 0.05) → reject $H_0$.
Result
$z=2.23$, $p=0.0256$ → at α = 0.05, B is better.
  • Type I error (false positive): a "difference" that isn't real; its rate is α.
  • Type II error (false negative): missing a real difference. Power = chance of catching a real effect; it grows with sample size.
Plan first
Fix the number of visitors before starting, and don't stop early the moment p dips below 0.05.

Multiple testing

Test 20 features with no real effect at α = 0.05, and the chance that at least one looks "significant" is $1-0.95^{20}\approx$ 0.64. Trying many things and reporting only the winner (p-hacking) is why many findings fail to replicate.

  • Bonferroni: use α / m for m tests (overall false-alarm rate back under 5%).
  • Benjamini–Hochberg: a gentler option that controls the false discovery rate.

In ML the same trap is overfitting the validation set by trying many models, which is why an untouched test set matters.

✎ in one line: probabilities measure uncertainty, Bayes updates beliefs, and averages get predictably more precise with more data.
