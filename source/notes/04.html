Probability & statistics

1Why?

Real data is messy: two same-size houses sell for different prices. Models live with this uncertainty, so they speak probability: a spam filter says "97% likely spam", not just "spam". Statistics gives the summaries (averages, spread) used in nearly every lesson.

2Probability basics

A number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). With equally likely outcomes: $P(A)=\frac{\text{outcomes where }A}{\text{all outcomes}}$.

Example: a die
$P(6)=1/6\approx0.167$; $P(\text{even})=3/6=0.5$. Over 600 rolls expect about 100 sixes. At least one 6 in two tries: $1-(5/6)^2\approx$ 0.31; in six tries about 0.67, not 1.
  • "Not A": $1-P(A)$.
  • Independent events multiply: 3 heads in a row $=0.5^3=1/8$.

3Conditional probability

$P(A\mid B)$ = chance of A among the cases where B happened:

$P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\text{ and }B)}{P(B)}$

Example
100 emails, 30 spam. 20 contain "free", 15 of those are spam. $P(\text{spam})=0.30$, but $P(\text{spam}\mid\text{"free"})=15/20=$ 0.75. The word is strong evidence (×2.5). This is the idea behind Naive Bayes (Lesson 12).
The big trap
$P(A\mid B)$ is not $P(B\mid A)$.

4Bayes' theorem

$P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}$

  • $P(A)$ = prior: belief before the evidence.
  • $P(B\mid A)$ = likelihood: how probable the evidence is if A is true.
  • $P(A\mid B)$ = posterior: updated belief.
The surprising medical test
Disease in 1% of people; test catches 90%, false-positive rate 5%. $P(+)=0.90\times0.01+0.05\times0.99=0.0585$, so $P(\text{sick}\mid+)=0.009/0.0585\approx$ 15%, not 90%!

5The 1,000-people demo

The disease is rare, so false alarms from the huge healthy group swamp the true cases. Always check the base rate.

6Random variables and expected value

A number that depends on chance (a die roll, tomorrow's temperature). Its expected value = the long-run average:

$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_x x\cdot P(X=x)$

Example
A fair die: $21/6=$ 3.5. You can never roll 3.5, but 1,000 rolls pay about 3,500.

7Mean, variance, standard deviation

Two coffee shops
Both average 5 minutes per order. One always takes 4–6 minutes, the other 1 to 15. Same centre, very different experience: you need the spread too.

$\mu=\frac1n\sum x_i,\qquad\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\mu)^2,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}$

Example
Scores 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 → mean 8 → gaps −4, −2, 0, 2, 4 → squared 16, 4, 0, 4, 16 → variance 40/5 = 8 → $\sigma\approx$ 2.83.

Skewed data (incomes)? A few huge values drag the mean; the median is more representative.

8z-scores (standardisation)

$z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$

Score 12 above → $z=(12-8)/2.83\approx1.41$. Turning every feature into z-scores (mean 0, sd 1) is standardisation (StandardScaler), because many algorithms struggle when features have wildly different scales.

9Distributions

  • Bernoulli: one yes/no with probability $p$. A classifier's "spam?" output.
  • Normal (Gaussian): the bell curve, set by its centre $\mu$ and width $\sigma$.

Rule of thumb: 68% / 95% / 99.7% within 1 / 2 / 3 standard deviations.

10Sampling demo

Draw random samples into a histogram: ragged with few samples, settling into the bell curve with many.

11How it connects to ML

  • Classifiers output probabilities ($P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})$ in logistic regression).
  • Naive Bayes is Bayes' theorem on features.
  • Data = true pattern + noise (often assumed normal), which is why squared error is so natural.
  • Evaluation reports means and standard deviations of scores; preprocessing standardises features.

★Going deeper: statistical inference for ML

A few more distributions worth knowing

Different kinds of randomness have different shapes: one yes/no (a single click), counting yeses (clicks out of 10), counting events per hour (calls), waiting times (minutes until the next call), and sums of many small effects (the bell curve).

Maximum likelihood: the parameters that make the data least surprising

Try every possible value of the coin's heads chance and ask, for each: "how unsurprised would I be by 7 heads in 10?" The value that makes the data least surprising wins.

Compare three guesses for 7 heads, 3 tails
$p=0.5$: likelihood $0.5^7\times0.5^3\approx0.00098$.
$p=0.7$: $0.7^7\times0.3^3\approx0.00222$ (the largest).
$p=0.9$: $0.9^7\times0.1^3\approx0.00048$.
So the best guess is $p=0.7$, the observed fraction.

Bayesian estimation, priors and MAP

Before tossing a coin you already believe it's probably fair. Ten tosses shouldn't erase that completely; a thousand should. Bayes is the exact rule for that blend.

Updating a Beta(2, 2) prior
Prior: like having seen 2 heads and 2 tails already.
Data: 7 heads, 3 tails. Just add: Beta($2+7$, $2+3$) = Beta(9, 5).
Mean: $9/14\approx0.643$, pulled from 0.7 towards 0.5.
MAP (the peak): $(9-1)/(9+5-2)=8/12\approx0.667$.

The central limit theorem and the standard error

One person's height can be anything; the average of 100 people's heights barely changes between groups. Averaging cancels out the extremes, and the result always looks like a bell curve.

The square-root rule
Spread of one value: $\sigma=1$.
Average of 4: $1/\sqrt4=0.5$.
Average of 16: $1/\sqrt{16}=0.25$.
So halving the error bar needs 4× the data.

Confidence intervals and the bootstrap

You only have one bag of 60 prices. Pretend it's the whole world: draw 60 prices from it at random (repeats allowed), compute the median, put them back, and do it again thousands of times. How much those medians vary is your error bar.

Hypothesis tests and A/B testing

A court case: assume innocence (no difference between A and B) and only convict if the evidence would be very surprising under that assumption.

The checkout test by hand
Rates: A 5.0%, B 6.5%. Difference 1.5 points.
Pooled rate: $(120+156)/4800=5.75\%$.
Standard error: $\sqrt{0.0575\times0.9425\times\tfrac{2}{2400}}$
$\approx0.67$ points.
$z=1.5/0.67\approx2.23$ standard errors → $p\approx0.026$, below 0.05.

Multiple testing: why "significant" findings often vanish

Buy enough lottery tickets and one will win. Test enough useless features and one will look "significant" by luck.

20 useless features
Each test: 95% chance of no false alarm.
All 20 clean: $0.95^{20}\approx0.36$.
At least one false alarm: $1-0.36=0.64$.
Bonferroni: test each at $0.05/20=0.0025$ → overall about 0.049.
In one breath
Pick the distribution that matches your data, fit it by maximum likelihood, blend in prior beliefs with Bayes (that is regularization), expect averages to wobble by $\sigma/\sqrt n$, use the bootstrap for error bars on anything, plan A/B tests in advance, and correct for testing many things.
✎ in one line: probabilities measure uncertainty, Bayes updates beliefs, and averages get predictably more precise with more data.
