Derivatives & gradients

1Why calculus?

In Lesson 1 you moved sliders and watched the loss. A computer can't watch; it needs a precise answer to: "if I nudge this parameter, does the loss go up or down, and how fast?" That's exactly what a derivative tells it, for every parameter.

2Slope of a straight line

slope $=\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$

For $y=3x+1$: $x$ from 1 to 2 moves $y$ from 4 to 7 → slope 3. A straight line has the same slope everywhere.

3The derivative: slope at a point

The speedometer
60 km/h isn't your average for the trip, it's how fast your position changes right now. A derivative is the speedometer reading of a function.

A curve is steeper in some places than others. Its slope at one point = the slope of the tangent line. Take two very close points and shrink the gap $h$ to zero:

$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$

Example: $x^2$ from scratch
$(x+h)^2=x^2+2xh+h^2$, so the ratio is $2x+h$ → as $h\to0$: $f'(x)=$ $2x$.

4The demo

On $f(x)=(x-1)^2+0.5$ the slope $2(x-1)$ is negative on the left, positive on the right, and zero exactly at the bottom.

5The rules you need

  • Constant: $c\to0$. Power: $x^n\to nx^{n-1}$ ($x^3\to3x^2$).
  • Constant multiple: $5x^2\to10x$. Sum: differentiate term by term.
  • $e^x\to e^x$, $\ln x\to\frac1x$.
Example
$4x^3-2x^2+7x-9$ → $12x^2-4x+7$. Slope 7 at $x=0$, 15 at $x=1$, 1,167 at $x=10$: for big inputs the highest power dominates.

6The chain rule (engine of deep learning)

A function inside a function: outer slope (at the inside) × inner slope.

$\dfrac{d}{dx}f(g(x))=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$

Example
$(3x+1)^2$ → $2(3x+1)\cdot3=$ $6(3x+1)$. At $x=0$: 6. Numerical check: $((1.003)^2-1)/0.001\approx6.009$ ✓
Don't forget the inside!
$2(3x+1)$ alone is wrong. A network is a chain of steps, and backpropagation is this rule applied step after step.

7Using it on a loss

Model $\hat{y}=wx$, one point $(x,y)=(2,6)$, loss $L(w)=(2w-6)^2$ → $\frac{dL}{dw}=4(2w-6)$.

  • $w=1$: slope −16 (negative) → increase $w$.
  • $w=5$: slope +16 → decrease $w$.
  • $w=3$: slope 0 → the minimum ($3\cdot2=6$, a perfect fit).

Move against the derivative and the loss always goes down. That's gradient descent.

8Partial derivatives

Many parameters: nudge one, hold the rest fixed (as constants, not zeros). Written with a curly $\partial$.

Example
$f=w^2+3wb+b^2$ → $\frac{\partial f}{\partial w}=2w+3b$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial b}=3w+2b$. At $(1,2)$: 8 and 7. Turn $w$ up by 0.1 → loss rises by about $8\times0.1=0.8$.

9The gradient

All the partial derivatives in one vector: $\nabla L$. It points in the direction where the loss increases fastest, so $-\nabla L$ points downhill. Above: $\nabla f(1,2)=(8,7)$.

Landscape picture
Position = the parameters, height = the loss. The gradient is an arrow pointing straight uphill. Training = walking downhill to a valley.

10Gradient descent in one line

$\mathbf{w}\leftarrow\mathbf{w}-\eta\,\nabla L(\mathbf{w})$

Step a little ($\eta$ = learning rate) downhill; repeat until the loss stops falling. Lesson 7 covers it fully.

★Going deeper: calculus for optimisation

The Jacobian

A gradient is for "many numbers in → one number out". A network layer is "many in → many out"; its derivative is the Jacobian, a matrix of $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$ (one row per output).

Example
$\mathbf{f}=(x^2y,\;5x+\sin y)$ → $J=\begin{pmatrix}2xy&x^2\\5&\cos y\end{pmatrix}$, so $J(1,2)=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\5&-0.4161\end{pmatrix}$.

The chain rule for vector functions = multiplying Jacobians. Backpropagation does this layer by layer (only as products with vectors, never building the full matrices).

The Hessian: curvature

Second derivatives form the Hessian $H_{ij}=\partial^2L/\partial w_i\partial w_j$. It's symmetric; its eigenvalues are the curvature in each direction. Where the gradient is zero:

  • all eigenvalues positive → minimum;
  • all negative → maximum;
  • mixed → saddle (a mountain pass).
Example
$w_1^2+10w_2^2$: eigenvalues 2 and 20, a minimum in a valley 10× steeper one way. $w_1^2-w_2^2$: −2 and 2, a saddle.

In neural networks most zero-gradient points are saddles, not bad minima, one reason gradient descent works better than you'd expect.

Convexity: exactly one valley

A convex function is bowl-shaped: the straight line between any two points on the graph never dips below it. Test: the Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere. Payoff: every local minimum is the global minimum, so gradient descent can't get stuck.

  • Convex: MSE for linear regression (Hessian $\frac2nX^\top X$), log loss (Lesson 8), hinge loss (Lesson 15).
  • Not convex: neural networks, so the starting point matters.

Newton's method

Near any point, a smooth function looks like a bowl (2nd-order Taylor): $L(\mathbf{w}+\Delta)\approx L+\nabla L^\top\Delta+\tfrac12\Delta^\top H\Delta$. Gradient descent uses only the slope; Newton jumps to the bottom of the bowl: $\Delta=-H^{-1}\nabla L$.

Example from the lesson
Valley $w_1^2+10w_2^2$ from (10, 1): gradient descent must keep $\eta<2/20$ or it blows up in the steep direction, so it needs 82 steps at best. Newton lands on the minimum in 1 step.

But the Hessian has $d^2$ entries and costs about $d^3$ to use: impossible for a million weights. So practical methods approximate curvature: L-BFGS (scikit-learn's default for logistic regression) and Adam.

Checking your gradients

Compare a hand-derived gradient with the central difference $\frac{L(\mathbf{w}+h\mathbf{e}_i)-L(\mathbf{w}-h\mathbf{e}_i)}{2h}$ (error shrinks like $h^2$). Use the relative error $\frac{\|g_1-g_2\|}{\|g_1\|+\|g_2\|}$: below $10^{-7}$ is right, around $10^{-4}$ suspicious, above $10^{-2}$ a bug.

Optimising with a rule: Lagrange multipliers

To minimise a function subject to a constraint (e.g. weights must have length 1), add the constraint times a multiplier $\lambda$ and set all derivatives to zero. At the best point the two gradients are parallel. SVMs (Lesson 15) and PCA (Lesson 17) are solved this way.

✎ in one line: the derivative is the slope, the chain rule links the steps, and learning walks against the gradient.
