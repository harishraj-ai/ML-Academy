New words for this section
  • Hazard: the churn rate right now among customers still here.
  • Hazard ratio: one group's hazard divided by the other's.
  • Proportional hazards: the ratio stays the same at all times.
  • Horizon: the time limit for a summary, e.g. the first 12 months.

When hazards aren't proportional

Picture it

A welcome offer helps new customers stay in their first months and then stops mattering. Averaging "very helpful early" with "no help later" into one number gives an answer that's true for no month at all.

A Cox model reports one hazard ratio for the whole period. A simple check: compute the ratio separately for early and late periods (churn events per month at risk in each group):

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5); n = 4000
g = rng.integers(0, 2, n)
# Group 1's benefit fades: hazard ratio 0.4 in the first 6 months, 1.0 afterwards.
t_early = rng.exponential(1 / (0.15 * np.where(g == 1, 0.4, 1.0)))
t = np.where(t_early < 6, t_early, 6 + rng.exponential(1 / 0.15, n))
e = (t < 24).astype(int); t = np.minimum(t, 24)
def hazard(grp, lo, hi):                       # events per month at risk, between months lo and hi
    m = g == grp
    events = ((e[m] == 1) & (t[m] >= lo) & (t[m] < hi)).sum()
    exposure = np.clip(np.minimum(t[m], hi) - lo, 0, None).sum()
    return events / exposure
for lo, hi in [(0, 6), (6, 24)]:
    print(f"months {lo}–{hi}: hazard ratio, group 1 vs group 0, {hazard(1, lo, hi) / hazard(0, lo, hi):.2f}")

Output:

months 0–6: hazard ratio, group 1 vs group 0, 0.39
months 6–24: hazard ratio, group 1 vs group 0, 1.04
Worked example — reading the two periods
  1. Months 0–6: 0.39. Group 1 churns at well under half group 0's rate.
  2. Months 6–24: 1.04. No advantage at all.
  3. One Cox number would land somewhere around 0.6, true of neither period.

Fixes: separate coefficients for each period, stratify by the variable, or report the curves directly. A classic visual check: plot $\log(-\log \hat S(t))$ for each group; under proportional hazards the lines are parallel.

Restricted mean survival time

Picture it

The area under a survival curve up to 12 months is simply the average number of months a customer stays during that first year. It's in plain units that anyone can use.

Worked example — area by hand
  1. Survival 1.0 for months 0–3, 0.8 for 3–6, 0.6 for 6–12.
  2. Area $=3\times1.0+3\times0.8+6\times0.6=3+2.4+3.6=9.0$.
  3. Customers stay 9.0 of the first 12 months on average.
def km_curve(tt, ee):
    times, s, at, out = np.sort(np.unique(tt[ee == 1])), 1.0, None, []
    for u in times:
        s *= 1 - ((tt == u) & (ee == 1)).sum() / (tt >= u).sum(); out.append((u, s))
    return out
def rmst(curve, horizon):
    area, prev_t, prev_s = 0.0, 0.0, 1.0
    for u, s in curve:
        if u > horizon: break
        area += prev_s * (u - prev_t); prev_t, prev_s = u, s
    return area + prev_s * (horizon - prev_t)
for grp in [0, 1]:
    print(f"group {grp}: restricted mean survival over 12 months {rmst(km_curve(t[g == grp], e[g == grp]), 12):.2f} months")

Output:

group 0: restricted mean survival over 12 months 5.45 months
group 1: restricted mean survival over 12 months 7.71 months

How to read it: over the first 12 months, group 1 stays 7.71 months on average and group 0 5.45: 2.3 more months per customer, which converts directly into revenue. RMST needs no proportional-hazards assumption, and "2.3 more months in the first year" is far easier for decision-makers than a hazard ratio.

In one breath

Check whether the hazard ratio changes over time before trusting a single Cox number; report the restricted mean survival time ("2.3 more months in the first year") for a clear summary that needs no such assumption.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

How do you check the proportional hazards assumption, and what do you do if it fails?

The trap: "It always holds for Cox models."

Plot log(−log S(t)) by group (parallel lines under PH), test Schoenfeld residuals against time, or compare hazard ratios across time periods. If it fails: stratify by the variable, add time-varying coefficients (an interaction with time or period), or report effects that don't need the assumption, such as RMST differences or survival at fixed horizons.
