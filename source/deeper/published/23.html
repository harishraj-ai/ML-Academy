13.Going deeper: Bayesian optimisation from scratch

In plain wordsThe idea behind smart tuning tools, built from scratch: a Gaussian process guesses the score of untried settings with an uncertainty band, and expected improvement picks the next setting by balancing "near the best so far" against "where we know little".

New words for this section
  • Surrogate: a cheap model that predicts the expensive validation score.
  • Gaussian process (GP): a model that gives a best guess and an uncertainty for every setting.
  • Exploitation: trying near the current best.
  • Exploration: trying where you know little.
  • Expected improvement (EI): how much a try is likely to beat the best so far, on average.

The surrogate: a Gaussian process

Picture it

You've measured the temperature at a few spots in a field and want to guess it everywhere else. Near your measurements you can be confident; far away you can only guess roughly. A Gaussian process draws a smooth best-guess curve with a band of uncertainty that's thin near measurements and wide far from them.

A Gaussian process is a probability distribution over smooth curves. From a few tried settings it predicts a mean $\mu(x)$ and a spread $\sigma(x)$ for every other setting. How smooth it assumes the curve to be is set by a kernel, the same idea as SVM kernels (Lesson 15). Here we use the common Matérn kernel, and the GP learns its length scale from the data.

Choosing the next trial: expected improvement

Picture it

Drilling for oil: drill next to your best well (likely good, small upside) or somewhere unexplored (could be terrible, could be a gusher). Expected improvement scores both kinds of spot on one scale: how much better than your best well you can expect to do.

Let $f^*$ be the best score so far. At a candidate setting the GP says the score is roughly normal with mean $\mu$ and spread $\sigma$. The expected improvement (no improvement counts as 0) is

$$\text{EI}(x) = (\mu - f^*)\,\Phi(z) + \sigma\,\phi(z), \qquad z = \frac{\mu - f^*}{\sigma}$$

$\Phi$ and $\phi$ are the standard normal CDF and density. The first term rewards a high predicted score (exploitation); the second rewards uncertainty (exploration).

Worked example — a safe spot vs an uncertain spot (best so far 0.78)
  1. Safe: $\mu=0.80$, $\sigma=0.05$. $z=0.4$, EI $\approx0.02\times0.66+0.05\times0.37\approx0.032$.
  2. Uncertain: $\mu=0.70$ (worse on average!), $\sigma=0.15$. $z\approx-0.53$, EI $\approx-0.08\times0.30+0.15\times0.35\approx0.028$.
  3. Nearly as attractive: big uncertainty means a real chance of a big win.

Each round: fit the GP to all results so far, try the setting with the highest EI, repeat. Below, the "validation score" is a made-up curve over $\log_{10}(\text{learning rate})$ with a narrow peak; each method starts fresh 20 times, and we report the average gap to the true best:

import numpy as np
import warnings
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")          # the GP optimiser warns about its bounds on tiny data
from scipy.stats import norm
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import Matern, ConstantKernel

# A made-up "validation score vs log10(learning rate)" curve with its peak near x = -2.3
f = lambda x: np.exp(-(x + 2.3) ** 2 / 0.3) * 0.3 + 0.65 + 0.02 * np.sin(5 * x)
grid = np.linspace(-5, 0, 501)[:, None]

def bayes_opt(n_iter, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X = rng.uniform(-5, 0, size=(3, 1)); y = f(X[:, 0])               # 3 random starting points
    kernel = ConstantKernel(1.0, (1e-3, 1e3)) * Matern(length_scale=0.5, length_scale_bounds=(0.05, 5), nu=2.5)
    gp = GaussianProcessRegressor(kernel, alpha=1e-6, normalize_y=True, n_restarts_optimizer=3, random_state=0)
    for _ in range(n_iter - 3):
        gp.fit(X, y)
        mu, sd = gp.predict(grid, return_std=True)
        z = (mu - y.max()) / np.maximum(sd, 1e-9)
        ei = (mu - y.max()) * norm.cdf(z) + sd * norm.pdf(z)             # expected improvement
        x_next = grid[np.argmax(ei)]
        X = np.vstack([X, x_next]); y = np.append(y, f(x_next[0]))
    return y.max()

def random_search(n_iter, seed):
    return f(np.random.default_rng(seed).uniform(-5, 0, n_iter)).max()

best = f(grid[:, 0]).max()
for n in [5, 10, 15]:
    bo = np.mean([best - bayes_opt(n, s) for s in range(20)])
    rs = np.mean([best - random_search(n, s) for s in range(20)])
    print(f"{n:2d} trials: average gap to the best score, Bayesian {bo:.4f}   random {rs:.4f}")

Output:

 5 trials: average gap to the best score, Bayesian 0.1297   random 0.1190
10 trials: average gap to the best score, Bayesian 0.0103   random 0.0510
15 trials: average gap to the best score, Bayesian 0.0000   random 0.0261

How to read it: at 5 trials there's no advantage (3 were random starting points). By 10 trials Bayesian optimisation is five times closer (0.0103 vs 0.0510); by 15 it finds the peak every time while random search is still 0.026 away. It helps most when each trial is expensive and you can afford only tens of them.

In practice

  • Dimensions. GPs work best with up to about 10–20 settings. Optuna's default, TPE, copes better with many settings and with categorical or conditional ones ("number of layers", then a width per layer).
  • Noise. Validation scores are noisy; let the surrogate model noise, or it will chase lucky trials.
  • Parallelism. Random search runs in parallel trivially; Bayesian optimisation is naturally one-at-a-time.
  • Pruning. Combining a smart sampler with early stopping of bad trials (Optuna pruners, Hyperband) usually gives the biggest savings.
In one breath

Bayesian optimisation fits a cheap surrogate (a Gaussian process) that gives a best guess and an uncertainty for every setting, then tries the setting with the highest expected improvement, balancing exploring and exploiting; it shines when trials are few and expensive.

Explain expected improvement and the exploration–exploitation trade-off it encodes.

The trap: Describing it only as "pick the best predicted point".

Given the surrogate's normal prediction $(\mu, \sigma)$ and the best score $f^*$, EI is the expected amount by which a trial beats $f^*$: $(\mu - f^*)\Phi(z) + \sigma\phi(z)$ with $z = (\mu - f^*)/\sigma$. The first term favours points predicted to be good (exploitation); the second favours uncertain points (exploration). Points with a low mean but high uncertainty can still be chosen, which prevents getting stuck near an early lucky result.

When would you choose Optuna's TPE over a Gaussian-process optimiser?

The trap: "TPE is always better."

When there are many hyperparameters, categorical or conditional ones (a choice of model with its own settings), or many trials: TPE scales to these, while GP cost grows cubically with the number of trials and GPs struggle beyond about 20 dimensions. A GP can be more sample-efficient for a few continuous hyperparameters and very expensive trials.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. What does a Gaussian process surrogate predict at an untried setting?

The uncertainty is what lets Bayesian optimisation explore sensibly.

2. In expected improvement, the term $\sigma\,\phi(z)$ rewards…

It grows with the uncertainty $\sigma$, so unexplored regions get tried.

3. When does Bayesian optimisation help most compared with random search?

Each trial's information matters most when trials are scarce.

4. Why is basic Bayesian optimisation hard to run in parallel?

Random search's trials are independent; Bayesian optimisation is sequential by design.

5. If the best so far is $f^* = 0.90$ and a candidate has $\mu = 0.90$, $\sigma = 0.02$, its expected improvement is about…

With $z = 0$: EI $= 0 + 0.02 \times \phi(0) = 0.02 \times 0.399 \approx 0.008$.
