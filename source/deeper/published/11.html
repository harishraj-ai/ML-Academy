The curse of dimensionality, measured

Picture it

In a small room, some people are close to you and some far away, so "nearest" is meaningful. In a space with hundreds of directions, every person differs from you a little in every direction, and those small differences add up to almost the same total for everyone. Everyone becomes roughly equally far away.

Here are 1,000 random points in a cube and one query point, measured in more and more dimensions:

import numpy as np

# Distance concentration: in high dimensions the nearest and farthest points are almost equally far
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for d in [2, 10, 100, 1000]:
    pts = rng.uniform(size=(1000, d)); q = rng.uniform(size=d)
    dist = np.linalg.norm(pts - q, axis=1)
    print(f"{d:4d} dimensions: nearest {dist.min():6.2f}   farthest {dist.max():6.2f}   ratio {dist.max() / dist.min():5.2f}")

Output:

   2 dimensions: nearest   0.01   farthest   1.33   ratio 135.62
  10 dimensions: nearest   0.47   farthest   2.11   ratio  4.48
 100 dimensions: nearest   3.35   farthest   4.65   ratio  1.39
1000 dimensions: nearest  12.17   farthest  13.55   ratio  1.11
Worked example — reading the ratio
  1. 2 dimensions: farthest/nearest $=1.33/0.01\approx136$. "Nearest" is very special.
  2. 100 dimensions: $4.65/3.35\approx1.39$.
  3. 1,000 dimensions: $13.55/12.17\approx1.11$. The nearest point is only 11% closer than the farthest, so it tells you little.

Why? Each dimension adds a small, independent random difference, and sums of many random differences all land close to their average: the central limit theorem again (Lesson 4's Going deeper).

Real data is rescued because it rarely fills all its dimensions. Photos, text embeddings and sensor readings usually lie near a much simpler surface (the manifold hypothesis), so their true dimension is small. That's why reducing dimensions first (PCA, Lesson 17) or using learned embeddings often makes kNN work well again.

Choosing the distance and the weighting

Picture it

Three ways to measure how far apart two places are: as the crow flies (Euclidean), along a city's grid of streets (Manhattan), or simply whether they lie in the same direction from home (cosine).

  • Euclidean (L2): the default for standardised measurements.
  • Manhattan (L1): less dominated by one big difference; sometimes better with many features or outliers.
  • Cosine distance, $1-\cos\theta$: compares directions and ignores length. Standard for text and embeddings, where a document twice as long shouldn't count as "farther".
Worked example — cosine ignores length
  1. Document A has word counts (1, 2); document B, twice as long, has (2, 4).
  2. Euclidean distance: $\sqrt{1^2+2^2}\approx2.24$, "far apart".
  3. Same direction, so $\cos\theta=1$ and cosine distance $=0$: "identical topic".

Distance weighting gives closer neighbours a bigger vote (weight $1/d$) instead of an equal vote for each of the $k$. It makes the result less sensitive to the exact $k$. On the standardised breast-cancer data with $k=15$:

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
for metric in ["euclidean", "manhattan", "cosine"]:
    for weights in ["uniform", "distance"]:
        m = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=15, metric=metric, weights=weights))
        print(f"{metric:9} {weights:8}: accuracy {cross_val_score(m, X, y, cv=10).mean():.3f}")

Output:

euclidean uniform : accuracy 0.961
euclidean distance: accuracy 0.963
manhattan uniform : accuracy 0.954
manhattan distance: accuracy 0.956
cosine    uniform : accuracy 0.963
cosine    distance: accuracy 0.963

How to read it: all six combinations score between 0.954 and 0.963, within cross-validation noise (Lesson 9's Going deeper). Once features are standardised the metric often hardly matters. What matters most for kNN, in order: scale the features, choose $k$ by cross-validation, use meaningful features. Tune the metric mainly for text, images or mixed data.

Finding neighbours fast

Picture it

Finding the closest café: checking every café in the country is slow. A map split into regions lets you check only your area (a tree). Asking a well-connected local who knows nearby places, and hopping from friend to friend, is even faster and almost always right (approximate search).

Brute-force kNN compares a query with all $n$ stored points: $O(nd)$ work, too slow for millions of points answered in milliseconds. Two families of fixes:

  • Space-partitioning trees (k-d trees, ball trees) split space into nested boxes or balls, and skip whole regions that can't contain anything closer than the best found so far. Exact, and very fast in low dimensions (roughly $O(\log n)$ per query), but the benefit fades above about 20 dimensions.
  • Approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) methods such as HNSW (a graph of near neighbours) or FAISS (clustering and compression) find over 95% of the true neighbours, thousands of times faster. Vector databases are built on them.
Worked example — how much a tree saves
  1. 1,000,000 points. Brute force: 1,000,000 distance checks per query.
  2. A balanced tree halves the candidates at each level: about $\log_2(10^6)\approx20$ levels.
  3. So a few dozen checks instead of a million (in low dimensions).
import time
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors

data = rng.normal(size=(200000, 3)); queries = rng.normal(size=(2000, 3))
found, times = {}, {}
for algo in ["brute", "kd_tree"]:
    nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=5, algorithm=algo).fit(data)
    t = time.perf_counter(); found[algo] = nn.kneighbors(queries)[1]; times[algo] = time.perf_counter() - t
print("same neighbours found by both:", np.array_equal(np.sort(found["brute"]), np.sort(found["kd_tree"])))
print("k-d tree faster:", times["kd_tree"] < times["brute"])   # exact timings vary from computer to computer

Output:

same neighbours found by both: True
k-d tree faster: True

On 200,000 points in 3 dimensions, the k-d tree finds exactly the same neighbours as brute force, much faster. Scikit-learn's algorithm="auto" chooses brute force, a k-d tree or a ball tree based on the data's size and dimension.

How good can kNN get?

Picture it

With unlimited data, the nearest neighbour of any new point is practically the same point, so copying its label is almost as good as knowing the true odds. The only mistakes left come from the problem's own randomness.

A classic result (Cover and Hart, 1967): with enough data, 1-nearest-neighbour's error is at most twice the Bayes error, the error of the best possible classifier. If $k$ grows slowly as data grows ($k\to\infty$ but $k/n\to0$), kNN's error approaches the Bayes error itself, so with enough data kNN can learn any boundary.

Worked example — what "twice" means
  1. A problem where even the perfect model is wrong 5% of the time (Bayes error 5%).
  2. With enough data, 1-NN is wrong at most 10% of the time.
  3. The catch: in many dimensions, "enough data" can be astronomically large.
In one breath

In high dimensions all distances look alike, so reduce dimensions or use good embeddings; scale features and tune $k$ before worrying about the metric (use cosine for text); use trees or approximate search for speed; and with enough data kNN gets close to the best possible.

Interview corner · intermediate

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

How would you serve nearest-neighbour search over 100 million embeddings with low latency?

The trap: Proposing brute force or a k-d tree.

Use an approximate nearest-neighbour index: graph-based (HNSW) or inverted-file with product quantization (IVF-PQ, as in FAISS), often in a vector database. Normalise the embeddings if using cosine similarity. Tune the index (graph degree, number of probed clusters) for the recall/latency trade-off, measure recall against brute force on a sample, and shard across machines when the index doesn't fit in memory.

Why do distances concentrate in high dimensions, and why does kNN still work on images and text?

The trap: Saying kNN never works in high dimensions.

Each dimension adds an independent random difference, so squared distances are sums of many terms, whose relative spread shrinks like $1/\sqrt{d}$. Nearest and farthest become similar. Real data, though, lies near a much lower-dimensional manifold, so its intrinsic dimension is small. Good representations (PCA, learned embeddings) expose that structure, and kNN works in that space.

When would you choose cosine distance over Euclidean?

The trap: "Cosine is always better for text." without saying why.

When the length of a vector reflects something irrelevant, such as document length or overall activity, and only the pattern matters. TF-IDF vectors, word and sentence embeddings, and user-preference vectors are typical. On unit-length vectors, cosine and Euclidean rank neighbours identically, because $\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta$.

What's the theoretical guarantee for 1-nearest-neighbour as data grows?

The trap: Claiming it reaches perfect accuracy.

Cover and Hart (1967): as $n \to \infty$, the 1-NN error is at most twice the Bayes error (the best achievable). With $k \to \infty$ and $k/n \to 0$, kNN's error approaches the Bayes error itself. The data needed to get close grows exponentially with dimension, which is the practical catch.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. In 1,000 dimensions, the farthest of 1,000 random points is only about 1.1 times farther than the nearest. What does that mean for kNN?

When all distances are similar, the nearest neighbours are hardly more similar than random points.

2. Which distance ignores vector length and compares only direction?

Cosine distance is $1 - \cos\theta$, which is why it's standard for text and embeddings.

3. What does weights="distance" do in kNN?

Each neighbour's vote is weighted by $1/d$.

4. When do k-d trees stop helping much?

In high dimensions, distance concentration means the search can rarely skip regions.

They usually find most true neighbours, far faster, which is what vector databases need.
