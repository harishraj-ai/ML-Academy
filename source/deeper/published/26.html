New words for this section
  • Lag $k$: the value $k$ steps ago.
  • Autocorrelation: how similar a series is to itself shifted by $k$ steps (−1 to 1).
  • Differencing: working with day-to-day changes instead of levels.
  • Prediction interval: a range the true value should fall in, say, 90% of the time.
  • Coverage: how often the true values really fell inside the interval.

Autocorrelation: which lags matter?

Picture it

Shop sales on a Monday look a lot like last Monday's, and less like yesterday's Sunday. Autocorrelation measures this: how much the series resembles itself shifted back by 1 day, 2 days, 7 days, and so on. The peaks tell you which "days ago" to give the model.

Compute it on the changes (or after removing the trend): a rising trend makes every lag look similar and hides the real pattern.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
t = np.arange(730)
y = pd.Series((200 + 0.15 * t) * np.array([0.85, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0, 1.05, 1.25, 1.1])[t % 7] * rng.lognormal(0, 0.08, 730))
change = y.diff(1)                                   # remove the trend first
acf = {lag: round(float(change.autocorr(lag)), 2) for lag in [1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 14, 21]}
print("autocorrelation of daily changes by lag:", acf)

Output:

autocorrelation of daily changes by lag: {1: -0.12, 2: -0.32, 3: -0.07, 6: 0.07, 7: 0.61, 14: 0.59, 21: 0.6}
Worked example — reading the numbers
  1. Lags 7, 14, 21: about 0.6, strongly similar. A weekly cycle.
  2. Lags 1–6: small. Yesterday tells you little once the weekday is known.
  3. Lag 2: −0.32, a typical side effect of differencing, not a real pattern.

So give the model "value 7, 14 and 21 days ago".

Plotting autocorrelation up to two seasons (plot_acf in statsmodels) is a standard first look at any new series.

Prediction intervals from held-out errors

Picture it

A weather forecast of "25°C" is less useful than "22 to 28°C". To get a range, look at how wrong your recent forecasts were and assume future misses will be similar.

A simple, model-agnostic method: forecast a held-out calibration period, take the 5th and 95th percentiles of the errors, and add them to future forecasts. This is the idea behind split conformal prediction. Then check the coverage on a later period:

Worked example — building a 90% interval
  1. Calibration errors: 5th percentile −40, 95th percentile +50.
  2. Forecast 1,000 → interval 960 to 1,050.
  3. Later, count how often the truth landed inside: ideally about 90%.
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
X = pd.DataFrame({f"lag_{l}": y.shift(l) for l in [7, 14, 21]}).dropna(); target = y[X.index]
cal, test = X.index[(X.index >= 550) & (X.index < 640)], X.index[X.index >= 640]
model = Ridge(1.0).fit(X[X.index < 550], target[target.index < 550])
resid = target[cal] - model.predict(X.loc[cal])
lo, hi = np.quantile(resid, [0.05, 0.95])            # 90% interval from held-out errors
pred = model.predict(X.loc[test])
inside = ((target[test] >= pred + lo) & (target[test] <= pred + hi)).mean()
print("interval offsets:", round(lo, 1), "to", round(hi, 1), "  coverage on the test period:", round(inside, 3))

Output:

interval offsets: -40.3 to 50.2   coverage on the test period: 0.856

How to read it: built for 90%, the interval covers only 85.6% of the test period. Why? Sales keep growing, so errors grow too, and errors from an earlier period understate later ones. Fixes: model the log of the series (so errors are relative), use only recent errors, or recompute the interval at each backtest step. Always report the coverage you actually get, by horizon.

In one breath

Pick lags where the autocorrelation of the changes peaks (here multiples of 7), and build prediction intervals from recent held-out errors, then check their real coverage, especially when the series is trending.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

How would you give prediction intervals for a gradient boosting forecast?

The trap: "Gradient boosting can't give intervals."

Train quantile models (for example the 5th and 95th percentiles with a quantile loss), or use conformal prediction: compute errors on a held-out calibration period and add their quantiles to the point forecast. Either way, backtest the coverage by horizon, because errors usually grow with the horizon and with the series' level, and refresh the calibration regularly.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. Why compute autocorrelation on the changes rather than the raw series?

Removing the trend lets the seasonal peaks stand out.

2. The autocorrelation of daily changes peaks at 7, 14 and 21. Which lags should a model use?

The peaks show a weekly cycle.

3. A 90% interval built from held-out errors covers 86% of test values. What does that mean?

Coverage below the nominal level means it understates uncertainty.

4. Why might errors from an earlier period understate later errors?

Relative (log) errors or recent errors fix this.

5. What does split conformal prediction need?

Its quantiles of errors give the interval, for any model.
