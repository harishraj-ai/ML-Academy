14.Going deeper: bad controls and regression discontinuity

In plain wordsTwo causal-inference lessons: "controlling" for a step on the path from cause to effect can make a real effect vanish, and a cut-off rule (like a score of 600) hides a near-experiment you can measure with regression discontinuity.

Intermediate · skip on a first read

You'll need: confounding, adjustment and causal diagrams from this lesson. New words are explained as they come.

New words for this section
  • Confounder: something that affects both treatment and outcome; adjust for it.
  • Mediator: a step on the path from treatment to outcome; usually don't adjust.
  • Collider: something caused by both treatment and outcome; never adjust.
  • Cut-off rule: treatment given to everyone above a threshold, e.g. a score of 600.

Don't adjust for what the treatment causes

Picture it

To test whether a painkiller works, you wouldn't "control for" how much pain people still feel: that's the very thing the pill changes. Adjusting for a step on the path from cause to effect hides the effect.

Here a randomised discount brings customers back more often, and all of its effect on spending works through those extra visits (true effect 12):

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
rng = np.random.default_rng(0); n = 20000
discount = rng.integers(0, 2, n)                               # randomised
visits = 2 + 3 * discount + rng.normal(0, 1, n)                 # the discount brings people back (a mediator)
spend = 50 + 4 * visits + rng.normal(0, 5, n)                   # all of the effect goes through visits: 4 × 3 = 12
total = LinearRegression().fit(np.c_[discount], spend).coef_[0]
blocked = LinearRegression().fit(np.c_[discount, visits], spend).coef_[0]
print("effect of the discount, not adjusting:", round(total, 2), "  'adjusting' for visits:", round(blocked, 2))

Output:

effect of the discount, not adjusting: 11.96   'adjusting' for visits: -0.08
Worked example — reading it
  1. Not adjusting: 11.96, matching the true 12.
  2. "Controlling" for visits: −0.08, as if the discount did nothing.
  3. Both use the same data; only the choice of control changed.

A similar trap is a collider, something caused by both the treatment and the outcome (such as "contacted support"); adjusting for it creates fake associations. A causal diagram tells you which variables are confounders (adjust), mediators (don't, unless you want only the direct effect) and colliders (never).

Regression discontinuity

Picture it

Students scoring 599 and 601 on an exam are practically identical, but only those at 600 or above get the scholarship. Comparing students right at the line is almost like flipping a coin to decide who gets it.

Regression discontinuity fits a line on each side, close to the threshold, and measures the jump at the cut-off:

# Regression discontinuity: customers with a score of 600 or more get a credit limit increase.
score = rng.uniform(450, 750, n)
treated = (score >= 600).astype(int)
spend2 = 20 + 0.1 * score + 15 * treated + rng.normal(0, 10, n)   # true jump: 15
naive2 = spend2[treated == 1].mean() - spend2[treated == 0].mean()
band = np.abs(score - 600) < 25                                     # only customers near the threshold
Xb = np.c_[treated[band], score[band] - 600, treated[band] * (score[band] - 600)]
rdd = LinearRegression().fit(Xb, spend2[band]).coef_[0]
print("naive difference:", round(naive2, 1), "  regression discontinuity (±25 points):", round(rdd, 1))

Output:

naive difference: 29.8   regression discontinuity (±25 points): 14.7
Worked example — reading it
  1. Naive difference, everyone above vs below: 29.8. This mixes the effect with "high-score customers spend more anyway".
  2. Only customers within 25 points, with a line on each side: 14.7, close to the true 15.

Key assumptions: people can't precisely push their score over the line, and nothing else changes at exactly 600. Check by plotting the outcome against the score and looking for a pile-up of people just above the cut-off.

In one breath

Adjust for confounders, but not for mediators (they erase the effect you want) or colliders (they create fake ones); and when treatment follows a cut-off, compare people just either side of it with regression discontinuity.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

When is adding a control variable harmful in a causal analysis?

The trap: "More controls are always better."

When it's a mediator (caused by the treatment and affecting the outcome), adjusting removes part of the effect; when it's a collider (caused by both treatment and outcome, or their causes), adjusting creates a spurious association. Adjust for pre-treatment confounders. A causal diagram, drawn from domain knowledge before looking at the results, decides which is which.
