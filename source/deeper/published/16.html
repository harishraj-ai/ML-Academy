15.Going deeper: mixture models, density and hierarchy

In plain wordsClustering beyond k-means: Gaussian mixtures for stretched or overlapping clusters (with "70% this group" answers), DBSCAN and linkage for clusters of any shape, and the silhouette and BIC for choosing how many clusters there are.

New words for this section
  • Gaussian: a bell-shaped cloud of points.
  • Covariance: how a cloud is stretched and tilted.
  • Responsibility: the probability that a cluster produced a given point.
  • ARI: adjusted Rand index: 1 = perfect match with the true groups, 0 = chance.
  • Linkage: the rule for how far apart two groups are.
  • Dendrogram: a tree showing which groups merge, and at what distance.

Gaussian mixture models and the EM algorithm

Picture it

k-means draws circles around groups. A mixture model draws stretched, tilted ovals, and instead of a hard "this point is in group 2" it says "70% group 2, 30% group 3". It learns by repeating two steps: guess who belongs where, then redraw the ovals.

A Gaussian mixture model imagines each point was made by first picking a cluster $k$ (with probability $\pi_k$), then drawing from a bell-shaped cloud with that cluster's centre $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ and shape (covariance) $\Sigma_k$. It's fitted by expectation–maximisation (EM):

  • E-step ("guess"): for every point, compute each cluster's responsibility for it, using Bayes' theorem.
  • M-step ("redraw"): re-estimate each cluster's $\pi_k$, $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ and $\Sigma_k$ as weighted averages, each point weighted by its responsibility.
Worked example — one E-step for one point
  1. Two clusters, equal sizes. The point's density under cluster A is 0.06, under B 0.02.
  2. Responsibility of A: $0.06/(0.06+0.02)=0.75$; B: 0.25.
  3. In the M-step this point counts as 0.75 of a point for A's new centre and 0.25 for B's.

Each round never lowers the likelihood, so EM settles down, though like k-means it can stop at a local optimum, so restart it a few times. k-means is a special case: round clusters of equal tiny spread with hard 0-or-1 responsibilities.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs, make_moons
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN, AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score, silhouette_score

# Stretched clusters: k-means assumes round blobs, a Gaussian mixture doesn't
Xb, yb = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=3, random_state=170)
Xs = Xb @ np.array([[0.6, -0.6], [-0.4, 0.8]])          # stretch and shear the blobs
km = KMeans(3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xs)
gmm = GaussianMixture(3, covariance_type="full", random_state=0).fit(Xs)
print("stretched blobs: ARI k-means", round(adjusted_rand_score(yb, km), 3), "  ARI Gaussian mixture", round(adjusted_rand_score(yb, gmm.predict(Xs)), 3))
p = gmm.predict_proba(Xs)
print("share of points the mixture is unsure about (max probability < 0.9):", round(np.mean(p.max(axis=1) < 0.9), 3))

Output:

stretched blobs: ARI k-means 0.58   ARI Gaussian mixture 1.0
share of points the mixture is unsure about (max probability < 0.9): 0.002

How to read it: on three stretched, tilted blobs, k-means scores ARI 0.58 (its circles cut the long clusters wrongly); the mixture scores 1.0, perfect. Only 0.2% of points are uncertain here (no cluster above 90%); in overlapping data those soft probabilities show which points are truly ambiguous.

Clusters of any shape: DBSCAN and linkage

Picture it

Think of islands on a map. Land connected by crowded ground forms one island, whatever its shape; lone rocks in the sea are noise. DBSCAN finds clusters this way, by following dense regions.

DBSCAN: a point with at least min_samples neighbours within distance eps is a "core" point; chains of connected core points form clusters; anything unreachable is noise.

Agglomerative (hierarchical) clustering starts with every point alone and keeps merging the two closest groups. The linkage rule says what "closest" means: single linkage uses the closest pair of points (it can follow long, thin shapes); Ward merges the pair that least increases spread inside clusters (it prefers compact blobs). All five methods on two interlocking half-moons:

Xm, ym = make_moons(n_samples=500, noise=0.06, random_state=0)
results = {
    "k-means":            KMeans(2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xm),
    "Gaussian mixture":   GaussianMixture(2, random_state=0).fit(Xm).predict(Xm),
    "DBSCAN":             DBSCAN(eps=0.2, min_samples=5).fit_predict(Xm),
    "agglomerative, single linkage": AgglomerativeClustering(2, linkage="single").fit_predict(Xm),
    "agglomerative, Ward linkage":   AgglomerativeClustering(2, linkage="ward").fit_predict(Xm),
}
for name, lab in results.items():
    print(f"{name:30}: ARI {adjusted_rand_score(ym, lab):.3f}")

Output:

k-means                       : ARI 0.253
Gaussian mixture              : ARI 0.495
DBSCAN                        : ARI 1.000
agglomerative, single linkage : ARI 1.000
agglomerative, Ward linkage   : ARI 0.559

How to read it: DBSCAN and single linkage both score 1.000 because they follow connected, dense paths. k-means (0.253), the mixture (0.495) and Ward (0.559) assume compact groups and cut each moon in half. Watch-outs: single linkage can "chain" two real clusters through a thin bridge of noise; DBSCAN depends on eps and struggles when clusters have very different densities. HDBSCAN (in scikit-learn since 1.3) tries many densities at once.

Hierarchical clustering also gives a dendrogram. Cutting it at different heights gives different numbers of clusters, handy when the right level of detail isn't known (customer segments, document topics).

Choosing the number of clusters

Picture it

A good grouping has points close to their own group and far from the next one. More clusters always make groups tighter, so a fair score must also penalise splitting for the sake of it.

  • Silhouette: for each point, $s=(b-a)/\max(a,b)$, where $a$ is its average distance to its own cluster and $b$ to the nearest other cluster. Near 1 = clearly placed; near 0 = on a border; negative = probably in the wrong cluster.
  • BIC for mixtures: $-2\log L + p\log n$, fit plus a penalty for the number of parameters $p$. Lower is better.
Worked example — one point's silhouette
  1. Average distance to its own cluster $a=2$, to the nearest other cluster $b=6$.
  2. $s=(6-2)/6\approx0.67$: clearly in the right place.
  3. If $a=5$, $b=4$: $s=(4-5)/5=-0.2$, probably mis-assigned.
# Choosing k without labels: silhouette score and the mixture's BIC
Xk, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=4, cluster_std=1.0, random_state=7)
print(" k   silhouette   BIC")
for k in range(2, 8):
    lab = KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xk)
    bic = GaussianMixture(k, random_state=0).fit(Xk).bic(Xk)
    print(f"{k:2d}   {silhouette_score(Xk, lab):10.3f}   {bic:7.0f}")

Output:

 k   silhouette   BIC
 2        0.588      6194
 3        0.762      5490
 4        0.811      5133
 5        0.680      5168
 6        0.577      5193
 7        0.446      5227

How to read it: the data has 4 true clusters. The silhouette peaks at $k=4$ (0.811) and BIC is lowest at $k=4$ (5133). On real data the signals are often weaker or disagree; treat them as guides, and also ask whether each cluster is useful: can someone describe it and act on it?

In one breath

Use a Gaussian mixture (fitted by EM) for stretched or overlapping clusters and soft membership, DBSCAN or single linkage for odd shapes and noise, and the silhouette or BIC, together with common sense about usefulness, to choose how many clusters.

Interview corner · intermediate

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

Compare k-means and Gaussian mixture models.

The trap: Saying they're the same thing.

Both alternate assignment and update steps. k-means makes hard assignments to the nearest centroid, assumes round clusters of similar size, and minimises within-cluster squared distance. A GMM makes soft probabilistic assignments, learns each cluster's covariance (stretched, tilted, different sizes) and maximises likelihood with EM. GMMs give uncertainty and a likelihood (so BIC can choose $k$), but have more parameters, can be unstable in high dimensions, and are slower.

When would you choose DBSCAN over k-means?

The trap: Only saying "for non-round clusters".

When clusters have arbitrary shapes, when you don't know the number of clusters, and when the data contains noise or outliers that shouldn't be forced into a cluster (DBSCAN labels them $-1$). Avoid it when cluster densities differ a lot (use HDBSCAN) or in high dimensions where distances concentrate (reduce dimensions first).

How can you evaluate a clustering if you do have labels for a sample?

The trap: Using accuracy directly.

Cluster numbers are arbitrary, so use label-invariant measures: the adjusted Rand index (pairs of points grouped consistently, corrected for chance), normalised mutual information, or homogeneity and completeness. A score near 0 means chance-level agreement. Remember the labels may reflect only one of several valid ways to group the data.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. What does the E-step of EM compute for a Gaussian mixture?

The E-step computes responsibilities; the M-step then updates the means, covariances and weights.

2. Which method can find two interlocking half-moon shapes?

DBSCAN follows dense connected regions; k-means and Ward prefer compact blobs.

3. A point's silhouette is near −1. What does that suggest?

Its own cluster is farther away on average than the nearest other cluster.

4. For Gaussian mixtures, which $k$ does BIC prefer?

BIC $= -2\log L + p\log n$: fit quality plus a penalty for parameters. Lower is better.
