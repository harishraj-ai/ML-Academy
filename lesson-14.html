Before you start

This lesson builds on:

  • Lesson 10: Overfitting, Regularization & Bias–Variance — Error = bias² + variance + noise. Regularization adds a penalty on weight size (L2 ridge shrinks weights; L1 lasso zeros some) to reduce variance; its strength is chosen by cross-validation.
  • Lesson 13: Decision Trees — A decision tree asks a sequence of 'feature ≤ threshold?' questions chosen to reduce impurity (Gini or entropy). Deep trees overfit and are unstable.

Where this fits: Combines many trees into some of the most accurate models available for tabular data — often the first thing professionals try.

1.The wisdom of crowds

In 1906, at a country fair, about 800 people guessed the weight of an ox. Individual guesses were all over the place — but their median was within 1% of the true weight. Many independent, imperfect opinions, combined, beat almost any single expert.

Ensemble methods apply this to models: train many models and combine their predictions. Lesson 13 showed that a single decision tree is unstable — small changes in data produce a different tree. Ensembles turn that weakness into a strength. Random forests and gradient boosting, the two families in this lesson, win a large share of real-world competitions on tabular data.

2.Why combining models works: the maths

Picture itAt a village fair, nobody guesses the ox's weight exactly, but the average of hundreds of guesses is remarkably close. Many imperfect models whose errors differ can be better together than any one of them.

In plain wordsIf many models make different mistakes, a majority vote is right far more often than any one of them — 25 models that are each right 60% of the time are right about 85% of the time together.

Suppose you have 25 classifiers, each correct 60% of the time, and — crucially — they make their mistakes independently. You take a majority vote. The vote is right whenever at least 13 of the 25 are right. Using the binomial distribution:

$$P(\text{majority correct}) = \sum_{k=13}^{25}\binom{25}{k}(0.6)^k(0.4)^{25-k} \approx 0.846$$

Twenty-five 60%-accurate models make an 85%-accurate committee. With 101 such models it rises to about 98%.

($\binom{25}{k}$, "25 choose $k$", counts the ways to pick which $k$ of the 25 models are right.)

The catch: independence

This only works if the models make different mistakes. Twenty-five copies of the same model all fail on the same examples, so voting gains nothing. The art of ensembles is making models that are individually good and diverse. Bagging and random forests create diversity through randomness; boosting creates it by making each model focus on the previous ones' errors.

For regression the effect is easy to see too: averaging $M$ predictions with independent errors divides the variance by $M$ — exactly the "variance" part of the bias–variance trade-off from Lesson 10.

3.Bagging: bootstrap aggregating

We only have one training set. How do we train many different trees? Bagging creates a new training set for each tree by bootstrap sampling: draw $n$ examples from the $n$ originals with replacement. Some examples appear twice or more, some not at all.

Worked example — how much of the data does each tree see?

The chance a particular example is not picked in one draw is $1 - \frac{1}{n}$. Over $n$ draws: $\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$. For large $n$ this approaches $e^{-1} \approx 0.368$.

So each bootstrap sample contains about $1 - 0.368 = 63.2\%$ of the distinct original examples. (For $n = 1000$: $1 - 0.999^{1000} \approx 0.632$.)

Make sense of it

The situation: A random forest trains each tree on a "bootstrap" sample: draw examples at random, with replacement, as many times as there are examples.

What the result tells you: Each tree sees about 63% of the distinct examples, some of them more than once. The other 37% are "out-of-bag" for that tree.

What if…?

  • Small data: with 10 examples, 65% are picked; with 3, 70%. It settles at 63.2% as $n$ grows.
  • The left-out 37% can test each tree for free — the out-of-bag score, a built-in validation estimate.

Why it matters in ML: Different samples make the trees different, so their mistakes partly cancel when they vote. That's how bagging reduces variance without needing more data.

The algorithm:

  1. For $m = 1, \dots, M$: draw a bootstrap sample and train a deep (low-bias, high-variance) tree on it.
  2. To predict: majority vote (classification) or average (regression) over the $M$ trees.

Out-of-bag (OOB) evaluation is a free bonus: each example was left out of about 37% of the trees, so we can predict it using only those trees and get a validation score without a separate validation set.

4.Random forests

Bagged trees are still quite similar: if one feature is very strong, every tree splits on it first. A random forest (Breiman, 2001) adds a second source of randomness: at each split, only a random subset of the features is considered. A common default for classification is $\sqrt{d}$ features out of $d$ (scikit-learn: max_features="sqrt").

This forces trees to use different features, making them less correlated — and the less correlated the trees, the more averaging helps.

SettingEffect
n_estimators (number of trees)More is better up to a plateau; more trees never cause overfitting, they just cost time.
max_featuresSmaller → more diverse trees but each is weaker.
max_depth, min_samples_leafTrees are usually grown deep; limiting them adds bias but can help on noisy data.
Why random forests are so popular

They work well "out of the box" with little tuning, need no feature scaling, handle many features, give feature importances, and are hard to badly overfit. They're an excellent default for tabular data.

5.Boosting: learning from mistakes

Bagging trains models in parallel, independently. Boosting trains them in sequence: each new model focuses on what the ensemble so far gets wrong. It typically uses weak, shallow trees (even depth-1 "stumps") and combines them into a strong model. Bagging mainly reduces variance; boosting mainly reduces bias.

Analogy

A student preparing for an exam does a practice test, then spends the next study session on the questions they got wrong, then retests, and repeats. Each session adds a little knowledge targeted at the remaining weaknesses.

6.AdaBoost

In plain wordsAdaBoost trains models one after another; after each round, the examples it got wrong count more, so the next model focuses on them. Better models get a bigger vote.

AdaBoost (adaptive boosting) gives every training example a weight, starting equal. Each round:

  1. Train a weak learner on the weighted data. Compute its weighted error rate $\varepsilon$.
  2. Give it a vote of strength $\alpha = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1 - \varepsilon}{\varepsilon}$ — accurate learners get louder voices.
  3. Increase the weights of misclassified examples (multiply by $e^{\alpha}$) and decrease the correct ones (multiply by $e^{-\alpha}$), then renormalise so weights sum to 1.

Final prediction: the $\alpha$-weighted vote of all learners.

Worked example — one AdaBoost round

10 examples, each weight 0.1. A stump misclassifies 3 of them: $\varepsilon = 0.3$.

Vote strength: $\alpha = \frac12\ln\frac{0.7}{0.3} = \frac12\ln 2.333 \approx 0.424$.

Wrong examples: $0.1\times e^{0.424} \approx 0.1\times1.528 = 0.153$. Correct ones: $0.1\times e^{-0.424} \approx 0.065$.

Sum: $3\times0.153 + 7\times0.065 \approx 0.917$. After dividing by it: wrong examples weigh about $0.167$ each, correct ones about $0.071$.

The 3 mistakes now hold 50% of the total weight (up from 30%). The next stump is forced to pay attention to them.

Make sense of it

The situation: A teacher sets the next practice test using the questions the class got wrong last time. AdaBoost does the same with its training examples.

What the result tells you: The stump is 70% right, so it gets a vote of 0.424. Then the 3 mistakes are up-weighted until they hold exactly half of the total weight.

What if…?

  • A better stump ($\varepsilon = 0.1$): its vote is 1.10, and again its mistakes end up with exactly 50% of the weight. This always happens: after each round, the last stump is no better than a coin toss on the new weights.
  • A coin-flip stump ($\varepsilon = 0.5$): its vote is 0 — it's ignored.
  • Worse than chance ($\varepsilon = 0.6$): its vote is negative (−0.20), so its predictions are flipped.

Why it matters in ML: Boosting focuses each new model on what the ensemble still gets wrong. That's powerful, but it also means noisy or mislabelled examples get more and more weight — boosting is sensitive to label noise.

7.Gradient boosting

Gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001) is the most widely used form today. The idea for regression with squared error is wonderfully simple:

  1. Start with a constant prediction $F_0(x) = \bar{y}$ (the mean).
  2. Compute the residuals — what's still wrong: $r_i = y_i - F_{m-1}(x_i)$.
  3. Fit a small tree $h_m$ to predict those residuals.
  4. Add it in, scaled by a learning rate $\eta$ (e.g. 0.1): $F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \eta\,h_m(x)$.
  5. Repeat for $M$ rounds.

Why "gradient"? For squared loss $\frac12(y - F)^2$, the negative gradient with respect to the prediction $F$ is exactly the residual $y - F$. So each tree takes a step of gradient descent (Lesson 7) — not in the space of weights, but in the space of predictions. Swapping in a different loss (log loss for classification, absolute error for robustness) gives a different "residual" to fit; the algorithm stays the same.

Worked example — two rounds by hand

Data: $x = 1, 2, 3$ with $y = 3, 5, 8$. Learning rate $\eta = 0.5$ (large, so the effect is visible).

Round 0: $F_0 = \bar{y} = 16/3 \approx 5.33$ for everyone. Residuals: $-2.33, -0.33, 2.67$. MSE $\approx 4.22$.

Round 1: fit a stump to the residuals. The best split is $x \le 2.5$: the left leaf predicts the mean residual of the first two, $(-2.33 - 0.33)/2 = -1.33$; the right leaf predicts $2.67$.

Update: $F_1 = 5.33 + 0.5\times(-1.33) = 4.67$ for $x = 1, 2$, and $5.33 + 0.5\times2.67 = 6.67$ for $x = 3$.

New residuals: $3 - 4.67 = -1.67$, $5 - 4.67 = 0.33$, $8 - 6.67 = 1.33$. MSE drops from 4.22 to $\approx 1.56$.

Round 2 fits a new stump to these new residuals, and so on. Each round fixes part of what's left.

Make sense of it

The situation: Predicting delivery times. The first guess is the average; each new small tree corrects part of what's still wrong.

What the result tells you: After one round the MSE falls from 4.22 to 1.56. Each tree predicts the residuals — the leftover errors — and only half its correction is applied.

What if…?

  • A third round (η = 0.5): MSE 0.20.
  • η = 1 (full corrections): MSE 0.67 after one round and 0.17 after two — faster, but with real noisy data this memorises the training set.
  • η = 0.1: after 10 rounds the MSE is still 0.85. Small steps need many trees, but generalise better.

Why it matters in ML: The learning rate and the number of trees work together: smaller steps plus more trees usually give the best test accuracy. Libraries like XGBoost and LightGBM use early stopping on a validation set to choose when to stop.

The learning rate and number of trees trade off: a small $\eta$ (0.01–0.1) with many trees generalises better than a large $\eta$ with few. Unlike random forests, boosting can overfit with too many rounds, so the number of trees is tuned — often with early stopping on a validation set.

Step-by-step derivationWhy each tree fits the residuals
  1. $$\ell = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(y - F)^2$$

    Squared loss for one example, where $F$ is the ensemble's current prediction.

  2. $$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial F} = -(y - F)$$

    Differentiate with respect to the prediction (chain rule; the $\frac12$ cancels the 2).

  3. $$-\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial F} = y - F = r$$

    The negative gradient is exactly the residual.

  4. $$F \leftarrow F - \eta\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial F} = F + \eta\,r$$

    A gradient descent step on the prediction itself.

  5. $$F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \eta\,h_m(x)$$

    We can't adjust predictions point by point for unseen data, so a small tree $h_m$ learns to approximate the residuals $r$ as a function of $x$.

Advanced · optionalGradient boosting is gradient descent in function space

Think of the predictions $F(x_1),\dots,F(x_n)$ as the parameters. For the loss $L = \sum_i \tfrac12(y_i - F(x_i))^2$ the gradient with respect to each prediction is

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial F(x_i)} = -(y_i - F(x_i)),$$

so the residuals are exactly the negative gradient. Fitting a small tree $h$ to the residuals and updating $F \leftarrow F + \eta h$ is one gradient-descent step, with the tree generalising the step to new points. For any other differentiable loss, boosting fits trees to the pseudo-residuals $-\partial L/\partial F(x_i)$: for log loss these are $y_i - p_i$, which is how gradient-boosted classifiers work. XGBoost goes one step further and also uses the second derivatives (a Newton step).

9.Modern boosting libraries

In plain wordsXGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are fast, polished versions of gradient boosting used widely in industry.

XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are highly optimised gradient-boosting libraries. XGBoost, for example, adds regularization of the trees directly into its objective:

$$\text{obj} = \sum_i \ell(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_k \Omega(f_k), \qquad \Omega(f) = \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\sum_j w_j^2$$

where $T$ is the number of leaves in a tree and $w_j$ are its leaf values — penalising both big trees and extreme predictions. scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier uses a similar fast approach: it bins each feature into at most 255 bins, handles missing values natively, and turns on early stopping automatically for datasets with more than 10,000 samples.

11.In Python

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import (RandomForestClassifier, AdaBoostClassifier,
                              GradientBoostingClassifier, HistGradientBoostingClassifier)

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
models = {
    "single tree": DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0),
    "random forest": RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0),
    "AdaBoost": AdaBoostClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=0),
    "gradient boosting": GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0),
    "hist. gradient boosting": HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0),
}
for name, model in models.items():
    s = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5)
    print(f"{name:24s} {s.mean():.3f} ± {s.std():.3f}")

rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, oob_score=True, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
print("out-of-bag accuracy:", round(rf.oob_score_, 3))

Output:

single tree              0.917 ± 0.016
random forest            0.963 ± 0.022
AdaBoost                 0.977 ± 0.009
gradient boosting        0.963 ± 0.021
hist. gradient boosting  0.965 ± 0.018
out-of-bag accuracy: 0.965

Every ensemble beats the single tree by 5–6 percentage points, with default settings. Notice the out-of-bag accuracy (0.965) closely matches the cross-validated score (0.963) — a free and reliable estimate. On this particular dataset AdaBoost edges ahead; on another dataset the order could differ, which is why we compare with cross-validation.

12.Running example: Chennai Homes

With 12 houses, leave-one-out accuracy is very noisy: a single tree scores 0.50, a random forest 0.58. The forest spreads importance across age, distance and size rather than relying on one split.

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, LeaveOneOut
cols = ["size_m2", "bedrooms", "dist_km", "age_yrs"]
for name, m in [("single tree", DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0)),
                ("random forest", RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0))]:
    acc = cross_val_score(m, df[cols], df["sold_fast"], cv=LeaveOneOut()).mean()
    print(f"{name:14s} leave-one-out accuracy {acc:.2f}")
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0).fit(df[cols], df["sold_fast"])
print({c: round(float(v), 2) for c, v in zip(cols, rf.feature_importances_)})

Output:

single tree    leave-one-out accuracy 0.50
random forest  leave-one-out accuracy 0.58
{'size_m2': 0.27, 'bedrooms': 0.04, 'dist_km': 0.31, 'age_yrs': 0.37}

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythMore trees in a random forest can cause overfitting.

RealityAdding trees to a random forest doesn't make it overfit — it only costs time. (Boosting is different: too many rounds can overfit.)

MythAn ensemble of weak models can't beat one strong model.

RealityCombining many diverse, decent models is one of the most reliable ways to get top accuracy on tabular data.

Responsible AI · Accuracy versus explainability

Forests and boosting are often more accurate than a single tree but much harder to explain. Post-hoc tools (feature importance, SHAP values, partial-dependence plots) help, but they describe the model approximately and can be misleading — permutation importance, for example, spreads credit unpredictably between correlated features. For high-stakes decisions, consider whether a slightly less accurate but interpretable model is the responsible choice.

13.Questions beginners often ask

Random forest or gradient boosting?

Random forests work well with almost no tuning. Gradient boosting is often slightly more accurate but needs its learning rate and number of trees tuned.

How many trees?

For random forests, hundreds is typical — more never overfits, it only costs time. For boosting, choose with early stopping or cross-validation.

Can I still interpret an ensemble?

Less directly than one tree, but feature importances and tools such as permutation importance and partial dependence plots help.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Using too many boosting rounds with a large learning rate, which overfits.
  • Assuming more random-forest trees causes overfitting — it only costs time.
  • Ensembling near-identical models, which barely helps.
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • Bagging vs boosting — Committee in parallel vs relay team in sequence.
  • AdaBoost vs gradient boosting — Re-weight the examples vs fit the leftovers.
  • Out-of-bag score vs validation score — Free built-in check vs a dedicated exam.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

Does bagging reduce bias or variance? And boosting?

The trap: "Both reduce error" without saying how.

Bagging (random forests) mainly reduces variance by averaging many deep, overfit trees. Boosting mainly reduces bias by adding simple models that correct remaining errors.

Will a random forest overfit if you add more trees?

The trap: Treating trees like boosting rounds.

No. More trees make the average more stable; the test error levels off, it doesn't rise. Gradient boosting is different: more rounds can overfit, so use early stopping.

Why does a random forest choose a random subset of features at each split?

The trap: "For speed."

To decorrelate the trees. The variance of an average of $B$ trees is $\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$, where $\rho$ is their correlation. With $\rho = 0.5$ and 100 trees it's 0.505σ²; with $\rho = 0.1$ it's 0.109σ². Bootstrap samples alone leave trees too similar, because they all split on the strongest feature.

What is the out-of-bag score?

The trap: Not knowing about it.

Each tree skips about 37% of the examples. Predicting each example using only the trees that didn't see it gives a free validation estimate, without a separate hold-out set.

14.Key takeaways

  • Combining many diverse models reduces error — but only if their mistakes are not all the same.
  • Bagging trains models on bootstrap samples (~63% of the data each) and averages them, reducing variance.
  • Random forests also pick a random subset of features at each split, making trees more diverse.
  • Boosting trains models in sequence; AdaBoost re-weights mistakes, gradient boosting fits each new tree to the residuals (the negative gradient).
  • Boosting needs the learning rate and number of trees tuned; random forests work well with little tuning.

15.Check your understanding

1. Why does a majority vote of many classifiers help?

If models err on different examples, the majority is usually right even when individuals are wrong.

2. What fraction of distinct examples does a bootstrap sample contain, roughly?

$1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \approx 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$.

3. What does a random forest add on top of bagging?

Feature subsampling decorrelates the trees, making the average stronger.

4. In gradient boosting with squared error, each new tree is trained to predict…

The residual is the negative gradient of the squared loss; fitting it is a gradient descent step in prediction space.

5. A weak learner has weighted error $\varepsilon = 0.5$. What is its AdaBoost vote $\alpha$?

$\alpha = \frac12\ln\frac{0.5}{0.5} = \frac12\ln 1 = 0$. A coin-flip learner gets no say.

16.Going deeper: tuning ensembles and how XGBoost works

In plain wordsWhy averaging many trees works only if they disagree (correlation sets the limit), how to tune boosting with a learning rate and early stopping, what XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost add to plain gradient boosting, and when combining different models (stacking) is worth it.

Intermediate · skip on a first read

Random forests and gradient boosting are the workhorses of tabular ML. This section explains the maths of why averaging works (and why it needs the trees to disagree), how to tune boosting with early stopping, what XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost add to plain gradient boosting, and when combining different models (stacking) is worth the effort.

You'll need: this lesson, tree instability from Lesson 13's "Going deeper", and early stopping from Lesson 10's.

Why averaging works: correlation is the limit

If $B$ models each have variance $\sigma^2$ and every pair has correlation $\rho$, their average has variance

$$\operatorname{Var}(\text{average}) = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2$$

The second term vanishes as you add trees, but the first doesn't. Correlation sets the floor: averaging 1,000 identical trees ($\rho = 1$) is no better than one. That's why a random forest considers only a random subset of features at each split (max_features): it makes the trees disagree more. The trade-off is that each tree becomes individually weaker.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0, stratify=y)
# max_features controls how different the trees are
for mf in [1.0, 0.5, "sqrt", 1]:
    rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, max_features=mf, oob_score=True, random_state=0, n_jobs=-1).fit(Xtr, ytr)
    preds = np.array([t.predict(Xte) for t in rf.estimators_])
    corr = np.corrcoef(preds)[np.triu_indices(len(preds), 1)].mean()
    print(f"max_features {str(mf):5}: tree-to-tree correlation {corr:.2f}   out-of-bag accuracy {rf.oob_score_:.3f}   test {rf.score(Xte, yte):.3f}")

Output:

max_features 1.0  : tree-to-tree correlation 0.83   out-of-bag accuracy 0.960   test 0.930
max_features 0.5  : tree-to-tree correlation 0.82   out-of-bag accuracy 0.957   test 0.942
max_features sqrt : tree-to-tree correlation 0.80   out-of-bag accuracy 0.967   test 0.953
max_features 1    : tree-to-tree correlation 0.70   out-of-bag accuracy 0.965   test 0.936

Using all features at every split (1.0, which is plain bagging), trees agree with correlation 0.83 and the test accuracy is 0.930. With $\sqrt{30} \approx 5$ features per split, correlation drops to 0.80 and accuracy rises to 0.953. With just 1 feature per split, correlation falls to 0.70, but each tree is now so weak that accuracy dips again (0.936). The default "sqrt" is a good middle. The out-of-bag accuracy, computed on the rows each tree didn't see in its bootstrap sample, tracks the right ordering without needing a separate validation set.

Tuning gradient boosting: learning rate and early stopping

Each boosting round adds a tree multiplied by the learning rate (also called shrinkage). A smaller rate means each tree corrects less, so you need more trees, but the combined model usually generalises better. Rather than guess the number of trees, hold back part of the training data and stop early when the validation score stops improving (Lesson 10's "Going deeper"):

from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

for lr in [1.0, 0.3, 0.1, 0.03]:
    gb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(learning_rate=lr, max_iter=2000, early_stopping=True,
                                        validation_fraction=0.2, n_iter_no_change=30, random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
    print(f"learning rate {lr:4}: trees used {gb.n_iter_:4d}   test accuracy {gb.score(Xte, yte):.3f}")

Output:

learning rate  1.0: trees used   35   test accuracy 0.924
learning rate  0.3: trees used   49   test accuracy 0.942
learning rate  0.1: trees used   83   test accuracy 0.947
learning rate 0.03: trees used  203   test accuracy 0.936

With rate 1.0, boosting stops after 35 trees at 0.924; at 0.1 it uses 83 trees and reaches 0.947. Going smaller still (0.03, 203 trees) doesn't help on this small dataset; the differences here are within test-set noise (Lesson 9's "Going deeper"). The usual recipe: pick a modest learning rate (0.05–0.1), let early stopping choose the number of trees, then tune tree size (depth or number of leaves) and regularization. Halving the learning rate roughly doubles the trees needed.

What XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost add

Plain gradient boosting fits each new tree to the negative gradient of the loss (this lesson's "Advanced" box). XGBoost made three key improvements:

  • Second-order steps. It uses each point's gradient $g_i$ and curvature $h_i$ (a Newton step, Lesson 3's "Going deeper"). The best value for a leaf is then a closed formula: $$w^* = -\frac{\sum_{i \in \text{leaf}} g_i}{\sum_{i \in \text{leaf}} h_i + \lambda}$$
  • Regularization built into the objective. A penalty $\gamma T + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_j w_j^2$ on the number of leaves $T$ and on the leaf values. A split is made only if its gain exceeds $\gamma$, which prunes automatically.
  • Engineering: it learns which way missing values should go at each split, computes in parallel, and supports sparse data.

LightGBM speeds this up by grouping each feature's values into histograms (about 255 bins) and growing trees leaf-wise, always splitting the leaf with the biggest gain. Scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier, used above, follows the same design. CatBoost handles categorical features natively, using carefully ordered target statistics that avoid leakage, and builds symmetric trees that are fast at prediction time. On most tabular problems the three are close; differences in tuning usually matter more than the choice of library.

Stacking: combining different models

Stacking trains several different models, then a simple "blender" (often logistic regression) that learns how much to trust each one. The blender must be trained on out-of-fold predictions; otherwise it learns from predictions on rows the models memorised, which is a form of leakage (Lesson 5).

from sklearn.ensemble import StackingClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

base = [("forest", RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0)),
        ("boost", HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0)),
        ("knn", make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(15)))]
for name, m in base:
    print(f"{name:7}: test accuracy {m.fit(Xtr, ytr).score(Xte, yte):.3f}")
stack = StackingClassifier(base, final_estimator=LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000), cv=5).fit(Xtr, ytr)
print(f"stacked: test accuracy {stack.score(Xte, yte):.3f}   blender weights {stack.final_estimator_.coef_.round(2)}")

Output:

forest : test accuracy 0.953
boost  : test accuracy 0.942
knn    : test accuracy 0.942
stacked: test accuracy 0.942   blender weights [[2.84 2.28 3.05]]

Here, stacking a forest, boosting and kNN scores 0.942: no better than the forest alone (0.953). That's an honest and common result: stacking helps most when the base models are strong and make different kinds of mistakes, and with 171 test cases small gains are invisible anyway. It's popular in competitions, where every 0.1% matters. In production, its extra complexity, latency and maintenance often aren't worth it.

Interview corner · intermediate

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

What does XGBoost do differently from classic gradient boosting?

The trap: "It's just faster."

It optimises a regularized objective with a second-order (Newton) approximation: each point contributes a gradient $g_i$ and curvature $h_i$, leaf values have the closed form $-\sum g/(\sum h + \lambda)$, and a split's gain must exceed $\gamma$ (a cost per leaf). It also learns default directions for missing values, uses column and row subsampling, and is engineered for parallel, sparse and out-of-core data.

How would you tune a gradient-boosted model in limited time?

The trap: Grid-searching everything at once.

Fix a moderate learning rate (0.05–0.1) and use early stopping on a validation set to pick the number of trees. Then tune tree complexity (max_depth or num_leaves, min samples per leaf), then subsampling (rows and columns) and L2 regularization, using random or Bayesian search with cross-validation. Finally, lower the learning rate, raise the tree limit, and refit with early stopping for a small final gain.

Why does a random forest choose features randomly at each split, in terms of the variance formula?

The trap: "To be random."

The variance of an average of $B$ trees is $\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/B$. Adding trees only shrinks the second term; the first, set by the correlation $\rho$ between trees, remains. Bootstrap samples alone leave trees highly correlated, because the strongest features dominate every tree's top splits. Random feature subsets lower $\rho$, and so the floor, at the cost of slightly weaker individual trees.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. Averaging 1,000 trees that are perfectly correlated ($\rho = 1$) reduces variance by…

$\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/B = \sigma^2$ when $\rho = 1$. Identical trees give no benefit.

2. In a random forest, lowering max_features…

Fewer candidate features per split means more disagreement between trees, at the cost of individual accuracy.

3. If you halve gradient boosting's learning rate, you usually need about…

Each tree corrects half as much. Let early stopping pick the exact number.

4. XGBoost's leaf value $w^* = -\sum g_i / (\sum h_i + \lambda)$ uses…

It's a regularized Newton step for each leaf.

5. Why must a stacking blender be trained on out-of-fold predictions?

Base models fit their own training rows too well; the blender needs predictions like those on new data.
