1.Why probability and statistics?

Real data is messy. Two houses of the same size sell for different prices; the same symptoms can come from different illnesses. ML models live with this uncertainty, so they speak the language of probability: a spam filter doesn't say "spam", it says "97% likely spam". Statistics gives us tools to summarise data — averages, spread — that we'll use in almost every lesson.

2.Probability basics

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how likely an event is: 0 = impossible, 1 = certain, 0.5 = as likely as not. We write $P(A)$ for "the probability of event $A$".

When all outcomes are equally likely:

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes where } A \text{ happens}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$
Worked example — a fair die

$P(\text{roll a 6}) = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$. $P(\text{roll an even number}) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5$ (outcomes 2, 4, 6).

Make sense of it

The situation: A board game needs a 6 to start. How often will that happen?

What the result tells you: A probability of 1/6 means that over many rolls, about one roll in six is a 6. Roll 600 times and you'd expect around 100 sixes — rarely exactly 100.

What if…?

  • Two tries: the chance of at least one 6 is $1 - (5/6)^2 \approx$ 0.31.
  • Six tries: about 0.67 — not 1. Even with six rolls, one game in three still waits.

Why it matters in ML: Classifiers output probabilities. A prediction of "70% spam" means that among many emails given 70%, about 7 in 10 should really be spam — a property called calibration.

Three rules do most of the work:

  • Complement: $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$. Probability of not rolling a 6 is $5/6$.
  • Addition (for events that can't both happen): $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$. Rolling a 1 or a 2: $1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3$.
  • Multiplication (for independent events — one doesn't affect the other): $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. Two coin flips both heads: $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$.

3.Conditional probability

Often we know something already, and that changes the odds. $P(A \mid B)$ — "probability of $A$ given $B$" — is the probability of $A$ when we only consider situations where $B$ happened:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}$$
Worked example — emails

Out of 100 emails, 30 are spam. 20 emails contain the word "free", and 15 of those are spam.

$P(\text{spam}) = 30/100 = 0.30$.
$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"}) = 15/20 = 0.75$.

Knowing the email contains "free" raised the spam probability from 30% to 75%. This is the core idea behind Naive Bayes spam filters (Lesson 12): features change our beliefs about the label.

Make sense of it

The situation: You run a mail service. Overall, 30% of emails are spam. Should the word "free" make you more suspicious?

What the result tells you: Among emails with "free", 75% are spam, compared with 30% overall. The word is strong evidence, because it raises the probability by a factor of 2.5.

What if…?

  • If only 6 of the 20 "free" emails were spam, $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{free}) = 0.30$ — the same as the overall rate. Then the word tells you nothing.
  • If 19 of 20 were spam, it would be 0.95 — almost decisive.

Why it matters in ML: A feature is useful exactly when it changes the probability of the label. Comparing $P(\text{label} \mid \text{feature})$ with $P(\text{label})$ is a quick way to find informative features.

Careful: $P(A \mid B) \neq P(B \mid A)$

$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"}) = 15/20 = 0.75$, but $P(\text{"free"} \mid \text{spam}) = 15/30 = 0.5$. Mixing these up is one of the most common reasoning errors — and the next section shows how to convert between them.

4.Bayes' theorem

Picture itAn airport scanner beeps. Is the bag dangerous? It depends on two things: how good the scanner is, and how rare dangerous bags are in the first place. Bayes' theorem combines both.

Bayes' theorem flips a conditional probability around:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}$$

In ML language, with $A$ = "a hypothesis" (e.g. has the disease) and $B$ = "the evidence" (e.g. tested positive):

  • $P(A)$ — the prior: what we believed before seeing evidence.
  • $P(B \mid A)$ — the likelihood: how probable the evidence is if the hypothesis is true.
  • $P(A \mid B)$ — the posterior: our updated belief after seeing the evidence.
Worked example — the surprising medical test

A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches it 90% of the time when present (sensitivity), but also gives a positive result to 5% of healthy people (false positives). You test positive. What's the chance you have the disease?

Most people guess around 90%. Let's compute.

$P(D) = 0.01$, $P(+ \mid D) = 0.90$, $P(+ \mid \text{healthy}) = 0.05$.

First, the overall chance of a positive test (sick-and-positive plus healthy-and-positive):

$$P(+) = 0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585$$

Then Bayes:

$$P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.90 \times 0.01}{0.0585} \approx 0.154$$

Only about 15%! Because the disease is rare, the false positives from the 99% of healthy people outnumber the true positives.

The same thing with counts (often easier): in 1,000 people, 10 are sick and 9 of them test positive. Of the 990 healthy people, about 49.5 test positive. So of roughly 58.5 positives, only 9 are sick: $9/58.5 \approx 15\%$.

Make sense of it

The situation: You're a doctor explaining a positive screening result to a worried patient.

What the result tells you: 15% means: among people who test positive, about 15 in 100 actually have the disease. The rest are false alarms, because healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones.

What if…?

  • A more common disease (10%): the chance becomes 67%. The same test means something very different in a different population.
  • A better test (false positives 1% instead of 5%): about 48%.
  • A second, independent positive test: start from 15% and apply Bayes again — about 77%.

Why it matters in ML: The base rate controls what a positive prediction means. Fraud, disease and fault detectors work with rare events, so a model with high accuracy can still produce mostly false alarms — which is why precision is reported separately (Lesson 9).

Step-by-step derivationDerive Bayes' theorem in four lines
  1. $$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}$$

    The definition of conditional probability.

  2. $$P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(A)}$$

    The same definition with the roles of $A$ and $B$ swapped.

  3. $$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$$

    Multiply both sides of the previous line by $P(A)$.

  4. $$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}$$

    Substitute into the first line. That's Bayes' theorem.

  5. $$P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \text{not }A)\,P(\text{not }A)$$

    To compute the bottom, add up the two ways $B$ can happen — exactly what we did in the medical-test example.

6.Random variables and expected value

In plain wordsA random variable is a number that depends on chance, like a dice roll. Its expected value is its long-run average — for a die, 3.5.

A random variable is a quantity whose value depends on chance — the result of a die roll, tomorrow's temperature, a house's sale price. Its expected value (mean) is the average you'd get over many repetitions: each possible value weighted by its probability.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x \cdot P(X = x)$$
Worked example — a die

$\mathbb{E}[X] = 1\cdot\tfrac16 + 2\cdot\tfrac16 + \dots + 6\cdot\tfrac16 = \tfrac{21}{6} = 3.5$. You can never roll 3.5, but it's the long-run average.

Make sense of it

The situation: A game pays you in rupees the number you roll. What's it worth on average?

What the result tells you: 3.5 is the long-run average payout per roll. No single roll pays 3.5, but over 1,000 rolls you'd collect about 3,500.

What if…?

  • A loaded die that shows 6 half the time (each other face 1/10): the expected value rises to 4.5.
  • Pay to play: if each roll costs 4, a fair die loses you 0.5 per roll on average.

Why it matters in ML: A model's true loss is an expected value over all possible data. We can't compute it exactly, so we estimate it with the average loss on a sample — the training or test set.

Advanced · optionalA useful identity: Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²

Write $\mu = E[X]$ and expand the definition, using the fact that expectation is linear:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(X-\mu)^2] = E[X^2] - 2\mu E[X] + \mu^2 = E[X^2] - \mu^2.$$

Check with a fair die: $E[X] = 3.5$ and $E[X^2] = (1+4+9+16+25+36)/6 = 91/6$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 91/6 - 12.25 = 35/12 \approx 2.917$. The same identity is how libraries compute variance in one pass over the data.

7.Describing data: mean, variance, standard deviation

Picture itTwo coffee shops both average 5 minutes per order. At one, every order takes 4–6 minutes; at the other, some take 1 minute and some 15. The average is identical — the experience isn't. To describe data you need a centre and a spread, and you need to know how one strange value can move them.

Given $n$ numbers $x_1, \dots, x_n$:

The mean (average) is the centre:

$$\bar{x} = \mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$

The variance measures spread — the average squared distance from the mean:

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2$$

The standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$ is spread in the original units.

Worked example

Exam scores: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12.

Mean: $\mu = (4+6+8+10+12)/5 = 40/5 = 8$.

Distances from the mean: $-4, -2, 0, 2, 4$. Squared: $16, 4, 0, 4, 16$.

Variance: $\sigma^2 = 40/5 = 8$. Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$.

So a typical score is about 2.8 points away from the average of 8.

Make sense of it

The situation: A teacher has five students' test scores: 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12. She wants one number for "how the class did" and one for "how spread out" the scores are.

What the result tells you: The mean, 8, is the balance point: the gaps below it (−4, −2) exactly cancel the gaps above it (+2, +4). The standard deviation, 2.83, says a typical student is about 3 points from that average.

What if…?

  • One outlier. A data-entry slip turns 12 into 52. The mean jumps from 8 to 16 — now higher than four of the five students — while the median stays 8. The standard deviation leaps from 2.83 to 18.1.
  • Add a sixth student who scores exactly 8: the mean stays 8, but the standard deviation falls to 2.58, because a score at the centre makes the class look less spread out.
  • Give everyone 10 bonus points: the mean becomes 18; the standard deviation stays 2.83. Shifting doesn't change spread.
  • Double every score: the mean becomes 16 and the standard deviation doubles to 5.66.
  • Same mean, different class: scores 0, 0, 8, 16, 16 also average 8, but their standard deviation is 7.16. And 8, 8, 8, 8, 8 has a standard deviation of 0. The mean alone can't tell these classes apart.

Why it matters in ML: The mean is pulled by every value, so a single extreme value can move it a lot; the median ignores how extreme the values are. That's why you check for outliers before averaging, fill missing values with the median (Lesson 20), and why MSE — which is an average — is sensitive to outliers. Standardising features uses exactly this mean and standard deviation.

Population vs sample

Dividing by $n$ gives the population variance. When estimating from a sample, statisticians often divide by $n-1$ instead (the "sample variance"), which corrects a small bias. NumPy's np.var divides by $n$ by default; pandas' .var() divides by $n-1$. For large datasets the difference is negligible.

Also useful: the median (middle value when sorted) is robust to outliers. Incomes of 30k, 35k, 40k, 45k and 1M have a mean of 230k but a median of 40k — the median describes the "typical" person far better.

8.Standardisation (z-scores)

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

A score of 12 above: $z = (12 - 8)/2.83 \approx 1.41$. Converting every feature to z-scores — mean 0, standard deviation 1 — is called standardisation. It's one of the most common preprocessing steps in ML (scikit-learn's StandardScaler), because many algorithms work badly when features have wildly different scales (Lessons 11 and 20).

9.Probability distributions

In plain wordsA distribution describes which values are likely. The most famous is the bell-shaped normal distribution: most values sit near the average, and about 95% lie within two standard deviations of it.

A distribution describes all the values a random variable can take and how likely each is.

Bernoulli: a single yes/no

One trial with success probability $p$: $P(1) = p$, $P(0) = 1-p$. A coin flip is Bernoulli with $p=0.5$. A classifier's "is this spam?" output is a Bernoulli probability.

Normal (Gaussian): the bell curve

Many natural quantities — heights, measurement errors — follow the bell-shaped normal distribution, described by its mean $\mu$ (centre) and standard deviation $\sigma$ (width). Its density is:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

You don't need to memorise the formula — just its shape and this rule of thumb:

  • about 68% of values lie within 1 $\sigma$ of the mean,
  • about 95% within 2 $\sigma$,
  • about 99.7% within 3 $\sigma$.
Worked example

Adult heights in a population: $\mu = 170$ cm, $\sigma = 10$ cm. About 95% of people are between $170 - 20 = 150$ and $170 + 20 = 190$ cm. Someone 200 cm tall is 3 $\sigma$ above the mean — rarer than 1 in 300 (only 0.3% fall outside $\pm 3\sigma$, half of them above).

Make sense of it

The situation: A clothing company is planning garment sizes for adults whose average height is 170 cm and standard deviation 10 cm.

What the result tells you: Within one standard deviation (160–180 cm) are about 68% of people; within two (150–190 cm) about 95%. 200 cm is three standard deviations up: about 1 person in 740 is taller.

What if…?

  • Someone 185 cm tall has $z = 1.5$: about 6.7% of people are taller.
  • A less varied population ($\sigma = 5$ cm): 95% now fall between 160 and 180 cm, and 200 cm is six standard deviations up — about 1 in a billion.

Why it matters in ML: z-scores put different features on a common scale and flag unusual values: a value with $|z| > 3$ is worth checking as a possible error or anomaly. Many models also assume errors are roughly normal.

10.Try it: sampling from a normal distribution

Each click draws random samples from a normal distribution and adds them to the histogram. With few samples the shape is ragged; with many, it settles into the bell curve. Adjust $\mu$ and $\sigma$ to see the centre move and the width change.

11.How this connects to machine learning

  • Classifiers output probabilities: logistic regression (Lesson 8) outputs $P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$.
  • Naive Bayes (Lesson 12) is Bayes' theorem applied to features.
  • Noise: we model data as "true pattern + random noise", often assumed normal. That's why the squared error loss is so natural.
  • Evaluation (Lesson 9) reports means and standard deviations of scores across experiments.
  • Preprocessing uses means and standard deviations to standardise features.

12.In Python

import numpy as np

scores = np.array([4, 6, 8, 10, 12])
print(scores.mean())           # 8.0
print(scores.var())            # 8.0   (divides by n)
print(scores.std())            # 2.828...
print(np.median([30, 35, 40, 45, 1000]))   # 40.0

z = (scores - scores.mean()) / scores.std()
print(z.round(2))              # [-1.41 -0.71  0.    0.71  1.41]

# Bayes: the medical test
p_d, sens, fpr = 0.01, 0.90, 0.05
p_pos = sens * p_d + fpr * (1 - p_d)
print(round(sens * p_d / p_pos, 3))        # 0.154

# Sample 100,000 heights and check the 68-95-99.7 rule
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
h = rng.normal(170, 10, size=100_000)
print(np.mean(np.abs(h - 170) < 20))       # ≈ 0.95

13.Running example: Chennai Homes

The average price is 72.1 lakh with a standard deviation of 17.3, so house 12 (96 lakh) has a z-score of 1.38. Half of all houses sold fast, but 75% of Central ones did: $P(\text{fast} \mid \text{Central}) = 0.75$. Turned around, only half of the fast sellers were Central — remember $P(A\mid B) \ne P(B\mid A)$.

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
p = df["price_lakh"]
print("mean", round(p.mean(), 2), "std", round(p.std(ddof=0), 2))
print("z-score of house 12:", round((96 - p.mean()) / p.std(ddof=0), 2))
print("P(sold fast) =", df["sold_fast"].mean())
central = df[df["area"] == "Central"]
print("P(sold fast | Central) =", central["sold_fast"].mean())
print("P(Central | sold fast) =", round((df[df["sold_fast"] == 1]["area"] == "Central").mean(), 3))

Output:

mean 72.08 std 17.29
z-score of house 12: 1.38
P(sold fast) = 0.5
P(sold fast | Central) = 0.75
P(Central | sold fast) = 0.5
Where this is used
  • Medical test interpretation
  • A/B testing of websites
  • Risk scores and probability forecasts
  • Anomaly detection (values many standard deviations from normal)

Choose it when: Whenever you need to reason about uncertainty or summarise data.

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythA 95%-accurate test means a positive result is 95% likely to be right.

RealityIt depends on how common the condition is. With rare conditions, most positives can be false — as the medical example showed (15%).

MythProbabilities of 0 or 1 are fine for a model to output.

RealityBeing certain is dangerous: one confident mistake gives infinite log loss. Real models keep a little doubt.

Responsible AI · Base rates and small groups

The Bayes demo shows that even an accurate test produces mostly false alarms when the condition is rare. The same maths applies to systems that flag rare behaviour such as fraud or crime: most people flagged may be innocent. Estimates for small subgroups are also far noisier than for the majority, so a model can look accurate overall while being unreliable for minorities.

14.Questions beginners often ask

Why does ML care about probability?

Because data is noisy and predictions are uncertain. Outputs like "92% likely spam" are more useful than a bare yes/no, and many models are built on probability rules.

Mean or median?

Use the median when data is skewed or has outliers (incomes, house prices). Use the mean when data is roughly symmetric.

Is the normal distribution always the right assumption?

No. It fits many measurements, but counts, waiting times, incomes and yes/no outcomes follow other distributions. Always look at your data.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Confusing $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$ — the base-rate trap in the medical test example.
  • Multiplying probabilities of events that are not independent.
  • Using the mean to describe skewed data such as incomes, where the median is far more representative.
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • Probability vs likelihood — Probability predicts data; likelihood judges parameters.
  • Independent vs mutually exclusive — Exclusive events tell you a lot about each other.
  • Correlation vs causation — Look for the hidden third variable.
  • Uncorrelated vs independent — Correlation only sees straight lines.
  • Standard deviation vs standard error — SD is about the people; SE is about the estimate.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

What's the difference between probability and likelihood?

The trap: Using the words interchangeably.

Same formula, different viewpoint. Probability fixes the parameters and asks how probable different data are (it sums to 1 over data). Likelihood fixes the observed data and asks how well different parameters explain it (it needn't sum to 1). Training by maximum likelihood picks the parameters under which the data are most probable.

If two variables have zero correlation, are they independent?

The trap: Yes, because nothing's going on.

No. Correlation only measures linear relationships. For $x = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2$ and $y = x^2$, the correlation is exactly 0, yet $y$ is completely determined by $x$.

Are mutually exclusive events independent?

The trap: They seem to have nothing to do with each other.

The opposite: if two events can't both happen (and each has non-zero probability), learning that one happened tells you the other didn't. That's strong dependence.

Why divide by $n - 1$ instead of $n$ for a sample variance?

The trap: "It's just the formula."

The sample mean is fitted to the same data, so deviations from it are slightly too small on average. Dividing by $n - 1$ (Bessel's correction) makes the estimate unbiased. For large $n$ the difference is tiny; NumPy's np.var divides by $n$ unless you set ddof=1.

15.Key takeaways

  • Probabilities run from 0 to 1. Key rules: complement, addition, multiplication for independent events.
  • Conditional probability $P(A\mid B)$ updates odds given information; $P(A\mid B) \ne P(B\mid A)$.
  • Bayes' theorem: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Rare events stay fairly unlikely even after positive evidence.
  • Mean = centre, variance/standard deviation = spread; z-scores standardise features.
  • The normal distribution: 68% / 95% / 99.7% within 1 / 2 / 3 standard deviations.

16.Check your understanding

1. You flip a fair coin 3 times. What is the probability of 3 heads?

Independent flips multiply: $0.5^3 = 1/8$.

2. Data: 2, 4, 6. What is the variance (dividing by $n$)?

Mean is 4. Squared distances: 4, 0, 4. Average: $8/3 \approx 2.67$.

3. In Bayes' theorem, $P(A)$ is called the…

The prior is your belief before seeing the evidence. The posterior $P(A\mid B)$ is after.

4. A value has z-score −2. What does that mean?

$z = (x - \mu)/\sigma = -2$ means $x$ is two standard deviations below the mean.

5. A normal distribution has $\mu = 50$, $\sigma = 5$. Roughly what fraction of values fall between 45 and 55?

45–55 is within 1 standard deviation of the mean: about 68%.

Each distribution is a model of a particular kind of randomness. Recognising which one fits your data tells you which loss function and which model to use.

from scipy import stats

print("Binomial: P(8 or more of 10 visitors click | p 0.5):", round(stats.binom.sf(7, 10, 0.5), 4))
print("Poisson:  P(no support calls in an hour | 3 per hour):", round(stats.poisson.pmf(0, 3), 4))

Output:

Poisson:  P(no support calls in an hour | 3 per hour): 0.0498

For example, if visitors click with probability 0.5, seeing 8 or more clicks out of 10 has probability 0.0547. If calls arrive at 3 per hour on average, a whole hour with no calls has probability $e^{-3} \approx 0.0498$. And if the average wait is 20 minutes, a wait of over 30 minutes has probability $e^{-30/20} \approx 0.2231$.

Maximum likelihood: the parameters that make the data least surprising

Suppose a coin lands heads 7 times in 10 flips. For any guess $p$ of the heads probability, the likelihood is the probability of exactly this data under that guess. We work with its log, because a product turns into a sum:

$$\log L(p) = 7\log p + 3\log(1-p)$$

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) picks the $p$ that makes this as large as possible. Set the derivative to zero:

$$\frac{7}{p} - \frac{3}{1-p} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 7(1-p) = 3p \;\Rightarrow\; p = 0.7$$

The answer is the obvious one, the observed fraction. But the same recipe works for every model: least squares is the MLE for normal noise (Lesson 6's "Going deeper"), and log loss is the MLE for yes/no outcomes (Lesson 8).

# Maximum likelihood for a coin: 7 heads in 10 flips
p_grid = np.linspace(0.01, 0.99, 99)
loglik = 7 * np.log(p_grid) + 3 * np.log(1 - p_grid)
print("p with the highest likelihood:", round(p_grid[np.argmax(loglik)], 2))

# MLE for a normal distribution divides by n, which underestimates the variance
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
samples = rng.normal(0, 2, size=(100000, 5))            # 100,000 small samples, true variance 4
print("average variance, divide by n:    ", round(samples.var(axis=1, ddof=0).mean(), 3))
print("average variance, divide by n − 1:", round(samples.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean(), 3))

Output:

p with the highest likelihood: 0.7
average variance, divide by n:     3.21
average variance, divide by n − 1: 4.012

The MLE isn't always unbiased. For a normal distribution, the MLE of the variance divides by $n$. Across 100,000 samples of size 5 from a distribution with true variance 4, it averages 3.21, close to the theoretical $\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 = \frac45 \times 4 = 3.2$. Dividing by $n - 1$ instead fixes the bias and averages 4.012. That's why sample variance uses $n - 1$, as this lesson mentioned.

Bayesian estimation, priors and MAP

MLE uses only the data. With just 10 flips, should we really believe $p = 0.7$? Bayesian estimation uses Bayes' theorem to combine a prior belief with the likelihood, giving a posterior distribution over $p$:

$$\text{posterior}(p) \propto \text{likelihood}(\text{data} \mid p) \times \text{prior}(p)$$

For a probability, a convenient prior is the Beta($a, b$) distribution, which you can read as "$a$ imaginary heads and $b$ imaginary tails seen before". Beta($2, 2$) is a mild belief that the coin is probably near fair. After seeing 7 heads and 3 tails, the posterior is simply Beta($2+7,\ 2+3$) = Beta($9, 5$). When the prior and posterior come from the same family like this, the prior is called conjugate.

# Bayesian updating: prior Beta(2, 2), then observe 7 heads and 3 tails
a, b = 2 + 7, 2 + 3
post = stats.beta(a, b)
print(f"posterior: Beta({a}, {b})")
print("posterior mean:", round(post.mean(), 3), " MAP (most likely value):", round((a - 1) / (a + b - 2), 3))
print("95% credible interval:", np.round(post.ppf([0.025, 0.975]), 3))

Output:

posterior: Beta(9, 5)
posterior mean: 0.643  MAP (most likely value): 0.667
95% credible interval: [0.386 0.861]

Three summaries of the posterior: its mean is $9/14 \approx 0.643$, pulled from 0.7 towards 0.5 by the prior. Its peak, the MAP estimate (maximum a posteriori), is $\frac{9-1}{9+5-2} \approx 0.667$. And a 95% credible interval runs from 0.386 to 0.861. That wide interval is honest: 10 flips really don't tell you much. With 1,000 flips the data would overwhelm the prior.

MAP is regularization in disguise

Taking logs, MAP maximises $\log(\text{likelihood}) + \log(\text{prior})$. For linear regression with a normal prior on the weights, $\log(\text{prior}) = -\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \text{const}$. That's exactly ridge regression. A Laplace (double-exponential) prior gives $-\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1$, which is lasso. So the regularization in Lesson 10 is the same thing as believing, before seeing data, that weights are probably small.

The central limit theorem and the standard error

Take any variable with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ (it doesn't need to be normal), and average $n$ independent values of it. The central limit theorem (CLT) says that for large $n$ the average is approximately normal, with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation

$$\text{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

That's the standard error of the mean. Below, we average values from an exponential distribution, which is strongly skewed (a long tail to the right) with $\sigma = 1$:

# Central limit theorem: averages of a skewed variable become bell-shaped
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for n in [1, 5, 30, 200]:
    means = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=(50000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    print(f"n {n:3d}: skewness {stats.skew(means):5.2f}   spread of the means {means.std():.3f}   1/sqrt(n) {1 / np.sqrt(n):.3f}")

Output:

n   1: skewness  1.98   spread of the means 0.997   1/sqrt(n) 1.000
n   5: skewness  0.90   spread of the means 0.445   1/sqrt(n) 0.447
n  30: skewness  0.37   spread of the means 0.182   1/sqrt(n) 0.183
n 200: skewness  0.15   spread of the means 0.071   1/sqrt(n) 0.071

Single values have skewness 1.98, far from the bell curve's 0. Averages of 30 have skewness 0.37, and averages of 200 have just 0.15. The spread of the averages matches $1/\sqrt{n}$ almost exactly. Notice the square root: to halve the error bar you need four times as much data. This is why test-set accuracy, cross-validation scores and A/B test results have predictable error bars.

Confidence intervals and the bootstrap

A 95% confidence interval comes from a method that, used on many datasets, captures the true value 95% of the time. For a mean, the CLT gives one directly: $\bar{x} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$. But what about a median, a correlation, or a model's F1 score, where there's no simple formula?

The bootstrap answers with brute force. Treat your sample as a stand-in for the population: draw a new sample of the same size from it with replacement, compute the statistic, and repeat thousands of times. The spread of those results shows how much the statistic would vary between samples. The middle 95% of them is a confidence interval.

# Bootstrap: a confidence interval for the median house price (simulated, lakh)
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
prices = rng.lognormal(mean=4.2, sigma=0.5, size=60)
boot = [np.median(rng.choice(prices, size=len(prices), replace=True)) for _ in range(10000)]
print("sample median:", round(np.median(prices), 1))
print("95% bootstrap interval:", np.round(np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5]), 1))

Output:

sample median: 72.6
95% bootstrap interval: [58.  78.7]

For 60 simulated house prices, the median is 72.6 lakh, with a 95% bootstrap interval of 58.0 to 78.7. The interval isn't symmetric around the median, because prices are skewed; the bootstrap captures that automatically. Bagging and random forests (Lesson 14) use exactly this resampling idea to build many different trees.

Hypothesis tests and A/B testing

A company tests a new checkout page: 2,400 visitors see the old page (A) and 2,400 see the new one (B). B converts at 6.5% against A's 5.0%. Is B really better, or was it luck? A hypothesis test makes this precise:

  1. State the null hypothesis $H_0$: the pages convert equally well.
  2. Compute a test statistic that measures the difference in standard-error units. For two proportions, using the pooled rate $\hat{p}$: $$z = \frac{\hat{p}_B - \hat{p}_A}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_A} + \frac{1}{n_B}\right)}}$$
  3. The p-value is the probability of a difference at least this large if $H_0$ were true. If it's below a threshold $\alpha$ chosen in advance (usually 0.05), reject $H_0$.
# A/B test: did the new checkout page raise the conversion rate?
conv = np.array([120, 156]); n = np.array([2400, 2400])
p1, p2 = conv / n
p_pool = conv.sum() / n.sum()
se = np.sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (1 / n[0] + 1 / n[1]))
z = (p2 - p1) / se
print(f"conversion: A {p1:.3f}, B {p2:.3f}   z {z:.2f}   two-sided p-value {2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z)):.4f}")

Output:

conversion: A 0.050, B 0.065   z 2.23   two-sided p-value 0.0256

$z = 2.23$ and $p = 0.0256$: a difference this big would happen by luck less than 3% of the time if the pages were equal, so at $\alpha = 0.05$ we conclude B is better. Two kinds of mistake are possible. A type I error (false positive) means declaring a difference that isn't real; its rate is $\alpha$. A type II error (false negative) means missing a real difference. The power of a test, $1 - P(\text{type II})$, is the chance of detecting a real effect. It grows with sample size, which is why A/B tests are planned with a minimum number of visitors before they start, and must not be stopped early as soon as $p$ dips below 0.05.

Multiple testing: why "significant" findings often vanish

Test 20 features that have no real effect, each at $\alpha = 0.05$, and the chance that at least one looks significant is $1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 0.64$:

# Multiple testing: 20 features with no real effect, each tested at the 5% level, in 5,000 experiments
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
groups = rng.normal(size=(5000, 20, 2, 30))              # two groups of 30, no true difference
p = stats.ttest_ind(groups[:, :, 0], groups[:, :, 1], axis=2).pvalue
print("share of single tests below 0.05:", round((p < 0.05).mean(), 3))
print("share of experiments with at least one 'significant' feature:", round((p < 0.05).any(axis=1).mean(), 3))
print("theory, 1 − 0.95^20:", round(1 - 0.95**20, 3))
print("with Bonferroni (p < 0.05/20):", round((p < 0.05 / 20).any(axis=1).mean(), 3))

Output:

share of single tests below 0.05: 0.05
share of experiments with at least one 'significant' feature: 0.643
theory, 1 − 0.95^20: 0.642
with Bonferroni (p < 0.05/20): 0.04

Each single test is fine: 5% false positives, as promised. But 64% of experiments "find" something. Trying many features, metrics, user segments or model variants and reporting only the winner is called p-hacking or the "garden of forking paths". It's a big reason why published results fail to replicate. The simplest fix, the Bonferroni correction, uses $\alpha/m$ for each of $m$ tests, bringing the overall false-alarm rate back under 5% (0.04 here). A less strict alternative that is popular when testing many features, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, controls the false discovery rate instead. In ML the same trap appears as overfitting to the validation set when you try many models on it, which is why a final untouched test set matters (Lesson 9).

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

What's the difference between maximum likelihood (MLE) and maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation?

The trap: Saying they always give the same answer.

MLE chooses the parameters that make the observed data most probable. MAP maximises likelihood × prior, so it also uses a belief about the parameters held before seeing the data. They agree with a flat prior or when there's lots of data. With little data, MAP is pulled towards the prior. MAP with a Gaussian prior is ridge regression, and with a Laplace prior it is lasso.

What does the central limit theorem actually say, and what doesn't it say?

The trap: "With enough data, everything becomes normally distributed."

It's about averages (and sums) of many independent values with finite variance: their distribution approaches a normal with standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. It does not make the data itself normal: 10,000 incomes are still skewed. It also needs independence and finite variance, and convergence is slow for very skewed or heavy-tailed data. It's why error bars on averages, test accuracy and A/B results work.

How does the bootstrap work, and when would you use it?

The trap: Describing it as "making more data".

Resample your dataset with replacement to the same size, recompute the statistic, and repeat thousands of times. The spread of the results estimates the statistic's sampling variability. The middle 95% gives a confidence interval. Use it when no simple formula exists: medians, percentiles, ratios, AUC or F1 on a test set. It doesn't create information. With a tiny or unrepresentative sample, the intervals are unreliable too.

Your A/B test reaches $p < 0.05$ on day 3 of a planned 14-day test. Can you stop and ship?

The trap: "Yes, it's significant."

No. Checking repeatedly and stopping at the first significant result (peeking) inflates the false-positive rate far above 5%, because random fluctuations cross the threshold at some point quite often. Run for the planned sample size, which should come from a power calculation, or use a method designed for continuous monitoring (sequential tests or Bayesian approaches). Also watch for weekday/weekend effects, which a 3-day window can't average out.

You tried 50 model variants and the best one beats the baseline with $p = 0.03$. How much do you trust it?

The trap: Trusting the p-value as if it were a single planned test.

Not much. With 50 tries, a few will look significant at the 5% level by chance alone (expected about 2.5). Correct for the number of comparisons (Bonferroni: $0.05/50 = 0.001$), or better, confirm on a fresh test set that wasn't used for choosing. Choosing a model on a validation set and reporting that same score is the ML version of this mistake.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. A coin shows 12 heads in 20 flips. What is the maximum-likelihood estimate of $p$?

For yes/no data the MLE is the observed fraction: $12/20 = 0.6$.

2. You start with a Beta(1, 1) prior and see 3 heads and 1 tail. What is the posterior?

Add the heads to $a$ and the tails to $b$: Beta($1+3$, $1+1$).

3. To halve the standard error of an average, you need…

SE $= \sigma/\sqrt{n}$, so halving it needs $\sqrt{n}$ to double, which means $n$ times 4.

4. MAP estimation of linear-regression weights with a normal (Gaussian) prior is the same as…

The log of a normal prior is $-\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$, the ridge penalty. A Laplace prior gives lasso.

5. You test 10 independent features with no real effect, each at $\alpha = 0.05$. The chance of at least one false positive is about…

$1 - 0.95^{10} \approx 0.401$.
