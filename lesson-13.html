Before you start

This lesson builds on:

  • Lesson 5: Data, Features & Generalization — Always judge a model on data it hasn't seen: split into train / validation / test, or use k-fold cross-validation. Overfitting = low training error but high test error. Never let test data leak into training.
  • Lesson 9: Evaluating Models — Accuracy can mislead. Precision $= TP/(TP+FP)$, recall $= TP/(TP+FN)$, and F1 balances them. Moving the threshold trades one for the other; ROC-AUC summarises all thresholds.

Where this fits: A completely different kind of model — questions instead of equations. Trees are the building blocks of the ensembles in Lesson 14.

1.Twenty questions with data

Remember the game "twenty questions"? You find an object by asking yes/no questions — "Is it alive?", "Is it bigger than a cat?" — each one narrowing down the possibilities. A decision tree classifies data the same way: it asks a sequence of simple questions about the features, like "Is petal width ≤ 0.8 cm?", until it reaches an answer.

Trees are one of the most popular model families in practice. A single tree is easy to read and explain; combined into forests and boosted ensembles (Lesson 14), trees are often the strongest models available for tabular data — spreadsheets and databases.

2.Anatomy of a tree

  • The root node at the top holds all the training data and asks the first question.
  • Each internal node tests one feature against a threshold: "$x_j \le t$?". Yes goes left, no goes right.
  • Each leaf at the bottom gives a prediction: the majority class of the training examples that ended up there (or their average value, for regression).
  • The depth is the number of questions on the longest path from root to leaf.

Here is a real depth-2 tree trained by scikit-learn on the Iris flower dataset (3 species, 4 measurements):

|--- petal width (cm) <= 0.80
|   |--- class: 0              (setosa)
|--- petal width (cm) >  0.80
|   |--- petal width (cm) <= 1.75
|   |   |--- class: 1          (versicolor)
|   |--- petal width (cm) >  1.75
|   |   |--- class: 2          (virginica)

To classify a flower with petal width 1.2 cm: is $1.2 \le 0.8$? No → go right. Is $1.2 \le 1.75$? Yes → versicolor. Anyone can follow that — a big advantage when decisions must be explained.

What the tree does to the feature space

Each question cuts the space with a straight line parallel to an axis. The tree chops the space into rectangles (boxes, in more dimensions), and each box gets one prediction. So decision boundaries look like staircases made of horizontal and vertical segments. You'll see this in the demo.

3.How does a tree choose its questions? Impurity

Picture itSorting laundry: a pile of only white shirts is "pure" — you know exactly what to do with it. A pile of mixed colours is "impure". A decision tree keeps asking questions until its piles are as pure as possible.

A good question separates the classes: after splitting, each side should be as "pure" as possible — ideally containing just one class. We need a number that measures impurity. Let $p_k$ be the fraction of examples in a node that belong to class $k$.

Gini impurity — the probability of mislabelling a random example if you labelled it randomly according to the node's class mix:

$$G = \sum_k p_k(1 - p_k) = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2$$

Entropy — from information theory, the average number of yes/no questions (bits) needed to identify the class:

$$H = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k$$

Both are 0 for a pure node and largest when classes are evenly mixed. For two classes the maximum Gini is 0.5 and the maximum entropy is 1 bit.

Worked example — impurity of a node

A node has 10 examples: 6 "yes", 4 "no". So $p_{\text{yes}} = 0.6$, $p_{\text{no}} = 0.4$.

Gini: $1 - (0.6^2 + 0.4^2) = 1 - (0.36 + 0.16) = 0.48$.

Entropy: $-(0.6\log_2 0.6 + 0.4\log_2 0.4) = -(0.6\times(-0.737) + 0.4\times(-1.322)) \approx 0.971$ bits.

A pure node (10 yes, 0 no): Gini $= 1 - 1^2 = 0$, entropy $= -1\cdot\log_2 1 = 0$.

Make sense of it

The situation: A bank sorts loan applicants into groups. A group where everyone repaid is easy to act on; a 50/50 group isn't. Impurity measures that mix.

What the result tells you: Gini 0.48 means: pick two people at random from this node and there's a 48% chance they have different labels. Entropy 0.971 bits says you'd need almost one yes/no question to learn the label.

What if…?

  • 5 yes, 5 no: Gini 0.5, entropy 1 bit — the worst possible mix for two classes.
  • 9 yes, 1 no: Gini 0.18, entropy 0.47 — nearly pure.
  • Three classes, equally mixed: Gini 0.667, entropy 1.585 bits — more classes, more uncertainty.

Why it matters in ML: A tree's whole job is to make its leaves pure. Impurity is the score it uses to choose every question it asks.

4.Scoring a split: information gain

A split sends $n_L$ examples left and $n_R$ right. Its quality is the weighted impurity of the two children, weighted by how many examples each gets:

$$G_{\text{split}} = \frac{n_L}{n}\,G(\text{left}) + \frac{n_R}{n}\,G(\text{right})$$

The improvement over the parent is the impurity decrease — called information gain when using entropy:

$$\text{Gain} = G(\text{parent}) - G_{\text{split}}$$
Worked example — compare two candidate splits

Parent: 6 yes / 4 no, Gini 0.48 (from above).

Split A sends 4 examples left (4 yes, 0 no) and 6 right (2 yes, 4 no).

  • Left Gini $= 0$ (pure).
  • Right Gini $= 1 - \big((2/6)^2 + (4/6)^2\big) = 1 - (0.111 + 0.444) \approx 0.444$.
  • Weighted: $\frac{4}{10}\times0 + \frac{6}{10}\times0.444 \approx 0.267$. Gain $= 0.48 - 0.267 = 0.213$.

Split B sends 5 left (3 yes, 2 no) and 5 right (3 yes, 2 no).

  • Both children have the same 60/40 mix as the parent: Gini 0.48 each. Weighted: 0.48. Gain $= 0$.

Split B is useless — it doesn't separate anything. Split A wins. (With entropy, split A's information gain is $0.971 - 0.6\times0.918 \approx 0.420$ bits; the ranking is the same.)

Make sense of it

The situation: The tree has to pick its first question. Each candidate split divides the 10 applicants differently.

What the result tells you: Split A creates one perfectly pure group and cuts impurity from 0.48 to 0.267 — a gain of 0.213. Split B leaves both groups as mixed as before, so it's worthless.

What if…?

  • A perfect split (6 yes on one side, 4 no on the other): both children are pure and the gain is the full 0.48.
  • Peeling off one example (1 yes alone, 5 yes + 4 no together): a pure leaf, but the gain is only 0.036. Tiny pure leaves look tempting and help little — that's how deep trees overfit.

Why it matters in ML: Trees choose splits greedily, one at a time, by the biggest gain. Limiting depth or requiring a minimum number of samples per leaf stops them chasing tiny pure leaves.

Advanced · optionalWhy a split never increases weighted impurity

Gini impurity $G(\mathbf{p}) = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2$ and entropy $H(\mathbf{p}) = -\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k$ are concave functions of the class proportions $\mathbf{p}$. A split sends a fraction $w_L$ of a node's examples left and $w_R = 1-w_L$ right, and the parent's proportions are the weighted average $\mathbf{p} = w_L\mathbf{p}_L + w_R\mathbf{p}_R$. Concavity (Jensen's inequality) gives

$$G(w_L\mathbf{p}_L + w_R\mathbf{p}_R) \;\ge\; w_L G(\mathbf{p}_L) + w_R G(\mathbf{p}_R),$$

so the information gain is always ≥ 0, and it is 0 only when both children have the same class mix as the parent. That's why a tree keeps finding "useful" splits all the way down to pure leaves — and why it must be stopped deliberately (Lesson 13's overfitting section).

5.Growing the tree: the CART algorithm

scikit-learn builds trees with an optimised version of CART (Classification And Regression Trees). It's greedy and recursive:

  1. At the current node, try every feature, and for each feature every possible threshold (the midpoints between sorted values).
  2. Pick the feature and threshold with the largest impurity decrease.
  3. Split the data into left and right children.
  4. Repeat on each child, until a stopping rule is met (node is pure, maximum depth reached, too few examples to split…).

"Greedy" means it picks the best split right now, without looking ahead. That's not guaranteed to find the best possible tree (that problem is far too hard to solve exactly), but it works very well in practice.

7.Trees for regression

For predicting numbers, a leaf predicts the average $y$ of its training examples, and impurity is measured by the variance (equivalently the MSE) within the node. A split is good if it creates groups whose $y$ values are tightly clustered. The result is a step function: flat predictions within each box, with jumps between boxes.

8.Trees overfit easily — and how to stop them

In plain wordsTrees keep splitting until they memorise the training data. Limit their depth or the minimum examples per leaf, or prune them after growing — and choose those settings with cross-validation.

Left alone, a tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure — it can memorise the training set perfectly, noise included. Ways to control it (all are hyperparameters, chosen by cross-validation):

  • max_depth — limit the number of questions.
  • min_samples_split / min_samples_leaf — don't split tiny groups; every leaf must contain at least this many examples.
  • max_leaf_nodes — cap the total number of leaves.
  • Cost-complexity pruning (ccp_alpha) — grow a full tree, then cut back branches that don't earn their keep. It minimises
$$R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\,|T|$$

where $R(T)$ is the tree's training error (impurity), $|T|$ is its number of leaves, and $\alpha$ charges a price per leaf — the same "fit + penalty" idea as regularization in Lesson 10.

9.Feature importance

Each split reduces impurity. Add up those reductions for each feature (weighted by the number of examples reaching the node) and normalise so they sum to 1: that's the tree's feature importance. It tells you which features the tree relied on most.

Read importances with care

Impurity-based importance favours features with many distinct values (like IDs or continuous measurements), and when two features are correlated, the tree may use just one, making the other look unimportant. It describes the model, not causation.

11.In Python

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

data = load_breast_cancer()
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    data.data, data.target, test_size=0.25, stratify=data.target, random_state=0)

for depth in [1, 2, 3, 5, None]:          # None = grow until leaves are pure
    tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=depth, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(f"max_depth={str(depth):4s} depth={tree.get_depth()} leaves={tree.get_n_leaves():2d} "
          f"train={tree.score(X_train, y_train):.3f} test={tree.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}")

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
top = sorted(zip(tree.feature_importances_, data.feature_names), reverse=True)[:3]
print([(str(name), round(float(imp), 3)) for imp, name in top])

Where this is used
  • Business rules that must be explainable (e.g. eligibility decisions)
  • Quick exploration of which features matter
  • The building block of random forests and boosting

Choose it when: You need a readable model, or features are a mix of numbers and categories.

Think twice when: You need top accuracy from a single model — use an ensemble instead.

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythFeature importance shows which features cause the outcome.

RealityIt shows what the tree used for splitting — biased towards features with many values, and silent about correlated alternatives.

MythA deeper tree is a better tree.

RealityDeeper trees fit training data better but overfit; depth must be tuned.

Responsible AI · Transparent, but not automatically fair

A small tree's rules can be read and audited, which is a real advantage for accountability. But readable is not the same as fair: a split on a proxy feature (such as a postcode) can encode discrimination plainly in the rules. Audit the splits, not just the accuracy.

13.Questions beginners often ask

Gini or entropy?

They usually give very similar trees. Gini is scikit-learn's default and slightly faster to compute.

Why don't trees need scaling?

Each split compares one feature with a threshold, so changing a feature's units just changes the threshold.

How deep should a tree be?

Choose max_depth (or min_samples_leaf) by cross-validation; shallow trees for explanation, deeper ones inside ensembles.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Letting trees grow without limits and trusting their 100% training accuracy.
  • Scaling features for trees (harmless, but unnecessary).
  • Reading impurity-based feature importance as causal, or as fair between correlated features.
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • Gini impurity vs entropy — Two rulers for the same mess.
  • Pre-pruning vs post-pruning — Stop early vs trim afterwards.
  • Impurity importance vs permutation importance — Credit claimed in training vs damage done when removed.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

Do decision trees need feature scaling?

The trap: Scaling everything by habit.

No. A split asks "is $x \le t$?", and rescaling $x$ just rescales $t$; the tree is identical. The same goes for random forests and gradient-boosted trees.

A regression tree is trained on $x = 0, \dots, 9$ with $y = 2x$. What does it predict for $x = 100$?

The trap: Expecting 200.

18 — the value of the last leaf. Trees predict constants inside regions, so they can't extrapolate beyond the training range. Random forests and boosted trees share this limitation; linear models don't.

Why do fully grown decision trees overfit?

The trap: "Trees are just bad."

Without limits a tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure, often isolating single noisy examples. Limit depth or leaf size, prune, or average many trees (a random forest).

Gini or entropy — does the choice matter much?

The trap: Spending a long time tuning it.

Rarely. They rank splits almost identically (in Lesson 13's example both preferred split A). Gini is slightly faster to compute. Depth and leaf-size limits matter far more.

14.Key takeaways

  • A decision tree asks a sequence of "feature ≤ threshold?" questions; leaves hold predictions.
  • Splits are chosen to reduce impurity: Gini $1 - \sum p_k^2$ or entropy $-\sum p_k\log_2 p_k$.
  • The CART algorithm greedily picks the split with the largest impurity decrease, then repeats on each child.
  • Unrestricted trees overfit; control them with max_depth, min_samples_leaf or pruning (ccp_alpha).
  • Trees are readable and need no scaling, but are unstable — which ensembles fix.

15.Check your understanding

1. A node has 5 examples of class A and 5 of class B. What is its Gini impurity?

$1 - (0.5^2 + 0.5^2) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5$ — the maximum for two classes.

2. Which split is better: one that creates two pure children, or one whose children have the same class mix as the parent?

Pure children have impurity 0 — the maximum possible gain. Keeping the parent's mix gives zero gain.

3. Why don't decision trees need feature scaling?

Rescaling a feature just rescales its thresholds; the same examples go left and right.

4. A tree has 100% training accuracy and 85% test accuracy. What would most likely help?

That gap is overfitting; restricting the tree's growth reduces variance.

5. What shape are a decision tree's decision boundaries?

Each split is "one feature ≤ threshold", a cut parallel to an axis. Together they make rectangles.

16.Going deeper: pruning, honest importances and instability

In plain wordsHow to cut a tree back properly (cost-complexity pruning, chosen by cross-validation), why the feature importances that tree libraries print can rank pure noise highly (use permutation importance instead), and why a small change in the data can produce a completely different tree, which is the weakness random forests fix.

Intermediate · skip on a first read

The lesson above grows a tree and limits its depth. This section covers the principled way to shrink a tree (cost-complexity pruning, chosen by cross-validation), why the feature importances printed by tree libraries can mislead you (and the honest alternative), and the trees' biggest weakness, instability, which is exactly what random forests and boosting (Lesson 14) are designed to fix.

You'll need: this lesson, cross-validation from Lesson 5 and the bootstrap from Lesson 4's "Going deeper".

Cost-complexity pruning

Limits like max_depth stop growth early, but a split that looks useless can enable a very useful split below it. Pruning grows the full tree first, then cuts back. Cost-complexity pruning scores a tree $T$ by its training error plus a price per leaf:

$$R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\,|\text{leaves}(T)|$$

For each $\alpha$ there's a best subtree. As $\alpha$ grows, branches whose improvement isn't worth their extra leaves are removed one at a time ("weakest-link" pruning), giving a nested sequence of smaller and smaller trees. Scikit-learn computes the whole sequence:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0, stratify=y)
path = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).cost_complexity_pruning_path(Xtr, ytr)
print(" ccp_alpha   leaves   train acc   test acc")
for a in path.ccp_alphas[::3]:
    t = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0, ccp_alpha=a).fit(Xtr, ytr)
    print(f"{a:9.4f}   {t.get_n_leaves():6d}   {t.score(Xtr, ytr):9.3f}   {t.score(Xte, yte):8.3f}")

Output:

 ccp_alpha   leaves   train acc   test acc
   0.0000       16       1.000      0.906
   0.0042       11       0.992      0.912
   0.0088        6       0.977      0.918
   0.0259        3       0.932      0.889

The full tree (16 leaves) fits the training data perfectly but scores 0.906 on the test set. Pruning to 6 leaves gives up a little training accuracy and improves the test score to 0.918; pruning to 3 leaves goes too far (0.889). It's the bias–variance trade-off again (Lesson 10), with $\alpha$ as the knob. Choose $\alpha$ by cross-validation on the training set only:

from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
search = GridSearchCV(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0), {"ccp_alpha": path.ccp_alphas}, cv=5).fit(Xtr, ytr)
best = search.best_estimator_
print("alpha chosen by CV:", round(search.best_params_["ccp_alpha"], 4), " leaves:", best.get_n_leaves(), " test accuracy:", round(best.score(Xte, yte), 3))

Output:

alpha chosen by CV: 0.0088  leaves: 6  test accuracy: 0.918

Cross-validation picks $\alpha \approx 0.0088$, the 6-leaf tree, which scores 0.918 on the untouched test set.

Feature importance: impurity vs permutation

The default feature_importances_ adds up how much each feature reduced impurity across all splits (the "mean decrease in impurity"). Two problems:

  • It's measured on the training data, so splits that only memorise noise still earn credit.
  • It's biased towards features with many distinct values (continuous numbers, IDs), because they offer many possible split points and some will fit noise well by chance.

Permutation importance fixes both. On held-out data, shuffle one feature's column, breaking its link to the target, and measure how much the score drops. A feature the model truly relies on causes a big drop; a useless one causes none. Here we add a column of pure noise with a different value on every row, like a customer ID:

from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

# Add a column of pure noise with many distinct values (like an ID number)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Xn_tr = np.column_stack([Xtr, rng.permutation(len(ytr))])
Xn_te = np.column_stack([Xte, rng.permutation(len(yte))])
tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(Xn_tr, ytr)
imp = tree.feature_importances_
perm = permutation_importance(tree, Xn_te, yte, n_repeats=30, random_state=0).importances_mean
print("noise column: impurity importance", round(imp[-1], 3), " rank", int((imp > imp[-1]).sum()) + 1, "of 31")
print("noise column: permutation importance on test data", round(perm[-1], 3))

Output:

noise column: impurity importance 0.011  rank 6 of 31
noise column: permutation importance on test data 0.0

Impurity importance ranks the meaningless noise column 6th of 31, ahead of 25 real medical measurements. Permutation importance on the test data gives it exactly 0. Use permutation importance (or SHAP values) when you report which features matter. Two further cautions: when features are correlated, shuffling one lets the model lean on its partner, so both can look unimportant; and importance means "the model uses it", not "it causes the outcome".

Trees are unstable

Because each split is chosen greedily and every later split depends on it, a small change in the data can change the top split, and with it the whole tree. Here we grow a tree on 100 bootstrap samples of the same training data and record the feature used at the root:

# Instability: which feature does the root split use on bootstrap samples?
names = load_breast_cancer().feature_names
roots = {}
for _ in range(100):
    i = rng.integers(0, len(ytr), len(ytr))
    r = names[DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(Xtr[i], ytr[i]).tree_.feature[0]]
    roots[str(r)] = roots.get(str(r), 0) + 1
print(sorted(roots.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]))

Output:

[('worst perimeter', 52), ('worst concave points', 30), ('worst radius', 8), ('mean concave points', 7), ('worst area', 3)]

Five different features end up at the root: "worst perimeter" 52 times, "worst concave points" 30 times, and three others. Each tree tells a different story, so explaining a decision by reading a single tree's structure is fragile. This high variance is the weakness that bagging and random forests (Lesson 14) exploit: average many unstable trees and the variance cancels, while keeping their low bias.

Practical details worth knowing

  • Regression trees choose splits that most reduce the squared error, and predict the average of each leaf. They can't extrapolate beyond the training range (Lesson 5's "Going deeper" showed this with a forest).
  • Categorical features. Scikit-learn trees need numbers; one-hot encoding works, though very many categories make splits weak. LightGBM and CatBoost can split categories directly.
  • Missing values. Recent scikit-learn trees can learn which side missing values should go to; gradient-boosting libraries have done this for years.
  • Finding the best tree is hard. Finding the smallest accurate tree is NP-hard, which is why every library grows trees greedily, one split at a time.
Interview corner · intermediate

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

How would you prune a decision tree, and how do you choose how much?

The trap: Only mentioning max_depth.

Grow the full tree, then apply cost-complexity (weakest-link) pruning: minimise training error + $\alpha$ × number of leaves. Increasing $\alpha$ gives a nested sequence of subtrees. Pick $\alpha$ by cross-validation (often the simplest tree within one standard error of the best score). Pre-pruning limits (max_depth, min_samples_leaf) are cheaper alternatives, but they can stop before a useful deeper split.

Your stakeholder asks which features drive the model. What do you report, and what caveats do you give?

The trap: Pasting the default feature_importances_ bar chart.

Use permutation importance on held-out data (or SHAP values for per-prediction explanations), with error bars from repeats. Caveats: correlated features share or hide importance, so group them; importance describes what the model uses, not what causes the outcome; and results depend on the model, so a different good model might rank features differently.

Why do people say random forests fix the weaknesses of decision trees?

The trap: "Because more trees is better."

A deep tree has low bias but high variance: small data changes give very different trees. Averaging many trees trained on bootstrap samples (bagging), with random feature subsets at each split to make them less correlated, cancels much of that variance while keeping low bias. The price is interpretability and some speed.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. In cost-complexity pruning, what does a larger $\alpha$ do?

$R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha|\text{leaves}|$: a higher price per leaf favours smaller trees.

2. Why can impurity-based importance rank a random ID column highly?

It's measured on training data and favours high-cardinality features.

3. Permutation importance measures…

Shuffling breaks the feature's link to the target; a useful feature's absence hurts the score.

4. Two strongly correlated features are both useful. What can permutation importance show for each?

Shuffling one leaves the other, so the score barely drops. Group correlated features or use other methods.

5. Why are single decision trees unstable?

This high variance is exactly what bagging and random forests average away.
