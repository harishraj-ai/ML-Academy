Before you start

This lesson builds on:

  • Lesson 2: Vectors & Matrices — A vector is a list of numbers; a dataset $X$ is a matrix with one row per example. The dot product $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} = \sum w_ix_i$ is how linear models predict, and $\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|$ is the distance between two examples.
  • Lesson 4: Probability & Statistics — Probabilities run from 0 to 1. Bayes' theorem turns $P(B\mid A)$ into $P(A\mid B)$. Mean and standard deviation summarise data; the z-score $(x-\mu)/\sigma$ standardises it.
  • Lesson 5: Data, Features & Generalization — Always judge a model on data it hasn't seen: split into train / validation / test, or use k-fold cross-validation. Overfitting = low training error but high test error. Never let test data leak into training.

Where this fits: Starts Part 3, the classic algorithm toolbox. kNN is the simplest one — no training at all — and highlights why scaling matters.

1.The simplest idea in machine learning

Picture itNew to a city and wondering whether a restaurant is any good? Ask the three people who live closest to it. That's k-nearest neighbours: no formula, just "you're probably like the examples you're near".

"Tell me who your neighbours are, and I'll tell you who you are." That proverb is the k-nearest neighbours algorithm (kNN). To classify a new example, find the $k$ training examples most similar to it and let them vote.

kNN has no equations to fit and no weights to learn — and yet it can draw curved, complex decision boundaries that linear models can't. It's a great way to understand the idea of similarity, which runs through all of ML, and it teaches two crucial lessons: the importance of feature scaling and the curse of dimensionality.

Analogy: estimating a house price

An estate agent valuing a house doesn't solve equations. They look at the 3–5 most similar houses that sold recently nearby (similar size, area, condition) and take something like their average price. That's kNN regression.

2.The algorithm

Training: just store all the training examples. That's it. (kNN is called a lazy learner, because all the work happens at prediction time.)

Predicting for a new point $\mathbf{q}$:

  1. Compute the distance from $\mathbf{q}$ to every training example.
  2. Pick the $k$ examples with the smallest distances — the nearest neighbours.
  3. Classification: predict the most common class among those $k$ (majority vote). Regression: predict the average of their $y$ values.

kNN is non-parametric: it doesn't summarise the data in a fixed set of weights. The training data itself is the model.

3.Measuring distance

"Nearest" needs a distance. The most common is the Euclidean distance (straight-line distance, from Pythagoras — Lesson 2):

$$d(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}(a_j - b_j)^2}$$

Alternatives:

  • Manhattan distance $\sum_j |a_j - b_j|$ — like walking along city blocks; less dominated by one big difference.
  • Minkowski distance $\big(\sum_j |a_j - b_j|^p\big)^{1/p}$ — the general family: $p = 1$ is Manhattan, $p = 2$ is Euclidean. scikit-learn's default is Minkowski with $p = 2$.
  • Cosine distance — based on the angle between vectors (Lesson 2), popular for text.

  • $k = 1$: nearest is B → red.
  • $k = 3$: B, A, E → 2 red, 1 blue → red.
  • $k = 5$: all five → 2 red, 3 blue → blue.

The answer depends on $k$. With $k = 5$ we're just counting every point, so the larger class wins regardless of where $\mathbf{q}$ is.

Make sense of it

The situation: A new customer, $\mathbf{q} = (2, 2)$, arrives. The two red points are customers who left; the three blue ones stayed. Will this one leave?

What the result tells you: kNN copies its nearest neighbours. With $k = 1$ or $3$ the closest customers are mostly red, so it predicts red. With $k = 5$ it asks everyone, and the majority (blue) wins wherever $\mathbf{q}$ is.

What if…?

  • Move the customer to (3.5, 3): the three nearest are all blue, so every $k$ from 1 to 5 says blue.
  • Move to (3, 2): the nearest red and the nearest blue are both exactly 1.414 away — a tie that the order of the data has to break.
  • Weight votes by 1/distance with $k = 5$: red scores $1/1 + 1/1.414 = 1.71$, blue scores 1.28, so close neighbours win and the answer is red again.

Why it matters in ML: $k$ controls how local the model is: small $k$ follows every quirk of the data (overfitting); large $k$ smooths everything towards the majority (underfitting). Pick it by cross-validation.

4.Choosing $k$: bias vs variance again

  • Small $k$ (e.g. 1): the boundary follows every single training point, including noisy or mislabelled ones. Jagged boundary, little islands of the wrong class. High variance → overfitting. Note that with $k = 1$, training accuracy is always 100% — each point is its own nearest neighbour — which tells you nothing.
  • Large $k$: votes average over a big neighbourhood, so the boundary is smooth. Too large and it blurs real structure, eventually just predicting the majority class everywhere. High bias → underfitting.

Choose $k$ by cross-validation (Lesson 5). For two classes, odd values avoid tied votes.

Distance-weighted voting

Instead of one vote each, neighbours can vote with weight $1/d$, so closer ones count more. In scikit-learn: weights="distance". This makes the choice of $k$ less sensitive.

6.Feature scaling is essential

Distances add up differences across all features. If features are on different scales, the large-scale feature completely dominates.

Worked example — age vs income

Person P is 30 years old earning 50,000. Q is 60 earning 51,000. R is 31 earning 70,000.

$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{30^2 + 1000^2} \approx 1000.4$
$d(P, R) = \sqrt{1^2 + 20000^2} \approx 20000.0$

So Q is "much closer" to P than R is — even though Q is twice P's age — because the income differences (thousands) swamp the age differences (tens). Age is effectively ignored.

After standardising each feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (Lesson 4), a 30-year age gap and a 20,000 income gap are each measured relative to how much that feature normally varies, so both count.

Make sense of it

The situation: A dating app matches people by age and income. P is 30 earning 50,000; Q is 60 earning 51,000; R is 31 earning 70,000. Who is P's closest match?

What the result tells you: On raw numbers Q looks 20 times closer than R, purely because income is measured in thousands and age in tens. The 30-year age gap barely registers.

What if…?

  • Standardise both features (using these three people): P–Q becomes 2.16 and P–R 2.17. Now the age gap and the income gap count about equally — a very different picture.
  • Income in thousands (50, 51, 70): P–Q $= \sqrt{30^2 + 1^2} \approx 30.0$ and P–R $= \sqrt{1 + 20^2} \approx 20.0$, so R becomes the closer one. The "nearest" person depends entirely on the units.

Why it matters in ML: Every distance-based method (kNN, k-means, SVMs with RBF kernels) is sensitive to scale. Standardising is not optional for them.

The code below shows the real effect on a dataset: accuracy jumps from 67% to 93% just by scaling.

7.kNN for regression

For a numeric target, predict the average of the neighbours' values:

$$\hat{y}(\mathbf{q}) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{q})} y_i$$

where $N_k(\mathbf{q})$ is the set of the $k$ nearest neighbours. With distance weighting, it's a weighted average. Example: the three nearest houses sold for 300k, 320k and 340k, so predict 320k.

8.The curse of dimensionality

In plain wordsWith many features, data points spread out so much that even the "nearest" neighbours are far away. That's the curse of dimensionality — kNN works best with fewer, relevant features.

kNN relies on nearby points being similar. In high dimensions, "nearby" stops meaning much — this is the curse of dimensionality.

Worked example — how big is a "neighbourhood"?

Suppose data is spread evenly inside a unit cube, and we want a small cube-shaped neighbourhood that contains 10% of the data. Its volume must be 0.1, so each side has length $0.1^{1/d}$:

Dimensions $d$12310100
Side length needed0.100.320.460.790.98

In 10 dimensions, to capture just 10% of the data, the "neighbourhood" must span 79% of each feature's range. In 100 dimensions, 98%. The "nearest" neighbours aren't near at all — they're scattered almost across the whole space.

Make sense of it

The situation: You want the 10% of houses most like yours. With one feature (size) that's a narrow band. Add more features and see what happens to "nearby".

What the result tells you: In 10 dimensions, a box holding 10% of the data must stretch 79% of the way along every feature. Your "neighbours" differ from you on almost everything.

What if…?

  • Ask for only 1% of the data as neighbours: the side length is still 0.63 in 10 dimensions and 0.955 in 100.
  • To keep a side of 0.1 (truly local) in 10 dimensions, the box holds only $0.1^{10}$ — one ten-billionth of the data. You'd need billions of examples to find a single neighbour.

Why it matters in ML: This is the curse of dimensionality: with many features, data becomes sparse and distances lose their meaning. Remove irrelevant features or reduce dimensions (Lesson 17) before using kNN.

Consequences: in many dimensions, all points end up roughly the same distance from each other, so nearest neighbours become nearly meaningless, and you'd need exponentially more data to fill the space. Remedies: remove irrelevant features, or reduce dimensions first with PCA (Lesson 17).

9.Strengths and weaknesses

StrengthsWeaknesses
Simple to understand and explainSlow predictions: compares to every training point
No training timeMust store the whole training set
Flexible, curved decision boundariesNeeds feature scaling
Works for classification and regressionStruggles in high dimensions and with irrelevant features

To speed up the search, libraries use tree-shaped indexes such as KD-trees and ball trees, which skip whole regions of space that can't contain a nearest neighbour. They work well in low dimensions; scikit-learn chooses automatically (algorithm="auto").

10.In Python

The wine dataset: 178 wines, 13 chemical measurements with very different scales (one ranges up to 1,680, others are below 1), and 3 grape varieties.

from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

raw = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
print("without scaling:", round(raw.score(X_test, y_test), 3))

scaled = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)).fit(X_train, y_train)
print("with scaling:   ", round(scaled.score(X_test, y_test), 3))

for k in [1, 5, 15, 51, 81, 101]:
    s = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k)),
                        X_train, y_train, cv=5)
    print(f"k={k:3d}  CV accuracy {s.mean():.3f} ± {s.std():.3f}")

Output:

Two lessons in one output. Scaling lifts accuracy from 67% to 93%. And $k$ matters: small $k$ is noisier (look at the larger ± for $k = 1$), moderate values work well, and once $k$ approaches the size of the training folds (about 106 examples), the vote is dominated by whole classes and accuracy collapses — extreme underfitting.

11.Running example: Chennai Homes

A new 100 m², 5-year-old house 6 km from the centre: after scaling, its 3 nearest neighbours are houses 5, 4 and 2 (88, 55 and 82 lakh), so kNN predicts their average, 75 lakh.

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
cols = ["size_m2", "dist_km", "age_yrs"]
knn = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=3)).fit(df[cols], df["price_lakh"])
new = pd.DataFrame({"size_m2": [100], "dist_km": [6], "age_yrs": [5]})
dist, idx = knn[-1].kneighbors(knn[0].transform(new))
print("nearest houses:", (df["id"].iloc[idx[0]]).tolist(), "prices:", df["price_lakh"].iloc[idx[0]].tolist())
print("predicted price:", round(knn.predict(new)[0], 1), "lakh")

Output:

nearest houses: [5, 4, 2] prices: [88, 55, 82]
predicted price: 75.0 lakh
Where this is used
  • Simple recommenders ("customers similar to you bought…")
  • Filling in missing values from similar rows (KNNImputer)
  • A quick baseline for small datasets

Choose it when: Small to medium data, few features, and a meaningful notion of similarity.

Think twice when: Very large datasets (slow predictions) or many features (curse of dimensionality).

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythkNN learns a model from the data.

RealityIt just stores the data and compares at prediction time.

MythLarger k is always safer.

RealityToo large k blurs real boundaries and, in the extreme, predicts the majority class everywhere.

Responsible AI · A model that stores its data

kNN keeps every training example and uses it at prediction time, so deploying it means deploying the raw data — with all its privacy obligations, including deleting a person's record if they ask. Distances also treat every feature as meaningful: if a sensitive attribute (or a proxy) is among the features, "similar people" will be judged alike for the wrong reasons.

12.Questions beginners often ask

How do I choose k?

Try a range (e.g. 1–50) with cross-validation and pick the best. Odd numbers avoid ties for two classes.

Why is kNN slow to predict?

It compares each new point with every training example. Tree indexes (KD-tree, ball tree) speed this up in low dimensions.

Does kNN learn anything?

Not in the usual sense — it stores the data. All the work happens at prediction time, which is why it's called a lazy learner.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Running kNN on unscaled features.
  • Judging $k = 1$ by its training accuracy (always 100%).
  • Keeping many irrelevant features, which dilute the meaning of distance.
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • kNN vs k-means — kNN looks at neighbours; k-means moves centres.
  • Lazy vs eager learning — Pay now or pay later.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

What does a kNN model learn during training?

The trap: Describing weights or a boundary.

Nothing beyond storing the training data (and perhaps building a search index). It's a "lazy" learner: all the work happens at prediction time, which is why predictions get slow on large datasets and why deploying it means deploying the data.

With $k = 1$, what's the training accuracy, and what does it tell you?

The trap: Reporting it as evidence the model is good.

Essentially 100%: each training point's nearest neighbour is itself (unless identical points have different labels). It tells you nothing about generalisation — always evaluate kNN on held-out data.

Why is an odd $k$ often recommended for two-class kNN?

The trap: Not noticing ties.

With an even $k$ the vote can tie (2 vs 2). An odd $k$ avoids ties for two classes; with more classes ties can still happen, so implementations break them by distance or order.

Your kNN accuracy jumps after you standardise the features. Why?

The trap: "Scaling just helps."

Distances are dominated by features with large numeric ranges. In Lesson 11, income in units of thousands drowned out age entirely. Standardising lets every feature contribute in proportion to how much it normally varies.

13.Key takeaways

  • kNN predicts from the $k$ most similar training examples: majority vote (classification) or average (regression).
  • It needs a distance — usually Euclidean $\sqrt{\sum(a_j - b_j)^2}$.
  • Small $k$ → jagged boundary, overfitting; large $k$ → smooth boundary, underfitting. Choose $k$ by cross-validation.
  • Always scale features — otherwise large-scale features dominate the distance.
  • The curse of dimensionality: in many dimensions, neighbourhoods stop being local.

14.Check your understanding

1. What is the Euclidean distance between $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 6)$?

$\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$. (7 is the Manhattan distance.)

2. With $k = 1$, what is kNN's accuracy on its own training data (no duplicate points)?

Each training point's nearest neighbour is itself, so it's always "correct" — which says nothing about generalization.

3. Your kNN boundary is jagged with many small islands. What should you change?

Jagged boundaries mean high variance. Averaging over more neighbours smooths the boundary.

4. The 3 nearest neighbours of a house sold for 200k, 260k and 230k. What does kNN regression ($k = 3$, unweighted) predict?

The average: $(200 + 260 + 230)/3 = 230$k.

5. Why does kNN struggle with hundreds of features?

The curse of dimensionality: neighbourhoods must stretch across most of the space, so neighbours aren't really similar.
