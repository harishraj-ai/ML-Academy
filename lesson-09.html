Before you start

This lesson builds on:

  • Lesson 5: Data, Features & Generalization — Always judge a model on data it hasn't seen: split into train / validation / test, or use k-fold cross-validation. Overfitting = low training error but high test error. Never let test data leak into training.
  • Lesson 8: Logistic Regression — Logistic regression outputs a probability $p = \sigma(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b)$, is trained with log loss, and classifies with a threshold (often 0.5), giving a straight decision boundary.

Where this fits: Now that you can build classifiers, you need to judge them properly. These metrics are used in every remaining lesson and in real projects.

1.Why "accuracy" is not enough

You've trained a classifier. Is it good? The obvious answer is accuracy: the fraction of predictions that are correct. But accuracy can be badly misleading.

The accuracy trap

A disease affects 1 in 100 people. A "model" that simply says "healthy" for everyone is 99% accurate — and completely useless, because it never finds a single sick person.

Make sense of it

The situation: A hospital vendor boasts that its screening model is "99% accurate".

What the result tells you: On a disease affecting 1 in 100 people, a model that never finds anyone sick is also 99% accurate. Accuracy is dominated by the large healthy group.

What if…?

  • A model that flags everyone finds every sick person but is only 1% accurate.
  • If half the population were sick, "always healthy" would be just 50% accurate. How good an accuracy is depends on the base rate.

Why it matters in ML: Always compare a model with the trivial baseline that predicts the most common class, and use recall, precision or AUC when one class is rare.

Different mistakes have different costs. Missing a cancer is far worse than an unnecessary follow-up test; flagging a real email as spam is worse than letting one spam through. This lesson gives you the tools to see exactly which mistakes a model makes, and to measure what actually matters for your problem.

2.The confusion matrix

For a binary classifier, every prediction falls into one of four boxes:

Predicted positivePredicted negative
Actually positiveTP — true positive
correctly caught		FN — false negative
missed ("Type II error")
Actually negativeFP — false positive
false alarm ("Type I error")		TN — true negative
correctly ignored

The word after "true/false" is what the model predicted; "true" means that prediction was right. So a false positive is a predicted positive that was wrong.

Worked example — a screening test on 100 patients

10 patients are actually sick, 90 are healthy. The model flags 18 patients: 8 of them are really sick.

Predicted sickPredicted healthyTotal
Actually sickTP = 8FN = 210
Actually healthyFP = 10TN = 8090

We'll compute every metric below from these four numbers.

Make sense of it

The situation: A clinic screens 100 patients; 10 are really sick. The model flags 18 for a follow-up test.

What the result tells you: It catches 8 of the 10 sick patients (recall 0.8), but only 8 of the 18 it flags are really sick (precision 0.44). Overall accuracy is 0.88.

What if…?

  • Flag more people (30) and catch all 10: recall 1.0, but precision falls to 0.33 and accuracy to 0.80. More follow-up tests, no missed patients.
  • Flag fewer (5), of whom 4 are sick: precision 0.80, recall only 0.40 — yet accuracy rises to 0.93, because missing sick people barely dents accuracy.
  • Flag everyone: recall 1.0, precision 0.10, accuracy 0.10.

Why it matters in ML: Precision and recall pull against each other as you move the threshold. Which matters more depends on costs: missing a cancer (low recall) is worse than an extra test (low precision); for spam filters it's the other way round.

3.Accuracy, precision, recall, specificity

Picture itAn airport security scanner can be tuned to beep at everything (it never misses a threat, but delays thousands of travellers) or almost nothing (smooth queues, real danger). Precision and recall put numbers on that trade-off.

Accuracy — what fraction of all predictions were right?

$$\text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} = \frac{8 + 80}{100} = 0.88$$

Precision — of the ones we flagged, how many were really positive? ("When the model says yes, can I trust it?")

$$\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} = \frac{8}{8 + 10} \approx 0.44$$

Recall (also sensitivity or true positive rate) — of the real positives, how many did we catch? ("Does the model find what it should?")

$$\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} = \frac{8}{8 + 2} = 0.80$$

Specificity (true negative rate) — of the real negatives, how many did we correctly leave alone?

$$\text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP} = \frac{80}{90} \approx 0.89$$
Remember them with a fishing net

You cast a net to catch a particular kind of fish. Recall: what fraction of those fish in the lake ended up in your net? Precision: what fraction of the things in your net are those fish (and not old boots)? A huge net has great recall but poor precision; a tiny careful net has great precision but poor recall.

So our screening test catches 80% of sick patients (good recall), but only 44% of its alarms are real (low precision). Whether that's acceptable depends on the situation: for an initial screening followed by a more accurate test, it may be fine.

Read the formulaPrecision and recall
$$\text{Precision} = \frac{\htmlClass{fd-1}{TP}}{\htmlClass{fd-1}{TP} + \htmlClass{fd-2}{FP}} \qquad \text{Recall} = \frac{\htmlClass{fd-1}{TP}}{\htmlClass{fd-1}{TP} + \htmlClass{fd-3}{FN}}$$
  • TP — true positives — real positives we caught
  • FP — false alarms — only precision is hurt by these
  • FN — misses — only recall is hurt by these

Hover over (or tap) a part to highlight it in the formula.

4.The F1 score

To summarise precision and recall in one number, use their harmonic mean, the F1 score:

$$F_1 = 2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN}$$

For our example: $F_1 = \frac{2\cdot8}{2\cdot8 + 10 + 2} = \frac{16}{28} \approx 0.57$.

Why the harmonic mean rather than the ordinary average? It's dragged down by whichever value is smaller, so a model can't hide terrible precision behind great recall. A model with precision 1.0 and recall 0.01 has an ordinary average of about 0.5, but an F1 of only about 0.02.

Weighting precision vs recall

The $F_\beta$ score lets you weight recall $\beta$ times as much as precision: $F_\beta = \frac{(1+\beta^2)\,TP}{(1+\beta^2)\,TP + \beta^2 FN + FP}$. $F_2$ favours recall (useful for medical screening); $F_{0.5}$ favours precision (useful for spam filtering).

Step-by-step derivationShow that F1 equals $\frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN}$
  1. $$F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R}, \qquad P = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \quad R = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$

    Start from the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

  2. $$2PR = \frac{2\,TP^2}{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)}$$

    Multiply the two fractions.

  3. $$P + R = \frac{TP(TP+FN) + TP(TP+FP)}{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)} = \frac{TP\,(2TP + FP + FN)}{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)}$$

    Add them over a common denominator.

  4. $$F_1 = \frac{2\,TP^2}{TP\,(2TP + FP + FN)} = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN}$$

    Divide: the common denominators cancel, then one $TP$ cancels.

5.The threshold trade-off

Most classifiers output a score or probability (Lesson 8). Turning it into a yes/no decision needs a threshold. Moving the threshold trades one kind of mistake for the other:

  • Lower threshold → flag more cases → more TPs and more FPs → recall ↑, precision ↓.
  • Higher threshold → flag fewer cases → fewer FPs but more FNs → precision ↑, recall ↓.

There is no threshold that is "correct" in general; you choose it based on the cost of each type of mistake.

7.ROC curves and AUC

In plain wordsThe ROC curve shows how a classifier trades catching positives against raising false alarms as you move the threshold. The area under it (AUC) is the chance the model ranks a random positive above a random negative: 0.5 is guessing, 1 is perfect.

Instead of picking one threshold, we can look at all of them at once. For each threshold, compute:

$$\text{TPR} = \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \qquad \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN} = 1 - \text{Specificity}$$

Plotting TPR (up) against FPR (across) for every threshold gives the ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic, a name from radar engineering).

  • The bottom-left corner $(0, 0)$ is threshold = 1: flag nothing.
  • The top-right corner $(1, 1)$ is threshold = 0: flag everything.
  • A perfect model reaches the top-left corner $(0, 1)$: all positives caught, no false alarms.
  • Random guessing gives the diagonal line.

The area under the ROC curve (AUC) summarises the whole curve in one number from 0.5 (random) to 1.0 (perfect). It has a lovely interpretation:

What AUC means

AUC is the probability that the model gives a randomly chosen positive example a higher score than a randomly chosen negative example.

Worked example — AUC by counting pairs

Scores for 3 positives: $0.9, 0.7, 0.4$. Scores for 3 negatives: $0.8, 0.3, 0.2$.

There are $3\times3 = 9$ positive–negative pairs. Count the pairs where the positive scores higher:

  • $0.9$ beats $0.8, 0.3, 0.2$ → 3
  • $0.7$ beats $0.3, 0.2$ (not $0.8$) → 2
  • $0.4$ beats $0.3, 0.2$ → 2

AUC $= 7/9 \approx 0.78$. The model ranks a positive above a negative 78% of the time.

Make sense of it

The situation: A fraud team only has time to check the riskiest transactions first. What matters is whether the model ranks fraud above normal transactions.

What the result tells you: AUC 0.78: pick one fraud and one normal transaction at random, and 78% of the time the model gives the fraud the higher score.

What if…?

  • The weakest positive improves (0.4 → 0.85): it now beats the 0.8 negative too, and AUC rises to 8/9 ≈ 0.89.
  • A negative scores 0.95 (instead of 0.2): AUC falls to 4/9 ≈ 0.44 — worse than guessing.
  • Add 0.05 to every score, or square them all: AUC doesn't change, because the order is the same.

Why it matters in ML: AUC measures ranking quality across all thresholds, so it's good for comparing models before you've chosen a threshold. It says nothing about whether the scores are well-calibrated probabilities.

AUC is useful because it doesn't depend on any particular threshold. But when positives are very rare, the ROC curve can look flattering, because FPR divides by the huge number of negatives.

Advanced · optionalWhy AUC equals a ranking probability

Sweep the threshold from high to low. Each time it passes a positive, the ROC curve steps up by $1/P$; each time it passes a negative, it steps right by $1/N$. The area added by a negative's right-step is $\tfrac{1}{N}\times$(fraction of positives already passed), i.e. $\tfrac{1}{N}\times\tfrac{\#\{\text{positives scored above it}\}}{P}$. Summing over all negatives:

$$\text{AUC} = \frac{1}{PN}\sum_{\text{neg } j}\ \sum_{\text{pos } i} \mathbb{1}[s_i > s_j] = P(\text{random positive outscores random negative})$$

(ties count ½). This is the Mann–Whitney U statistic divided by $PN$ — so AUC depends only on the ranking of scores, not their values, which is why it ignores calibration.

8.Precision–recall curves

In plain wordsWhen positives are rare, a precision–recall curve gives a more honest picture than the ROC curve.

When positives are rare (fraud, disease, defects), a precision–recall curve — precision (up) against recall (across) for every threshold — is usually more informative. Its summary is average precision (AP), a weighted average of precision across recall levels. A random model's AP equals the fraction of positives (e.g. 0.01 for 1% fraud), so the baseline is much lower than ROC's 0.5, making differences between models easier to see.

9.More than two classes

In plain wordsWith more than two classes, compute precision and recall for each class and average them.

With $K$ classes the confusion matrix is $K\times K$: row = true class, column = predicted class, and the diagonal holds the correct predictions. Off-diagonal cells show which classes get confused (e.g. "3" mistaken for "8"). Precision, recall and F1 are computed per class, then averaged:

  • Macro average: plain average over classes — every class counts equally.
  • Weighted average: weighted by how many examples each class has.

10.Metrics for regression

For predicting numbers, we measure the size of the errors $e_i = \hat{y}_i - y_i$:

MetricFormulaNotes
MAE — mean absolute error$\frac{1}{n}\sum|e_i|$In the target's units; robust to outliers
MSE — mean squared error$\frac{1}{n}\sum e_i^2$Punishes large errors; squared units
RMSE — root MSE$\sqrt{\text{MSE}}$Back in the target's units
$R^2$$1 - \frac{\sum e_i^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$Fraction of variance explained (Lesson 6)
Worked example

True $y = 3, 5, 2, 7$; predicted $\hat{y} = 2.5, 5, 4, 8$. Errors: $-0.5, 0, 2, 1$.

MAE $= (0.5 + 0 + 2 + 1)/4 = 0.875$.
MSE $= (0.25 + 0 + 4 + 1)/4 = 1.3125$, RMSE $= \sqrt{1.3125} \approx 1.15$.
$\bar{y} = 4.25$, $\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2 = 14.75$, so $R^2 = 1 - 5.25/14.75 \approx 0.64$.

Notice RMSE (1.15) > MAE (0.875): the single error of 2 counts extra in RMSE. If you care a lot about avoiding big misses, use RMSE; if every unit of error costs the same, use MAE.

Make sense of it

The situation: A delivery app predicts arrival times in minutes for four orders.

What the result tells you: MAE 0.875: on average the estimate is off by under a minute. RMSE 1.15 is larger because the one 2-minute miss counts extra.

What if…?

  • One bad miss (predict 8 for the order that took 2): MAE becomes 1.875, RMSE 3.05, and $R^2$ drops to −1.53 — worse than predicting the average for every order.
  • Predict the average (4.25) for everyone: $R^2 = 0$, MAE 1.75.

Why it matters in ML: MAE treats every minute of error the same; RMSE punishes big misses. A large gap between them tells you a few predictions are badly wrong — worth investigating.

11.Choosing the right metric

SituationFocus on
Balanced classes, mistakes cost the sameAccuracy
Missing a positive is very costly (disease, fraud)Recall (with acceptable precision), $F_2$
False alarms are very costly (spam filter, legal flags)Precision, $F_{0.5}$
Need one balanced number for imbalanced dataF1, average precision
Comparing models independent of thresholdROC-AUC (or AP if positives are rare)
Regression, all errors equally badMAE
Regression, big errors especially badRMSE
Always compare to a baseline

Before celebrating a score, compute what a trivial model gets: always predicting the most common class, or always predicting the mean. A 95% accurate model is unimpressive if the baseline is 94%. And always measure on data the model hasn't trained on (Lesson 5).

12.In Python

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, classification_report, roc_auc_score

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
y = 1 - y        # make "malignant" the positive class (1)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(X_train, y_train)
proba = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]          # P(malignant)

for threshold in [0.5, 0.2]:
    pred = (proba >= threshold).astype(int)
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y_test, pred).ravel()
    print(f"threshold {threshold}: TP={tp} FN={fn} FP={fp} TN={tn}  "
          f"precision={tp/(tp+fp):.3f} recall={tp/(tp+fn):.3f}")

print("ROC-AUC:", round(roc_auc_score(y_test, proba), 3))
print(classification_report(y_test, (proba >= 0.5).astype(int), target_names=["benign", "malignant"]))

At the default threshold of 0.5 the model missed 2 malignant tumours. Lowering the threshold to 0.2 missed only 1 (recall 0.96 → 0.98) but raised 9 false alarms (precision 1.00 → 0.85) — exactly the trade-off from the demo. 13.Running example: Chennai Homes

To evaluate honestly with so little data, we use cross-validated predictions: every house is predicted by a model that didn't see it. The model caught all 6 fast sellers (recall 1.0) and raised one false alarm (precision 0.86).

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_predict
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, precision_score, recall_score
clf = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression())
pred = cross_val_predict(clf, df[["dist_km", "age_yrs"]], df["sold_fast"], cv=4)   # honest predictions
tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(df["sold_fast"], pred).ravel()
print("TP", tp, "FP", fp, "FN", fn, "TN", tn)
print("precision", round(precision_score(df["sold_fast"], pred), 2), "recall", round(recall_score(df["sold_fast"], pred), 2))

Output:

TP 6 FP 1 FN 0 TN 5
precision 0.86 recall 1.0

Where this is used
  • Choosing alert thresholds for fraud and disease screening
  • Comparing models before deployment
  • Monitoring models in production

Choose it when: Always — choose the metric that reflects the real cost of each mistake.

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythAccuracy is the most important metric.

RealityOn imbalanced data a useless model can reach 99% accuracy. Choose the metric that matches the real cost of mistakes.

MythThere is one correct threshold (0.5).

RealityThe threshold is a business decision that trades precision against recall.

Responsible AI · Evaluate for every group, not just on average

An overall accuracy can hide large differences between groups. Report precision and recall for each relevant subgroup. Fairness has several competing definitions — equal false-positive rates, equal true-positive rates, calibrated scores within each group — and research by Chouldechova (2017) and by Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2016) proved that when base rates differ between groups, these criteria cannot all hold at once. Choosing which one matters is a decision for people, not the algorithm.

14.Questions beginners often ask

Which single metric should I report?

The one that matches the real cost of mistakes. If unsure, report several: precision, recall, F1 and ROC-AUC — plus a baseline.

Why is my accuracy high but the model useless?

Probably class imbalance: predicting the majority class already gives high accuracy. Look at recall and precision for the minority class.

ROC-AUC or precision–recall?

ROC-AUC for balanced classes; precision–recall (average precision) when positives are rare.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Reporting only accuracy on imbalanced data.
  • Choosing the threshold on the test set — that's tuning, so use validation data.
  • Forgetting that scikit-learn's error scorers are negated (e.g. neg_mean_squared_error).
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • Precision vs recall — Precision = purity of the flags; recall = coverage of the positives.
  • Sensitivity vs specificity — Sensitive tests catch the sick; specific tests clear the healthy.
  • Type I vs Type II error — Type I: the boy who cried wolf. Type II: the wolf nobody noticed.
  • ROC curve vs precision–recall curve — Rare positives? Read the PR curve.
  • Micro vs macro averaging — Macro is fair to small classes.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

For cancer screening, would you optimise precision or recall? For a spam filter?

The trap: Answering "accuracy".

Cancer screening: recall — missing a cancer is far worse than an extra follow-up test. Spam filter: precision — losing a real email in the spam folder is worse than letting some spam through.

Your fraud model has ROC-AUC 0.95 but the fraud team says most alerts are false. How can both be true?

The trap: Trusting ROC-AUC on imbalanced data.

FPR is divided by the huge number of normal transactions, so it looks tiny. With 10 frauds in 1,000 transactions, catching 9 at a 5% FPR still means about 49.5 false alarms: precision is about 15%. Use precision–recall curves and PR-AUC for rare positives.

A model has ROC-AUC 0.3. Is it useless?

The trap: Saying yes.

It's worse than random, which means it ranks consistently in the wrong direction. Flip its scores and AUC becomes 0.7. Often a sign of inverted labels somewhere in the pipeline.

Does changing the threshold change ROC-AUC?

The trap: Thinking AUC depends on the operating point.

No. AUC summarises all thresholds at once and depends only on how the scores rank positives against negatives. Precision, recall, F1 and accuracy do change with the threshold.

15.Key takeaways

  • The confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN) shows exactly which mistakes a classifier makes.
  • Precision $= \frac{TP}{TP+FP}$: trust in positive predictions. Recall $= \frac{TP}{TP+FN}$: share of positives found. F1 balances them.
  • Accuracy misleads on imbalanced data — always compare with a trivial baseline.
  • The threshold trades precision against recall; ROC-AUC and average precision summarise all thresholds.
  • Regression: MAE, RMSE (punishes big errors) and $R^2$.

16.Check your understanding

1. TP = 30, FP = 10, FN = 20. What is the precision?

Precision $= 30/(30 + 10) = 0.75$. (Recall would be $30/(30+20) = 0.60$.)

2. A fraud detector must miss as few frauds as possible. Which metric matters most?

Missing a fraud is a false negative; recall $= TP/(TP+FN)$ measures how many frauds are caught.

3. You lower the classification threshold from 0.5 to 0.3. What usually happens?

More examples get flagged positive: more true positives are caught (recall ↑) but more false alarms too (precision ↓).

4. A model has ROC-AUC = 0.5. What does that suggest?

AUC 0.5 means a random positive is scored above a random negative only half the time — like a coin flip.

5. Errors are 1, −1, 1, −5. Which is larger, MAE or RMSE?

MAE $= 8/4 = 2$. MSE $= (1+1+1+25)/4 = 7$, RMSE $\approx 2.65$. Squaring makes the big error count more.
