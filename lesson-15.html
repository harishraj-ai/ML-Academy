Before you start

This lesson builds on:

  • Lesson 2: Vectors & Matrices — A vector is a list of numbers; a dataset $X$ is a matrix with one row per example. The dot product $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} = \sum w_ix_i$ is how linear models predict, and $\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|$ is the distance between two examples.
  • Lesson 8: Logistic Regression — Logistic regression outputs a probability $p = \sigma(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b)$, is trained with log loss, and classifies with a threshold (often 0.5), giving a straight decision boundary.
  • Lesson 10: Overfitting, Regularization & Bias–Variance — Error = bias² + variance + noise. Regularization adds a penalty on weight size (L2 ridge shrinks weights; L1 lasso zeros some) to reduce variance; its strength is chosen by cross-validation.

Where this fits: A different idea of the "best" boundary (the widest margin), plus the kernel trick for curved boundaries. Completes the classic toolbox.

1.Which line is best?

When two classes can be separated by a straight line, there are usually infinitely many lines that do it. Logistic regression (Lesson 8) picks one by maximising likelihood. A support vector machine (SVM) has a different and very intuitive answer: choose the line that stays as far as possible from both classes — the one with the widest "safety corridor" between them.

Analogy: the widest road

Imagine two villages on either side of a field, and you must build a straight road between them. A road that grazes the houses on one side is risky — a slight error and it cuts through someone's garden. The safest road is the widest one you can fit, running right through the middle of the gap. SVMs build the widest possible road; the houses at its edges are the support vectors.

A wide margin means that new points can land a bit off from the training points and still be classified correctly — better generalization. Combined with the kernel trick, SVMs also draw curved boundaries, and for years they were among the best general-purpose classifiers.

2.Setting up: labels ±1 and a hyperplane

For SVMs it's convenient to label the classes $y = +1$ and $y = -1$ (instead of 1 and 0). As in Lesson 8, the boundary is the set of points where

$$\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0$$

and we predict $\text{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b)$: $+1$ if positive, $-1$ if negative. The score $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b$ also measures how far a point is from the boundary: the actual distance is

$$\text{distance}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b|}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$$

A handy trick: a point is correctly classified exactly when $y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) > 0$ — the label and the score have the same sign.

3.The margin

Picture itParking between two cars, you aim for the middle of the gap, not just anywhere that fits — leaving the most room for error on both sides. An SVM chooses its boundary the same way.

The scale of $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ is arbitrary (doubling both gives the same line), so we fix it: require the points closest to the boundary to have score exactly $\pm1$. All points must then satisfy

$$y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 \quad \text{for every } i$$

The two "gutters" $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = +1$ and $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = -1$ are the edges of the road. Each is at distance $1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ from the boundary, so the road's total width — the margin — is

$$\text{margin} = \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$$
Worked example — measuring a margin

Suppose an SVM has $\mathbf{w} = (1, 1)$, $b = -3$. Then $\|\mathbf{w}\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$, so the margin is $2/\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$.

Point $(1, 1)$: score $= 1 + 1 - 3 = -1$ → class $-1$, sitting exactly on the gutter — a support vector.

Point $(3, 2)$: score $= 3 + 2 - 3 = 2$ → class $+1$, distance from boundary $2/\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ — safely outside the road.

Make sense of it

The situation: Picture a road between two neighbourhoods. The SVM wants the widest road that keeps every red house on one side and every blue house on the other.

What the result tells you: The road is 1.41 units wide. A point with score exactly ±1, like (1, 1), sits on the edge of the road: a support vector.

What if…?

  • Double the weights to $\mathbf{w} = (2, 2),\ b = -6$: the boundary line is the same, but the road narrows to 0.71. The margin is set by $\|\mathbf{w}\|$, which is why SVMs minimise it.
  • Halve them to $(0.5, 0.5),\ b = -1.5$: road width 2.83 — but now points like (1, 1) are inside the road.

Why it matters in ML: A wider margin usually means the boundary is less sensitive to small changes in the data, so it generalises better.

Step-by-step derivationWhy the margin is $2/\|\mathbf{w}\|$
  1. $$\text{distance}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b|}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$$

    Distance from a point to the boundary $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0$.

  2. $$\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = \pm 1$$

    Points on the gutters have score exactly $\pm1$ (that's how we fixed the scale).

  3. $$\text{distance} = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$$

    So each gutter is $1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ from the boundary.

  4. $$\text{margin} = \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$$

    Two gutters, one on each side.

  5. $$\max \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|} \iff \min \|\mathbf{w}\| \iff \min \tfrac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$$

    Maximising the margin means making $\mathbf{w}$ small; squaring gives the same answer with nicer maths.

4.The optimisation problem

In plain wordsThe SVM picks the line with the widest gap between the classes. Mathematically, a wide gap means small weights, so it finds the smallest weights that still keep every point on the correct side.

Maximising the margin $2/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ is the same as minimising $\|\mathbf{w}\|$, and for convenient maths we minimise $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$. The hard-margin SVM is:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}, b}\ \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 \ \text{ for all } i$$

"Make the road as wide as possible, while keeping every point off the road and on its correct side." This is a convex problem with one best solution, solved by specialised algorithms.

Support vectors

In the solution, only the points lying exactly on the gutters (or, with soft margins, inside the road or misclassified) affect where the boundary is. These are the support vectors. Delete any other training point and the boundary doesn't move at all. That's where the name comes from — they "support" the boundary.

5.Soft margins and the hinge loss

Real data overlaps and has outliers; often no line separates the classes perfectly, and the hard-margin problem has no solution. The soft-margin SVM lets points violate the margin, at a price. The cleanest way to see it is through the hinge loss:

$$\ell_{\text{hinge}} = \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b)\big)$$
Worked example — hinge loss for a positive point ($y = +1$)
Score $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b$Hinge lossMeaning
1.5$\max(0, 1 - 1.5) = 0$correct and outside the road — no penalty
0.4$\max(0, 1 - 0.4) = 0.6$correct side, but inside the road — small penalty
−0.5$\max(0, 1 + 0.5) = 1.5$wrong side — bigger penalty

Unlike log loss (Lesson 8), hinge loss is exactly zero once a point is safely classified. Those points stop mattering — which is why only the support vectors define the boundary.

Make sense of it

The situation: A strict but fair examiner: full marks with no comment if you're clearly right, a small penalty if you're right but only just, a bigger one if you're wrong.

What the result tells you: Hinge loss is zero once a point is past the edge of the road (score ≥ 1), and grows in a straight line as the point moves into or across the road.

What if…?

  • Score exactly 1: loss 0 — on the edge, no penalty.
  • Score 0 (on the boundary): loss 1.
  • A negative point ($y = -1$) with score −1.5: loss $\max(0, 1 - (-1)(-1.5)) = 0$ — correctly classified and outside the road.

Why it matters in ML: Because confident, correct points have zero loss, they have no influence on the solution. Only points near or across the road shape the boundary — which makes SVMs robust to far-away points but sensitive to the ones near the boundary.

The soft-margin SVM balances margin width against total hinge loss:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}, b}\ \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b)\big)$$

Look closely: it's "regularization + loss", the same shape as ridge regression in Lesson 10. The parameter $C$ sets the balance:

  • Small $C$: margin violations are cheap → wide road, many support vectors, smoother boundary. More regularization (more bias).
  • Large $C$: violations are expensive → narrow road that tries to classify every training point correctly. Less regularization (more variance).
Read the formulaSoft-margin SVM objective
$$\htmlClass{fd-1}{\tfrac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2} + \htmlClass{fd-2}{C}\htmlClass{fd-3}{\sum_i \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b)\big)}$$
  • ½‖w‖² — small w = wide margin
  • C — how expensive margin violations are
  • Σ max(0, …) — hinge losses: zero for points safely outside the margin

7.The kernel trick: curved boundaries

In plain wordsKernels let an SVM draw curved boundaries by quietly working in a richer space where a straight line is enough. The popular RBF kernel measures how similar two points are, and its setting γ controls how wiggly the boundary can be.

What if the classes form a ring — one class in the middle, the other all around? No straight line works. The idea: map the data into a higher-dimensional space where a straight boundary does work.

Worked example — lifting data to separate it

One feature: class $+1$ at $x = -3, 3$ and class $-1$ at $x = -1, 0, 1$. On a number line, the $-1$ class is in the middle — no single threshold separates them.

Add a feature $x^2$, so each point becomes $(x, x^2)$: the $+1$ points go to $(\pm3, 9)$, the $-1$ points to $(-1, 1), (0, 0), (1, 1)$. Now the horizontal line $x^2 = 5$ separates them perfectly. A straight line in the new space is a curved boundary ($x = \pm\sqrt5$) in the original one.

Make sense of it

The situation: Normal blood pressure is in the middle; both very low and very high are dangerous. No single threshold separates healthy from unhealthy.

What the result tells you: Adding $x^2$ as a feature turns "far from 0 in either direction" into "large $x^2$", which one threshold can separate.

What if…?

  • Bring the outer points in to ±2: their $x^2 = 4$ is still above the inner points' 1, so a threshold between 1 and 4 still works.
  • Put a −1 point at ±3 too: then no threshold on $x^2$ works, and you'd need yet another feature.

Why it matters in ML: Many non-linear problems become linear with the right extra features. Kernels add them implicitly; neural networks learn them.

The problem: with many features, the number of such combinations explodes. Here's the magic. The SVM's solution (in its "dual" form) only ever uses the training data through dot products $\phi(\mathbf{x}_i)\cdot\phi(\mathbf{x}_j)$ of mapped points. A kernel is a function that computes that dot product directly from the original vectors, without ever building $\phi(\mathbf{x})$:

$$K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \phi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\phi(\mathbf{z})$$
Worked example — the trick in action

For 2D vectors, take $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (x_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\ x_2^2)$. Let $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)$, $\mathbf{z} = (3, 1)$.

The long way: $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (1,\ 2\sqrt2,\ 4)$, $\phi(\mathbf{z}) = (9,\ 3\sqrt2,\ 1)$. Dot product: $9 + 2\sqrt2\cdot3\sqrt2 + 4 = 9 + 12 + 4 = 25$.

The kernel way: $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = (\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z})^2 = (1\cdot3 + 2\cdot1)^2 = 5^2 = 25$.

Same answer, without computing the 3D vectors. For higher degrees and dimensions, the savings are enormous.

Make sense of it

The situation: Comparing two documents by every pair of words they contain would mean building enormous vectors. The kernel gets the same similarity from the short vectors.

What the result tells you: Both ways give 25: the kernel $(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z})^2$ equals a dot product in the 3-D feature space, without building it.

What if…?

  • Perpendicular inputs $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2),\ \mathbf{z} = (2, -1)$: $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z} = 0$, so $K = 0$.
  • 100 features, degree 3: the explicit feature space has 171,700 dimensions; the kernel needs one 100-term dot product and a cube.

Why it matters in ML: The kernel trick lets SVMs use huge (even infinite, for the RBF kernel) feature spaces cheaply. The cost moves elsewhere: kernel SVMs compare every pair of training points, so they slow down badly beyond tens of thousands of examples.

Common kernels

KernelK(x, z)Boundary shape
Linear$\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z}$Straight line / plane
Polynomial$(\gamma\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z} + r)^d$Polynomial curves
RBF (Gaussian)$\exp(-\gamma\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}\|^2)$Flexible, smooth, any shape

The RBF kernel (radial basis function) is the most popular. It's a similarity score: 1 when $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{z}$, falling towards 0 as they move apart. Each support vector acts like a "bump" of influence, and the boundary is built from these bumps. For $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{z} = (3, 1)$, $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}\|^2 = 5$: with $\gamma = 0.1$, $K = e^{-0.5} \approx 0.61$ (fairly similar); with $\gamma = 1$, $K = e^{-5} \approx 0.007$ (barely related).

  • Small $\gamma$: each point influences a wide area → smooth boundary (can underfit).
  • Large $\gamma$: influence is very local → wiggly boundary around individual points (can overfit).

Tune $C$ and $\gamma$ together with a grid search over values spaced by factors of 10.

Advanced · optionalThe maths behind kernels

An explicit example. For $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^2$, let $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (x_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\ x_2^2)$. Then

$$\phi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\phi(\mathbf{z}) = x_1^2z_1^2 + 2x_1x_2z_1z_2 + x_2^2z_2^2 = (\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z})^2.$$

So the kernel $(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z})^2$ computes a dot product in a 3-dimensional feature space without ever building $\phi$. With $d$ features and degree $p$ the explicit space has $\binom{d+p-1}{p}$ dimensions; the kernel still costs one dot product.

Why only dot products are needed. The SVM's optimal weights are a combination of training points, $\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i$ (with $\alpha_i > 0$ only for support vectors). Substituting, the decision function becomes

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}) + b,$$

and training (the dual problem) also uses the data only through $K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)$. Mercer's condition: a function $K$ corresponds to some feature map exactly when every kernel matrix $[K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)]$ it produces is symmetric positive semi-definite. The RBF kernel $e^{-\gamma\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|^2}$ passes this test, and its feature space is infinite-dimensional.

8.SVMs in practice

  • Scale your features. Distances and dot products depend on scale, just like kNN.
  • Size limits. Kernel SVM training time grows roughly between $n^2$ and $n^3$ in the number of examples, so it suits small and medium datasets (up to tens of thousands). For large data, use LinearSVC or SGDClassifier(loss="hinge").
  • Probabilities. SVMs output scores, not probabilities. SVC(probability=True) adds a calibration step, at extra cost.
  • Regression. SVR applies the same ideas to regression: errors smaller than $\varepsilon$ are ignored (a "tube" around the prediction).

9.In Python

A ring-shaped dataset where one class surrounds the other:

from sklearn.datasets import make_circles
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, GridSearchCV
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

X, y = make_circles(n_samples=400, noise=0.2, factor=0.5, random_state=0)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0)

for kernel in ["linear", "poly", "rbf"]:
    m = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel=kernel, degree=2)).fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(f"{kernel:6s} test accuracy {m.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}   support vectors {m[-1].n_support_.sum()}")

grid = {"svc__C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100], "svc__gamma": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}
search = GridSearchCV(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf")), grid, cv=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
print("best:", search.best_params_, "CV", round(search.best_score_, 3), "test", round(search.score(X_test, y_test), 3))

m = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf", C=100, gamma=10)).fit(X_train, y_train)
print("C=100, gamma=10: train", round(m.score(X_train, y_train), 3), "test", round(m.score(X_test, y_test), 3))

Output:

linear test accuracy 0.470   support vectors 297
poly   test accuracy 0.950   support vectors 97
rbf    test accuracy 0.930   support vectors 112
best: {'svc__C': 0.1, 'svc__gamma': 1} CV 0.893 test 0.9
C=100, gamma=10: train 0.983 test 0.86

The linear SVM is no better than guessing (47%) — a straight line can't separate a ring — and nearly every point becomes a support vector. A degree-2 polynomial kernel (which can draw circles) and the RBF kernel both succeed. The last line shows overfitting with large $C$ and $\gamma$: 98% on training data but only 86% on the test set.

10.Running example: Chennai Homes

A linear SVM on distance and age separates fast and slow sellers. Only 5 houses — the support vectors — define the boundary; the others could be removed without changing it.

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
cols = ["dist_km", "age_yrs"]
svm = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="linear", C=1.0)).fit(df[cols], df["sold_fast"])
w = svm[-1].coef_[0]
print("margin width (standardised units):", round(2 / np.linalg.norm(w), 2))
print("support vectors: houses", (df["id"].iloc[svm[-1].support_]).tolist())

Output:

margin width (standardised units): 1.18
support vectors: houses [3, 7, 8, 2, 4]
Where this is used
  • Text classification with many features
  • Small-to-medium datasets needing a strong classifier
  • Historically: handwriting and image classification

Choose it when: Medium-sized data with clear margins or many features; when a kernel captures the shape well.

Think twice when: Very large datasets (slow kernel training) or when you need probabilities.

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythEvery training point shapes the SVM boundary.

RealityOnly the support vectors matter; the others could be deleted without changing it.

MythThe kernel trick computes the high-dimensional features.

RealityIt computes dot products in that space directly, without ever building the features.

Responsible AI · Scores are not probabilities

An SVM's output is a distance from the boundary, not a probability, and a large margin is not a guarantee of being right. If a decision needs a probability ("85% chance of default"), calibrate the scores and check calibration for each group. And for consequential decisions, keep a human able to review and override the model.

11.Questions beginners often ask

What does C actually control?

The trade-off between a wide margin and classifying every training point correctly. Small C = wider margin, more tolerance of errors (more regularization).

Which kernel should I start with?

RBF is the usual default. Try linear first if you have very many features, such as text.

Why are SVMs less popular than they were?

Gradient boosting tends to win on tabular data and deep learning on images and text; kernel SVMs also scale poorly to very large datasets. They remain a solid choice for medium-sized problems.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Forgetting to scale features before an SVM.
  • Tuning $C$ and $\gamma$ separately instead of together.
  • Using kernel SVMs on hundreds of thousands of examples (use LinearSVC or SGD).
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • Hard margin vs soft margin — No exceptions vs a fine for each exception.
  • C vs gamma in an RBF SVM — C is strictness; gamma is reach.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

Which training points determine an SVM's boundary?

The trap: "All of them."

Only the support vectors: points on or inside the margin, or misclassified. Points safely beyond the margin have zero hinge loss and could be deleted without changing the model.

What happens as you make C very large in a soft-margin SVM?

The trap: Thinking larger C means more regularisation.

The opposite: C is the penalty for margin violations, so a large C tolerates almost none — a narrow margin that fits the training data closely and can overfit. A small C allows more violations and a wider, simpler margin. (C is like $1/\lambda$.)

What does the RBF kernel's gamma control?

The trap: Confusing it with C.

How far each training point's influence reaches. Large gamma: very local influence, wiggly boundaries, overfitting. Small gamma: smooth boundaries, possibly underfitting. Tune C and gamma together with cross-validation.

Does the kernel trick compute the high-dimensional features?

The trap: Thinking it transforms the data explicitly.

No — that's the point. The kernel gives the dot product in the feature space directly from the original vectors. For 100 features and a degree-3 polynomial kernel, the feature space has 171,700 dimensions, yet each kernel value costs one 100-term dot product.

12.Key takeaways

  • An SVM chooses the boundary with the largest margin $2/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ between the classes.
  • Only the support vectors — points on or inside the margin — determine the boundary.
  • Soft-margin SVM: $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C\sum\text{hinge}$; small $C$ = wider margin, more regularization.
  • The kernel trick computes dot products in a high-dimensional space cheaply, giving curved boundaries.
  • The RBF kernel is the usual default; tune $C$ and $\gamma$ together, and scale features first.

13.Check your understanding

1. An SVM has $\|\mathbf{w}\| = 4$. How wide is its margin?

Margin $= 2/\|\mathbf{w}\| = 2/4 = 0.5$.

2. A point with $y = -1$ has score $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0.5$. What is its hinge loss?

$\max(0, 1 - y\cdot\text{score}) = \max(0, 1 - (-1)(0.5)) = 1.5$. It's on the wrong side.

3. You delete a training point that is far from the boundary and outside the margin. What happens to the SVM?

Only support vectors define the solution; points with zero hinge loss don't affect it.

4. Your RBF SVM gets 100% training accuracy and 70% test accuracy. What should you try?

That's overfitting. Smaller $\gamma$ smooths the boundary; smaller $C$ allows more margin violations — both regularize.

5. What does a kernel function compute?

$K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \phi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\phi(\mathbf{z})$, computed directly from $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{z}$.
