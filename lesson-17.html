Before you start

This lesson builds on:

  • Lesson 2: Vectors & Matrices — A vector is a list of numbers; a dataset $X$ is a matrix with one row per example. The dot product $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} = \sum w_ix_i$ is how linear models predict, and $\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|$ is the distance between two examples.
  • Lesson 4: Probability & Statistics — Probabilities run from 0 to 1. Bayes' theorem turns $P(B\mid A)$ into $P(A\mid B)$. Mean and standard deviation summarise data; the z-score $(x-\mu)/\sigma$ standardises it.
  • Lesson 16: Clustering — Clustering groups unlabelled data. k-means alternates assigning points to the nearest centroid and moving each centroid to the mean of its points.

Where this fits: The second unsupervised tool: compressing many features into a few, for visualisation, speed and noise reduction.

1.Too many features

Picture itA photo of a teapot from its best angle tells you almost everything about its shape; a photo straight down the spout tells you very little. PCA finds the "best angles" to look at your data from, so that a few of them show nearly everything.

A small 8×8 image of a handwritten digit is 64 numbers. A 1-megapixel photo is 3 million. A customer record might have hundreds of columns. Lots of features cause real problems:

  • The curse of dimensionality (Lesson 11): in many dimensions, data is sparse and distances lose meaning.
  • Overfitting: more features means more parameters to fit noise (Lesson 10).
  • Slowness and memory use grow with the number of features.
  • We can't see it: humans can plot 2 or 3 dimensions, not 64.

Fortunately, real features are often redundant. Height in cm and height in inches carry the same information; neighbouring pixels in an image are strongly correlated. Dimensionality reduction compresses many features into a few new ones while keeping most of the information. The most important method is principal component analysis (PCA).

Analogy: photographing a teapot

You have to describe a 3D teapot with a single 2D photo. Some angles (straight down from the top) lose almost everything — you just see a circle. The best angle, from the side, shows the spout, handle and lid: the photo keeps the most "spread-out" view of the object. PCA finds the best camera angle for your data: the directions in which the data varies the most.

2.The key idea: keep the directions with the most variance

Imagine points in 2D that lie roughly along a diagonal line. Two numbers $(x_1, x_2)$ describe each point, but really the points differ mainly in one way: how far along the line they are. If we rotate our axes so the first axis runs along the line, then:

  • the first new coordinate captures almost all the differences between points;
  • the second new coordinate (perpendicular) is nearly the same for every point — mostly noise.

Dropping the second coordinate loses very little. That's PCA: find new axes, ordered by how much of the data's variance they capture, and keep only the first few.

Principal components

The first principal component (PC1) is the direction along which the data has the largest variance. The second (PC2) is the direction of largest remaining variance that is perpendicular to PC1, and so on. With $d$ features there are $d$ principal components; we keep the first $k$.

3.Try it: find the best direction

Rotate the orange line. Each point is projected (dropped at a right angle) onto the line, and the readout shows the variance of those projected positions — how much "spread" this 1D view keeps. Find the angle that maximises it: that's PC1. Press Snap to PC1 to check, and notice that the best direction also minimises the total squared length of the projection lines (the information lost).

4.Measuring how features vary together: covariance

In plain wordsCovariance measures whether two features rise and fall together. PCA uses it to find the directions in which the data is most spread out.

To find directions of maximum variance, we need to know how features vary together. The covariance of two features (after subtracting their means) is the average product of their deviations:

$$\text{cov}(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i1} - \bar{x}_1)(x_{i2} - \bar{x}_2)$$
  • Positive: they tend to be above average together (like height and weight).
  • Negative: when one is above average, the other tends to be below.
  • Near zero: no linear relationship.

The covariance of a feature with itself is its variance (Lesson 4). Putting all the pairs in a table gives the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ ($d \times d$, symmetric). For centred data matrix $X_c$ it's simply

$$\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1}X_c^\top X_c$$

5.Eigenvectors: the directions PCA finds

In plain wordsThe directions PCA finds are called eigenvectors; how spread out the data is along each one is its eigenvalue. Bigger eigenvalue = more important direction.

Here's the central fact (proved in linear algebra courses): the principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and the variance along each one is its eigenvalue.

An eigenvector of a matrix $\Sigma$ is a special direction $\mathbf{v}$ that the matrix only stretches, without turning:

$$\Sigma\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$$

The stretch factor $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue. For a covariance matrix, eigenvectors are perpendicular to each other and eigenvalues are never negative. Sorting the eigenvectors by eigenvalue, largest first, gives PC1, PC2, …

The fraction of the total variance captured by component $j$ is the explained variance ratio:

$$\text{explained}_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_d}$$
For 2×2 matrices there's a formula

For $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ b & c\end{bmatrix}$ the eigenvalues are $\lambda = \frac{a+c}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{a-c}{2}\right)^2 + b^2}$. Bigger matrices are handled by software (scikit-learn computes PCA with the singular value decomposition, an equivalent and more numerically stable method).

Step-by-step derivationWhy the best direction is the top eigenvector
  1. $$z_i = \mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \|\mathbf{u}\| = 1$$

    Project each centred point onto a unit direction $\mathbf{u}$.

  2. $$\text{Var}(z) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x}_i)^2 = \mathbf{u}^\top\Big(\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i \mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top\Big)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\,\mathbf{u}$$

    The variance along $\mathbf{u}$ is a simple expression in the covariance matrix.

  3. $$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}_j \Rightarrow \mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}_j^\top\lambda_j\mathbf{v}_j = \lambda_j$$

    Along an eigenvector, the variance is its eigenvalue.

  4. $$\mathbf{u} = \sum_j c_j\mathbf{v}_j \Rightarrow \mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{u} = \sum_j \lambda_j c_j^2, \quad \sum_j c_j^2 = 1$$

    Any unit direction is a mix of the (perpendicular) eigenvectors.

  5. $$\max_{\mathbf{u}} \mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{u} = \lambda_1 \ \text{ at } \ \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}_1$$

    A weighted average of eigenvalues is largest when all the weight is on the biggest one: PC1.

Read the formulaExplained variance ratio
$$\text{explained}_j = \frac{\htmlClass{fd-1}{\lambda_j}}{\htmlClass{fd-2}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_d}}$$
  • λⱼ — variance along component j (its eigenvalue)
  • λ₁ + … + λ_d — total variance in the data

Hover over (or tap) a part to highlight it in the formula.

6.Worked example: PCA by hand

Worked example — five points in 2D

Data: $(1, 2),\ (2, 3),\ (3, 5),\ (4, 4),\ (5, 6)$.

Step 1 — centre. Means: $\bar{x}_1 = 3$, $\bar{x}_2 = 4$. Subtract them:

$$(-2, -2),\ (-1, -1),\ (0, 1),\ (1, 0),\ (2, 2)$$

Step 2 — covariance matrix (dividing by $n - 1 = 4$):

$\text{var}(x_1) = \frac{4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4}{4} = 2.5$, $\ \text{var}(x_2) = \frac{4 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 4}{4} = 2.5$,
$\text{cov}(x_1, x_2) = \frac{4 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 4}{4} = 2.25$.

$$\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 & 2.25 \\ 2.25 & 2.5\end{bmatrix}$$

Step 3 — eigenvalues: $\lambda = 2.5 \pm \sqrt{0 + 2.25^2} = 2.5 \pm 2.25$, so $\lambda_1 = 4.75$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.25$.

Step 4 — eigenvectors: PC1 $= \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$, the diagonal. Check: $\Sigma\,(1, 1) = (2.5 + 2.25,\ 2.25 + 2.5) = (4.75, 4.75) = 4.75\,(1, 1)$ ✓. PC2 $= \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$, perpendicular.

Step 5 — explained variance: PC1 keeps $\frac{4.75}{4.75 + 0.25} = 95\%$ of the variance. One number per point instead of two, losing only 5%.

Step 6 — project: each point's new coordinate is the dot product of its centred vector with PC1. For $(-2, -2)$: $\frac{-2 - 2}{\sqrt2} \approx -2.83$. All five: $-2.83,\ -1.41,\ 0.71,\ 0.71,\ 2.83$.

Make sense of it

The situation: Two exam scores per student — maths and physics — that rise and fall together. Could one number describe each student almost as well?

What the result tells you: PC1 is the diagonal direction "good at both". It holds 95% of the variation, so a single score per student keeps nearly all the information. PC2, "better at one than the other", holds only 5%.

What if…?

  • Points exactly on a line (1, 1), (2, 2), …: the second eigenvalue is 0 and PC1 keeps 100%. One number loses nothing.
  • Weakly related scores (1, 4), (2, 2), (3, 6), (4, 4), (5, 4): PC1 keeps only 62%. Dropping PC2 would throw away a lot.
  • Measure the second score in different units (×10): PC1 now keeps 99.8% and points almost entirely along that feature (0.996). Nothing about the students changed — PCA simply chases the feature with the biggest numbers.

Why it matters in ML: PCA finds the directions of greatest variance, so it works best when features are correlated — and it must be run on standardised features, or the units decide the answer. Check the explained-variance ratio before deciding how many components to keep.

7.PCA step by step

In plain wordsIn practice: standardise the features, let the library find the components, keep the first few that explain most of the variance (for example 95%), and use those as your new features.

  1. Centre the data (subtract each feature's mean). Usually also standardise (divide by the standard deviation) if features are in different units — otherwise a feature measured in large numbers dominates the variance.
  2. Compute the covariance matrix (or use the SVD directly).
  3. Find its eigenvectors and eigenvalues, sorted by eigenvalue.
  4. Choose $k$ and keep the top $k$ eigenvectors as the columns of a matrix $W_k$ ($d \times k$).
  5. Project: $Z = X_c W_k$, giving $n \times k$ new features.

You can also approximately reconstruct the original data from the compressed version: $\hat{X} = Z W_k^\top + \bar{\mathbf{x}}$. The reconstruction error is exactly the variance you threw away.

Advanced · optionalDeriving PCA — from maximum variance and from the SVD

Maximum variance. For centred data with covariance $C$, the variance along a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ is $\mathbf{u}^\top C\mathbf{u}$. Maximise it subject to $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u} = 1$ with a Lagrange multiplier: $\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\big[\mathbf{u}^\top C\mathbf{u} - \lambda(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u}-1)\big] = 2C\mathbf{u} - 2\lambda\mathbf{u} = 0$, so $C\mathbf{u} = \lambda\mathbf{u}$: $\mathbf{u}$ must be an eigenvector, and the variance it captures is $\mathbf{u}^\top C\mathbf{u} = \lambda$. The best direction is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue.

Via the SVD (how libraries actually do it). Write the centred $n\times d$ data matrix as $X = U\Sigma V^\top$, with $U$, $V$ orthogonal and $\Sigma$ diagonal with singular values $s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \dots$. Then

$$C = \frac{X^\top X}{n-1} = \frac{V\Sigma U^\top U\Sigma V^\top}{n-1} = V\,\frac{\Sigma^2}{n-1}\,V^\top.$$

This is an eigendecomposition of $C$: the columns of $V$ are the principal components and the eigenvalues are $\lambda_i = s_i^2/(n-1)$. The projected data is $XV = U\Sigma$. The SVD is preferred because it never forms $X^\top X$, which would square the condition number and lose numerical precision.

8.How many components?

  • Explained variance threshold: keep enough components to explain, say, 95% of the variance. In scikit-learn: PCA(n_components=0.95).
  • Scree plot: plot eigenvalues in order and look for an elbow, as with k-means.
  • For a downstream model: treat $k$ as a hyperparameter and choose it by cross-validation.
  • For visualisation: $k = 2$ or $3$.

9.Uses and limitations

Good forWatch out for
Visualising high-dimensional data in 2DComponents are mixtures of features — harder to interpret
Speeding up and regularizing other modelsOnly finds linear structure (straight directions)
Removing noise (drop low-variance components)High variance ≠ useful for prediction; a low-variance direction may matter for the label
Compressing dataSensitive to feature scale — standardise first

10.Beyond PCA: t-SNE and UMAP for visualisation

When structure is curved (like the moons in Lesson 16), a linear projection can squash different groups on top of each other. t-SNE is a non-linear method designed purely for visualisation: it places points in 2D so that points that were neighbours in the original space stay neighbours. Its main setting, perplexity, roughly controls how many neighbours each point pays attention to. UMAP is a newer, faster method with a similar goal.

Reading t-SNE plots safely

t-SNE pictures are easy to over-interpret. The authors of the guide "How to Use t-SNE Effectively" show that cluster sizes in a t-SNE plot mean nothing, distances between well-separated clusters may not mean anything either, and with a low perplexity even pure random noise can appear to form clusters. Different runs can give different pictures. Use t-SNE to explore, never as proof — and unlike PCA, it can't transform new data for a model.

11.In Python

The digits dataset: 1,797 images of handwritten digits, each 8×8 = 64 pixel features.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
pca = PCA().fit(X)
cum = np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
for k in [2, 10, 20, 30]:
    print(f"{k:2d} components keep {cum[k-1]:.1%} of the variance")
print("components needed for 95%:", np.argmax(cum >= 0.95) + 1)

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)
for k in [5, 10, 20, 40, 64]:
    model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=k), LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000))
    model.fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(f"{k:2d} components: test accuracy {model.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}")

Output:

 2 components keep 28.5% of the variance
10 components keep 73.8% of the variance
20 components keep 89.4% of the variance
30 components keep 95.9% of the variance
components needed for 95%: 29
 5 components: test accuracy 0.820
10 components: test accuracy 0.867
20 components: test accuracy 0.956
40 components: test accuracy 0.971
64 components: test accuracy 0.969

29 of the 64 pixel features hold 95% of the variance. With 40 components the classifier is as accurate as with all 64 (slightly better, in fact), using under two-thirds of the features. Even 20 components give 95.6%. To plot the digits in 2D, use PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(X) — or sklearn.manifold.TSNE for a non-linear picture.

12.Running example: Chennai Homes

PCA on the four standardised features: the first component captures 61.5% of the variance. Its weights are all positive — it measures "big, far and older" all at once — and four components can be squeezed into two while keeping about 82.5% of the information.

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
cols = ["size_m2", "bedrooms", "dist_km", "age_yrs"]
pca = PCA().fit(StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[cols]))
print("explained variance ratio:", pca.explained_variance_ratio_.round(3))
print("PC1 weights:", {c: round(float(v), 2) for c, v in zip(cols, pca.components_[0])})

Output:

explained variance ratio: [0.615 0.21  0.165 0.01 ]
PC1 weights: {'size_m2': 0.61, 'bedrooms': 0.57, 'dist_km': 0.42, 'age_yrs': 0.34}

Where this is used
  • Visualising high-dimensional data in 2D
  • Compressing features before another model
  • Removing noise from signals and images

Choose it when: Many correlated features, or you need a 2D picture.

Think twice when: Interpretability of individual original features matters, or structure is strongly non-linear.

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythPCA picks the most important original features.

RealityEach component is a mixture of all the original features.

MythDistances in a t-SNE picture are meaningful.

RealityCluster sizes and gaps between clusters in t-SNE plots can be misleading; it's for exploration only.

Responsible AI · Reducing dimensions does not anonymise

Compressing or transforming data does not make it anonymous. Narayanan and Shmatikov (2008) re-identified users in the "anonymised" Netflix Prize dataset by matching a handful of their movie ratings with public reviews. A few dimensions, or a few rare values, can be enough to single someone out; treat derived features of personal data as personal data.

13.Questions beginners often ask

Does PCA throw away information?

Yes, the variance in the dropped components — by design, usually a small amount. The explained-variance ratio tells you how much you kept.

Should I always use PCA before a model?

No. It helps with many correlated features, visualisation or speed. With few features, or when interpretability matters, it's often unnecessary.

PCA or t-SNE?

PCA for compression and as input to models; t-SNE (or UMAP) only for exploring a 2D picture.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Running PCA on unstandardised features measured in different units.
  • Fitting PCA on all data before the train/test split.
  • Reading cluster sizes or gaps in a t-SNE plot as real distances.
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • PCA vs t-SNE — PCA to compress; t-SNE to look.
  • Feature selection vs feature extraction — Choose columns vs make new ones.
  • Eigenvector vs eigenvalue — Direction vs amount.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

Should you standardise before PCA?

The trap: Running PCA on raw features.

Almost always. PCA chases variance, so a feature in large units dominates. In Lesson 17, measuring one score ×10 made PC1 keep 99.8% of the variance and point almost entirely along that feature.

PCA keeps 95% of the variance. Can it still remove the feature that best predicts the target?

The trap: Assuming high variance means useful.

Yes. PCA ignores the target. A low-variance direction can be the most predictive one, and PCA might discard it. If prediction is the goal, compare with supervised selection, or tune the number of components by cross-validated performance.

You fit PCA on the full dataset, then split into train and test. Problem?

The trap: Thinking unsupervised steps can't leak.

Yes, a mild leak: the components were shaped by the test data. Fit PCA on the training data only (inside a Pipeline) and apply that transformation to the test data.

In a t-SNE plot, two clusters are far apart. Are they very different?

The trap: Reading t-SNE like a map.

Not necessarily. t-SNE preserves local neighbourhoods, not global distances or cluster sizes, and its picture changes with the perplexity setting. Use it to explore, not to measure.

14.Key takeaways

  • Dimensionality reduction compresses many correlated features into a few new ones.
  • PCA finds perpendicular directions of maximum variance: the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, ordered by eigenvalue.
  • Explained variance ratio $= \lambda_j / \sum\lambda$; keep enough components for e.g. 95%.
  • Centre (and usually standardise) first; project with $Z = X_c W_k$.
  • PCA is linear; t-SNE/UMAP give non-linear pictures for exploration only.

15.Check your understanding

1. What is the first principal component?

PC1 is the direction of maximum variance — the eigenvector of the covariance matrix with the largest eigenvalue.

2. Eigenvalues are 6, 3 and 1. What fraction of the variance do the first two components explain?

$(6 + 3)/(6 + 3 + 1) = 9/10 = 90\%$.

3. Why standardise features before PCA?

PCA maximises variance; a feature in millimetres has far more raw variance than the same feature in metres.

4. $\Sigma\mathbf{v} = 3\mathbf{v}$. What is $\mathbf{v}$ and what is 3?

The matrix only stretches $\mathbf{v}$ by the factor 3, so $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3.

5. A t-SNE plot shows one big cluster and one small one. What can you conclude about their real sizes?

t-SNE expands dense clusters and contracts sparse ones, so visual sizes aren't trustworthy.
