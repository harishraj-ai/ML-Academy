1.A classifier built from Bayes' theorem

In Lesson 4 you used Bayes' theorem to update a belief given evidence: a positive test changed the probability of disease. Naive Bayes turns that idea into a classifier. The "evidence" is the example's features; the "hypothesis" is the class.

It's one of the oldest and simplest ML methods — the classic spam filters of the early 2000s were Naive Bayes — and it's still useful today: it trains almost instantly, needs little data, and works surprisingly well on text. In this lesson you'll build a spam filter completely by hand.

2.Bayes' theorem for classification

We want the probability of each class $y$ given the features $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_d)$:

$$P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{P(y)\;P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)}{P(\mathbf{x})}$$
  • $P(y)$ — the prior: how common the class is overall (e.g. 40% of emails are spam).
  • $P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$ — the likelihood: how probable these features are for this class.
  • $P(\mathbf{x})$ — the evidence: the same for every class, so for comparing classes we can ignore it.

So the prediction is the class with the largest $P(y)\,P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$. We can always divide by the sum over classes at the end to get proper probabilities.

3.The "naive" assumption

The hard part is $P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$: the probability of a whole combination of features. An email has thousands of possible words, and we'll never see most combinations even once, so we can't count them.

Naive Bayes makes a bold simplification: assume the features are independent of each other, given the class. Then the joint probability is just a product (the multiplication rule from Lesson 4):

$$P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d \mid y) = P(x_1 \mid y)\,P(x_2 \mid y)\cdots P(x_d \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid y)$$

which gives the Naive Bayes classifier:

$$\hat{y} = \arg\max_{y}\; P(y)\prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid y)$$

($\arg\max_y$ means "the value of $y$ that makes this largest"; $\prod$ means "multiply together", like $\sum$ means "add up".)

Why "naive"?

The assumption is usually false. In real emails, "free" and "money" tend to appear together — they're not independent. Naive Bayes ignores that. Yet it often classifies well anyway, because it only needs to get the ranking of classes right, not the exact probabilities. Its probability estimates, however, tend to be over-confident (too close to 0 or 1).

Step-by-step derivationFrom Bayes' theorem to the Naive Bayes rule
  1. $$P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{P(y)\,P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)}{P(\mathbf{x})}$$

    Bayes' theorem for a class $y$ and features $\mathbf{x}$.

  2. $$\hat{y} = \arg\max_y\ P(y)\,P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$$

    $P(\mathbf{x})$ is the same for every class, so it can't change which class wins.

  3. $$P(\mathbf{x} \mid y) = \prod_{j} P(x_j \mid y)$$

    The naive assumption: features independent given the class.

  4. $$\hat{y} = \arg\max_y\ P(y)\prod_{j} P(x_j \mid y)$$

    Substitute.

  5. $$\hat{y} = \arg\max_y\ \Big[\ln P(y) + \sum_j \ln P(x_j \mid y)\Big]$$

    Take logs: the logarithm only ever increases, so the winner is the same — and sums don't underflow.

4.Worked example: a spam filter by hand

Our tiny training set: 10 emails, 4 spam and 6 not spam ("ham").

Spam (4)Ham (6)
win money nowmeeting at noon
free money offerlunch with the team now
win a free prize nowproject meeting notes
claim your free prizesee you at lunch
your project report is ready
notes from the team meeting

Task: is the new email "free money now" spam?

Step 1 — priors

$P(\text{spam}) = 4/10 = 0.4$, $\quad P(\text{ham}) = 6/10 = 0.6$.

Step 2 — word probabilities (likelihoods)

We treat each email as a "bag of words" and count how often each word appears in each class. The spam emails contain 15 words in total; the ham emails contain 25. There are 24 different words in the vocabulary overall.

The probability of a word given a class is its count divided by the total word count of that class. But there's a trap: "free" never appears in ham, so $P(\text{free} \mid \text{ham}) = 0/25 = 0$ — and since we multiply, a single zero would make the whole ham probability zero, no matter what the other words say. One unseen word shouldn't be able to veto everything.

Laplace (add-one) smoothing

Pretend every vocabulary word was seen once more than it really was:

$$P(w \mid y) = \frac{\text{count}(w, y) + 1}{\text{total words in } y + |V|}$$

where $|V|$ is the vocabulary size (24). This guarantees no probability is ever zero. (In scikit-learn this "+1" is the parameter alpha=1.0.)

WordCount in spamP(w | spam)Count in hamP(w | ham)
free3$\frac{3+1}{15+24} = \frac{4}{39} = 0.1026$0$\frac{0+1}{25+24} = \frac{1}{49} = 0.0204$
money2$\frac{3}{39} = 0.0769$0$\frac{1}{49} = 0.0204$
now2$\frac{3}{39} = 0.0769$1$\frac{2}{49} = 0.0408$

Step 3 — multiply

$$\text{spam score} = 0.4 \times 0.1026 \times 0.0769 \times 0.0769 \approx 0.000243$$ $$\text{ham score} = 0.6 \times 0.0204 \times 0.0204 \times 0.0408 \approx 0.0000102$$

Step 4 — normalise into probabilities

$$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free money now"}) = \frac{0.000243}{0.000243 + 0.0000102} \approx 0.96$$

96% spam. Notice how each word contributed: "free" is about 5× more likely in spam than ham, "money" about 3.8×, and "now" about 1.9× — together they overwhelm the prior, which favoured ham.

5.Working with logs

In plain wordsMultiplying many small probabilities gives numbers too small for a computer to store, so in practice we add their logarithms instead. The winning class stays the same.

A real email has hundreds of words. Multiplying hundreds of numbers like 0.001 gives results so small that computers round them to exactly 0 (underflow). The fix: take logarithms. Since $\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$, products become sums:

$$\ln\!\Big(P(y)\prod_j P(x_j \mid y)\Big) = \ln P(y) + \sum_j \ln P(x_j \mid y)$$

The logarithm keeps the ordering (bigger stays bigger), so we can compare the log-scores directly. Every Naive Bayes implementation works this way.

Worked example — the same email in log space

Spam: $\ln 0.4 + \ln 0.1026 + \ln 0.0769 + \ln 0.0769 \approx -0.916 - 2.277 - 2.565 - 2.565 = -8.32$.

Ham: $\ln 0.6 + \ln 0.0204 + \ln 0.0204 + \ln 0.0408 \approx -0.511 - 3.892 - 3.892 - 3.199 = -11.49$.

Spam's log-score is higher (less negative) → predict spam. The difference, $-8.32 - (-11.49) = 3.17$, means spam is $e^{3.17} \approx 24$ times more likely, i.e. $24/25 = 0.96$. Same answer.

Make sense of it

The situation: A real spam filter multiplies hundreds of small probabilities. Computers can't store numbers that tiny, so they add logarithms instead.

What the result tells you: The log scores −8.32 (spam) and −11.49 (ham) differ by 3.17, so spam is $e^{3.17} \approx 24$ times more likely: a 96% chance.

What if…?

  • The email also says "meeting" (3 times in ham, never in spam): the chance of spam falls to 88%. One ham-like word pulls it back.
  • "free" appears twice: its evidence counts twice, and the chance rises to 99.2%.
  • Spam is rarer (prior 0.1 instead of 0.4): the same words now give 80%. The prior matters.
  • No smoothing: "free" has probability 0 in ham, the ham score is 0, and the email is 100% spam whatever else it says.

Why it matters in ML: Each word adds its own log-evidence for or against a class, so the model is easy to explain: you can list the words that pushed the decision. The "naive" independence assumption makes repeated or correlated words over-count.

Advanced · optionalNormalising log-scores safely: the log-sum-exp trick

To turn class log-scores $a_k$ into probabilities you need $P(k) = e^{a_k}/\sum_j e^{a_j}$, but $e^{a_k}$ underflows to 0 when $a_k$ is around −800. Subtract the largest score $m = \max_j a_j$ first:

$$\log\sum_j e^{a_j} = m + \log\sum_j e^{a_j - m}, \qquad P(k) = e^{\,a_k - m}\Big/\sum_j e^{a_j - m}.$$

Every exponent is now ≤ 0 and the largest is exactly $e^0 = 1$, so nothing overflows and the sum is at least 1. Example: scores $-1000$ and $-1001$ would both underflow, but after subtracting $m = -1000$ they become $0$ and $-1$, giving probabilities $1/(1+e^{-1}) \approx 0.731$ and $0.269$. The same trick sits inside every softmax in Lessons 18–19.

7.Variants for different kinds of features

In plain wordsDifferent kinds of features need different versions: word counts (Multinomial), yes/no features (Bernoulli) or measurements like height (Gaussian, which assumes a bell curve for each class).

The formula $P(y)\prod_j P(x_j \mid y)$ is always the same; what changes is how $P(x_j \mid y)$ is modelled.

VariantFeatures$P(x_j \mid y)$ comes fromTypical use
Multinomial NBCountsSmoothed relative frequencies (as above)Text classification
Bernoulli NBBinary (present / absent)Fraction of examples where the feature is 1Short texts, yes/no features
Gaussian NBContinuous numbersA normal distribution per class and featureMeasurements
Complement NBCountsStatistics from all other classesImbalanced text data
Categorical NBCategoriesSmoothed frequency of each categoryCategorical data

Gaussian Naive Bayes

For continuous features, assume each feature follows a normal distribution (Lesson 4) within each class, with its own mean $\mu_{y}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{y}$ estimated from the training data:

$$P(x_j \mid y) = \frac{1}{\sigma_{y}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x_j - \mu_{y})^2}{2\sigma_{y}^2}\right)$$
Worked example — apple or orange?

From training data: apples weigh on average $\mu = 150$ g ($\sigma = 20$); oranges $\mu = 200$ g ($\sigma = 25$). Equal priors (0.5 each). A fruit weighs 170 g.

Apple: $z = (170 - 150)/20 = 1$. Density $= \frac{1}{20\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-1^2/2} \approx 0.01995 \times 0.607 \approx 0.0121$.

Orange: $z = (170 - 200)/25 = -1.2$. Density $= \frac{1}{25\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-1.44/2} \approx 0.01596 \times 0.487 \approx 0.0078$.

$P(\text{apple} \mid 170\text{ g}) = \frac{0.5 \times 0.0121}{0.5 \times 0.0121 + 0.5 \times 0.0078} \approx 0.61$.

A 61% chance it's an apple — 170 g is closer to the apple average, measured in standard deviations. With more features (colour, diameter…) we'd multiply in more densities.

Make sense of it

The situation: A fruit-sorting machine on a conveyor belt weighs each fruit and must decide: apple or orange?

What the result tells you: 170 g is 1 standard deviation above the apple average but 1.2 below the orange average, so it's a bit more apple-like: 61%.

What if…?

  • 160 g: 80% apple. 175 g: 49% — almost a coin toss. 185 g: 25%. 200 g: 5%.
  • 80% of the fruit are apples (prior 0.8): a 170 g fruit becomes 86% apple, and even 185 g is 56% apple. A strong prior shifts the boundary.

Why it matters in ML: Gaussian Naive Bayes turns each feature into a likelihood using its mean and standard deviation, then multiplies. It trains instantly and gives probabilities — a good first baseline.

9.In Python

The spam filter from this lesson, with scikit-learn. CountVectorizer turns each text into word counts:

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

spam = ["win money now", "free money offer", "win a free prize now", "claim your free prize"]
ham = ["meeting at noon", "lunch with the team now", "project meeting notes",
       "see you at lunch", "your project report is ready", "notes from the team meeting"]

vec = CountVectorizer(token_pattern=r"(?u)\b\w+\b")   # keep 1-letter words like "a"
X = vec.fit_transform(spam + ham)
y = [1] * 4 + [0] * 6                                   # 1 = spam, 0 = ham

nb = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0).fit(X, y)                 # alpha=1 is Laplace smoothing
tests = ["free money now", "meeting notes for the team", "win lunch now"]
print(nb.predict_proba(vec.transform(tests)).round(3))  # columns: [P(ham), P(spam)]

Output:

[[0.04  0.96 ]
 [0.985 0.015]
 [0.335 0.665]]

The first row matches our hand calculation exactly: 96% spam. "win lunch now" is mixed — "win" points to spam, "lunch" to ham — giving 67% spam.

Gaussian NB on the wine dataset from Lesson 11:

from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB

X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)
print(round(GaussianNB().fit(X_train, y_train).score(X_test, y_test), 3))   # 0.956

95.6% accuracy with no scaling and no tuning, trained in milliseconds — better than kNN's 93.3% on the same split.

10.Running example: Chennai Homes

Naive Bayes on the listing text alone: words like "new" and "metro" push towards selling fast, "old" and "renovation" away. "New flat near metro" scores 0.96; "old villa needs renovation" just 0.02; "quiet flat with garden" is mixed (0.59).

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
vec = CountVectorizer()
Xw = vec.fit_transform(df["description"])
nb = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0).fit(Xw, df["sold_fast"])
for text in ["new flat near metro", "old villa needs renovation", "quiet flat with garden"]:
    print(f"{text:28s} P(sells fast) = {nb.predict_proba(vec.transform([text]))[0, 1]:.2f}")

Where this is used
  • Spam and sentiment classification
  • Document categorisation
  • A very fast baseline for text

Choose it when: Text or many features, little data, and when speed matters.

Think twice when: Features strongly interact, or you need well-calibrated probabilities.

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythNaive Bayes needs the features to really be independent.

RealityIt often works well even when they aren't — it only needs the right class to score highest.

MythNaive Bayes probabilities are reliable confidence levels.

RealityThey tend to be pushed towards 0 and 1. Use them for ranking, not as exact confidence.

Responsible AI · Language models of who is "normal"

Text classifiers learn which words go with which label. Sap et al. (2019) found that tweets written in African-American English were far more likely to be labelled offensive by widely used toxicity datasets and the models trained on them. A spam or moderation filter can silence some groups more than others; test it on varied dialects and writing styles before trusting it.

11.Questions beginners often ask

If the independence assumption is wrong, why does it work?

Because classification only needs the correct class to score highest. Errors in the exact probabilities often don't change which class wins.

Which variant should I use?

Multinomial for word counts, Bernoulli for present/absent features, Gaussian for continuous measurements.

Is Naive Bayes still used today?

Yes — as a fast baseline, for text classification with little data, and wherever training must be nearly instant.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Forgetting smoothing, so one unseen word sets a class's probability to zero.
  • Multiplying many raw probabilities instead of adding log-probabilities (underflow).
  • Trusting Naive Bayes probabilities as well-calibrated confidence levels.
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • Generative vs discriminative models — Describe each class vs draw the line between them.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

Why is Naive Bayes called "naive", and why does it still work?

The trap: Thinking the assumption must hold for it to work.

It assumes features are independent given the class, which is rarely true (words like "free" and "prize" go together). It still classifies well because it only needs the right class to score highest, not accurate probabilities — the ranking often survives the wrong assumption.

Can you trust Naive Bayes probabilities?

The trap: Reporting them as calibrated.

Usually not. Counting correlated evidence several times pushes probabilities towards 0 or 1 — in Lesson 12, repeating "free" lifted the spam probability from 96% to 99.2%. Use it for ranking or decisions, and calibrate if you need probabilities.

A word never appeared in any ham email during training. What happens without smoothing?

The trap: Thinking one word can't matter much.

Its estimated probability in ham is 0, so the whole ham score is 0 and the email is classified as spam with certainty, whatever its other words say. Laplace (add-one) smoothing prevents this.

Why do implementations add log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities?

The trap: "It's just a convention."

Multiplying hundreds of small numbers underflows to 0 in floating point. Logs turn the product into a sum of moderate numbers, and the class ranking is unchanged because log is increasing.

12.Key takeaways

  • Naive Bayes picks the class maximising $P(y)\prod_j P(x_j \mid y)$ — Bayes' theorem plus an independence assumption.
  • The "naive" independence assumption is usually wrong, but the classifier often works well anyway.
  • Laplace smoothing (+1 counts) prevents a single unseen word from zeroing out a class.
  • Implementations add log-probabilities instead of multiplying, to avoid underflow.
  • Multinomial NB for word counts, Bernoulli for yes/no features, Gaussian for continuous measurements.

13.Check your understanding

1. What does the "naive" assumption say?

Conditional independence lets us multiply individual feature probabilities instead of modelling every combination.

2. Why is Laplace smoothing needed?

Without smoothing, one zero factor in the product wipes out all the other evidence.

3. A class has 20 words in total, the vocabulary has 30 words, and "deal" appears 4 times in the class. With add-one smoothing, $P(\text{deal} \mid \text{class})$ is…

$(4 + 1)/(20 + 30) = 5/50 = 0.1$.

4. Why do implementations add log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities?

Products of hundreds of small probabilities become too small to store. Logs turn them into sums of manageable numbers, without changing the ranking.

5. Which variant suits features like height and weight?

Continuous measurements are modelled with a normal (Gaussian) distribution per class.

14.Going deeper: smoothing, text pipelines and generative models

Intermediate · skip on a first read

Naive Bayes is still a strong, fast baseline for text. This section explains where its smoothing rule really comes from, how to read what it has learned, how a real text pipeline is built (TF-IDF and word pairs), and the big idea it represents: generative models, which learn how each class produces data, compared with discriminative models such as logistic regression, which learn the boundary directly.

You'll need: this lesson, and Bayesian estimation with priors from Lesson 4's "Going deeper".

Where smoothing comes from

Without smoothing, a word never seen in one class gets probability 0 for that class, and a single such word wipes out all the other evidence. In this tiny spam example, both new messages contain words that appeared in only one class:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

texts = ["win cash prize now", "cheap meds win big", "claim your free prize", "free cash offer now",
         "meeting moved to monday", "lunch on monday?", "project report attached", "see you at the meeting"]
labels = np.array([1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0])                # 1 = spam
vec = CountVectorizer().fit(texts)
Xc = vec.transform(texts)
for alpha in [1e-10, 1.0]:
    nb = MultinomialNB(alpha=alpha).fit(Xc, labels)
    p = nb.predict_proba(vec.transform(["free lunch on monday", "win a free meeting"]))[:, 1]
    print(f"alpha {alpha:g}: P(spam) for the two new messages {p.round(3)}")

Output:

alpha 1e-10: P(spam) for the two new messages [0. 1.]
alpha 1: P(spam) for the two new messages [0.184 0.735]

With essentially no smoothing ($\alpha = 10^{-10}$) the answers are absurdly certain, exactly 0 and 1, decided by one unseen word each. With $\alpha = 1$ (Laplace smoothing) they become sensible: 0.184 and 0.735. The smoothing formula,

$$P(\text{word } j \mid c) = \frac{\text{count}_{j,c} + \alpha}{\text{total}_c + \alpha V}$$

where $V$ is the vocabulary size, is not an arbitrary fix. It is the Bayesian posterior mean when the word probabilities get a symmetric Dirichlet prior, which is the many-category version of the Beta prior in Lesson 4's "Going deeper". Adding $\alpha$ to every count is like having seen every word $\alpha$ extra times before training. $\alpha$ is a regularization strength: tune it by cross-validation (values between 0.01 and 1 are common for text).

Reading what the model learned

Naive Bayes is easy to interpret. For each word, the log-ratio $\log P(\text{word}\mid\text{spam}) - \log P(\text{word}\mid\text{ham})$ is exactly how much that word adds to the spam score each time it appears:

# Which words push towards spam? The log-ratio log P(word | spam) − log P(word | ham)
nb = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0).fit(Xc, labels)
ratio = nb.feature_log_prob_[1] - nb.feature_log_prob_[0]
words = vec.get_feature_names_out()
order = np.argsort(ratio)
print("most spam-like:", [(str(words[i]), float(round(ratio[i], 2))) for i in order[::-1][:4]])
print("most ham-like: ", [(str(words[i]), float(round(ratio[i], 2))) for i in order[:4]])

Output:

most spam-like: [('win', 1.07), ('cash', 1.07), ('now', 1.07), ('prize', 1.07)]
most ham-like:  [('monday', -1.12), ('meeting', -1.12), ('at', -0.72), ('attached', -0.72)]

"win", "cash", "now" and "prize" each add 1.07 to the log-odds of spam; "monday" and "meeting" subtract 1.12. Notice the classifier is linear in the word counts: the log-odds is a weighted sum of counts plus a constant (the log prior ratio). Multinomial naive Bayes is a linear classifier, just like logistic regression, but with its weights set by counting rather than by optimisation.

A realistic text pipeline

Real text classifiers usually add three things:

  • Word pairs (bigrams), so "not good" isn't treated as "not" plus "good".
  • TF-IDF weighting: term frequency × inverse document frequency. Words that appear in almost every document ("the", "to") are down-weighted, and distinctive words count more.
  • Complement naive Bayes: estimates each class's word statistics from all the other classes. It's more stable when classes are imbalanced, and often beats standard multinomial NB on text.
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.naive_bayes import ComplementNB

# A text pipeline: words and word pairs, TF-IDF weighting, then naive Bayes
pipe = make_pipeline(TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2)), ComplementNB(alpha=0.5)).fit(texts, labels)
for msg in ["claim your cash now", "report for the monday meeting"]:
    print(f"{msg!r:34} -> {'spam' if pipe.predict([msg])[0] else 'ham'}")
print("number of features (words and word pairs):", len(pipe[0].get_feature_names_out()))

Output:

'claim your cash now'              -> spam
'report for the monday meeting'    -> ham
number of features (words and word pairs): 47

Even on 8 training messages the pipeline classifies both new ones correctly, using 47 features (words plus word pairs). On real datasets with thousands of documents, this kind of pipeline trains in seconds and is a hard baseline to beat without much more expensive models. Try a linear SVM or logistic regression on the same TF-IDF features too; on text, they're often slightly more accurate. Modern alternatives replace TF-IDF with learned embeddings or a fine-tuned transformer (Lesson 19).

Generative vs discriminative models

Naive Bayes is a generative model: it learns $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$ and $P(y)$, a story of how each class produces data, and then uses Bayes' theorem to get $P(y\mid\mathbf{x})$. Logistic regression is discriminative: it models $P(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ directly and never describes $\mathbf{x}$ itself. The two can be exact counterparts. Gaussian naive Bayes with a shared variance per feature produces a log-odds that is linear in $\mathbf{x}$, the same form as logistic regression.

A well-known analysis (Ng and Jordan, 2001) showed that naive Bayes approaches its own best error with far fewer examples (roughly logarithmic in the number of features), while logistic regression needs more (roughly linear) but ends lower when the independence assumption is wrong. In practice: with very few labelled examples, or as a quick baseline, try naive Bayes; with plenty of data, a discriminative model usually wins. Today's generative AI (Lesson 19) is the generative idea at enormous scale: models of $P(\mathbf{x})$ that can produce new text or images.

Interview corner · intermediate

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

Naive Bayes vs logistic regression for text: how do you choose?

The trap: Saying one is always better.

Naive Bayes trains by counting, needs very little data to reach its best accuracy, and handles huge vocabularies cheaply, but its independence assumption double-counts correlated words, so its probabilities are over-confident and its final accuracy is often lower. Logistic regression (or a linear SVM) on the same TF-IDF features usually wins with enough data and gives better-calibrated probabilities. Try both: they're cheap. NB is great as a baseline, for tiny datasets, or for streaming updates.

What is TF-IDF and why use it instead of raw counts?

The trap: Only giving the acronym.

Term frequency × inverse document frequency: a word's count in a document, multiplied by $\log(N/\text{df})$, where df is the number of documents containing it (implementations add smoothing). Very common words get weights near 0; distinctive words get more. Vectors are usually normalised to unit length, so long documents don't dominate. It gives linear models and kNN a much better representation than raw counts.

Why is the smoothing parameter α a regularization hyperparameter?

The trap: Thinking it only avoids division by zero.

It pulls every class's word distribution towards uniform, like a prior. A large α means estimates trust the prior more, giving simpler, less confident models (more bias, less variance); a tiny α trusts sparse counts, giving extreme, overfit probabilities. So it's tuned by cross-validation like any regularization strength.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. Laplace smoothing in naive Bayes is the same as…

It's the posterior mean under a symmetric Dirichlet prior.

2. What does TF-IDF do to a word that appears in almost every document?

The inverse-document-frequency factor $\log(N/\text{df})$ is near 0 for very common words.

3. Multinomial naive Bayes's decision rule is…

The log-odds is a constant plus a sum of (log-ratio × count) over words.

4. Which model is generative?

It models $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$ for each class, then applies Bayes' theorem.

5. With only 30 labelled examples and 1,000 word features, a quick strong baseline is…

Generative models like naive Bayes typically reach their best accuracy with far fewer examples.
