Before you start

This lesson builds on:

  • Lesson 2: Vectors & Matrices — A vector is a list of numbers; a dataset $X$ is a matrix with one row per example. The dot product $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} = \sum w_ix_i$ is how linear models predict, and $\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|$ is the distance between two examples.
  • Lesson 11: k-Nearest Neighbours — kNN predicts from the $k$ most similar training examples, so it depends on distances — features must be scaled, and it struggles in very high dimensions.

Where this fits: Starts Part 4: learning without labels. Instead of predicting answers, we discover groups in the data.

1.Learning without labels

Picture itA shop owner looks at what people buy and notices groups: families buying in bulk, students buying snacks, office workers buying lunch. Nobody labelled them — the groups emerged from the data. That's clustering.

Every model so far learned from labelled examples: houses with prices, emails marked spam. But most of the world's data has no labels. A shop has millions of purchase records; nobody has tagged each customer as "bargain hunter" or "loyal regular". Clustering finds natural groups in data on its own — examples within a group are similar to each other and different from those in other groups.

Uses: customer segmentation, grouping news articles by topic, organising photos, finding unusual activity (points that fit no group), and compressing images by reducing them to a few colours.

Analogy: sorting laundry

Nobody tells you the categories, but looking at a pile of clothes you naturally form groups: whites, darks, colours. You're clustering by similarity. Different people might sort slightly differently — clustering has no single "right answer", which makes it both powerful and tricky to evaluate.

2.k-means: the classic algorithm

k-means splits the data into exactly $k$ clusters (you choose $k$). Each cluster is represented by its centroid $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j$ — the average of its points. The goal is to make points close to their own centroid, measured by the inertia (within-cluster sum of squares):

$$\text{inertia} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\ \min_{j}\ \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|^2$$

"For each point, the squared distance to its nearest centroid; add them all up." Lower is better.

Lloyd's algorithm

  1. Initialise: pick $k$ starting centroids (e.g. $k$ random data points).
  2. Assign: put each point in the cluster of its nearest centroid.
  3. Update: move each centroid to the mean of the points assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2–3 until the assignments stop changing.

Each step can only lower (or keep) the inertia, so the algorithm always converges — usually within a few iterations.

Worked example — k-means by hand in 1D

Points: $1, 2, 3, 8, 9, 10$. $k = 2$. A deliberately bad start: centroids $\mu_1 = 1$, $\mu_2 = 2$.

Iteration 1 — assign: point 1 is nearest $\mu_1$; points 2, 3, 8, 9, 10 are nearest (or tied with) $\mu_2$. Clusters: $\{1\}$ and $\{2, 3, 8, 9, 10\}$.
Update: $\mu_1 = 1$, $\mu_2 = (2+3+8+9+10)/5 = 6.4$.

Iteration 2 — assign: point 2: distance 1 to $\mu_1$, 4.4 to $\mu_2$ → cluster 1. Point 3: 2 vs 3.4 → cluster 1. Points 8, 9, 10 → cluster 2. Clusters: $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and $\{8, 9, 10\}$.
Update: $\mu_1 = 2$, $\mu_2 = 9$.

Iteration 3: assignments don't change → converged.

Inertia: $(1-2)^2 + 0 + (3-2)^2 + (8-9)^2 + 0 + (10-9)^2 = 4$.

Make sense of it

The situation: A delivery company has customers along one road at kilometres 1, 2, 3, 8, 9 and 10, and wants two depots. Where should they go?

What the result tells you: k-means puts the depots at 2 and 9 — the middle of each group of customers. The inertia, 4, is the total squared distance from every customer to their depot.

What if…?

  • A sensible start (depots at 1 and 10): it reaches the same answer in one round instead of two.
  • Three depots, started at 2, 8 and 10: groups {1, 2, 3}, {8, 9}, {10}, and the inertia falls to 2.5. More clusters always lower the inertia — six depots would make it 0 — so inertia alone can't choose $k$.
  • Three depots, started at 1, 5 and 10: the middle depot attracts no customers and stays empty, and the inertia stays at 4. A bad start can waste a cluster — which is why k-means++ and several restarts are used.
  • A far-away customer at 50 with two depots: one depot moves out to serve that single customer, the other has to cover 1–10 from 5.5, and the inertia leaps to 77.5. Outliers can grab a whole cluster.

Why it matters in ML: k-means minimises squared distances, so like the mean it is pulled by outliers, depends on its starting points and on feature scale, and always returns exactly $k$ groups whether or not they really exist.

Step-by-step derivationWhy the centroid update uses the mean
  1. $$J(\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{i \in \text{cluster}} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}\|^2$$

    The inertia contributed by one cluster, as a function of its centroid.

  2. $$\nabla J = -2\sum_{i \in \text{cluster}} (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu})$$

    Differentiate (chain rule, as in Lesson 7).

  3. $$\sum_i \mathbf{x}_i - m\,\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}$$

    Set to zero; $m$ is the number of points in the cluster.

  4. $$\boldsymbol{\mu} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i \mathbf{x}_i$$

    The best centroid is exactly the mean — so the update step can only lower the inertia.

Advanced · optionalWhy k-means always stops

Let $J = \sum_i \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c(i)}\|^2$ be the inertia for assignments $c$ and centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}$.

  • Assign step: moving each point to its nearest centroid can only lower or keep its own term, so $J$ does not increase.
  • Update step: for a fixed cluster, $\sum_{i\in C}\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{m}\|^2$ is minimised by $\mathbf{m}$ = the mean (set its gradient $-2\sum_{i\in C}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{m})$ to zero). So $J$ does not increase.

$J$ never goes up, and there are only finitely many ways to assign $n$ points to $k$ clusters, so the algorithm cannot cycle and must stop. What's not guaranteed is the global minimum — finding that is NP-hard in general, which is why k-means++ starts and several restarts help.

4.Initialisation matters: k-means++

In plain wordsk-means can land in a poor solution depending on where it starts. Starting with spread-out centroids (k-means++) and running it several times fixes this.

Lloyd's algorithm always converges, but possibly to a local minimum — a poor clustering that a different start would beat. For example, two centroids might start inside the same natural group and split it, while another group gets merged with its neighbour.

Two standard fixes:

  • k-means++ initialisation: pick the first centroid at random, then pick each next one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centroid already chosen. Starting centroids end up spread out. It's scikit-learn's default.
  • Multiple restarts (n_init): run k-means several times from different starts and keep the result with the lowest inertia.

5.Choosing $k$

Inertia always decreases as $k$ grows — with $k = n$, every point is its own cluster and inertia is 0. So we can't just minimise it. Two common guides:

The elbow method

Plot inertia against $k$. It drops steeply while $k$ is below the true number of groups, then flattens. The "elbow" where it bends is a good choice.

The silhouette score

For each point $i$:

  • $a(i)$ = average distance to the other points in its own cluster (how tight its cluster is);
  • $b(i)$ = average distance to the points of the nearest other cluster (how far the next cluster is).
$$s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\big(a(i),\ b(i)\big)}$$

$s$ ranges from $-1$ to $1$: near 1 means well inside its cluster and far from others; near 0 means on a border; negative means probably in the wrong cluster. The silhouette score is the average over all points; choose the $k$ that maximises it.

Worked example

A point's average distance to its own cluster is $a = 1$, and to the nearest other cluster $b = 4$. Then $s = (4 - 1)/\max(1, 4) = 3/4 = 0.75$ — clearly well placed.

If instead $a = 3$ and $b = 2$: $s = (2 - 3)/3 \approx -0.33$ — it's closer to the other cluster, so it's probably misassigned.

Make sense of it

The situation: Checking whether each customer really belongs with their depot or would be better served by the next one.

What the result tells you: 0.75 means the point is much closer to its own cluster than to the next one. A negative value means it's closer to another cluster — probably in the wrong one.

What if…?

  • a = 0.5, b = 4: $s = 0.875$ — even better placed.
  • a = b: $s = 0$ — right on the border between two clusters.

Why it matters in ML: The average silhouette over all points is a common way to compare different values of $k$: higher is better. Look at the individual values too — a cluster full of near-zero scores may not be a real group.

6.Limitations of k-means

  • You must choose $k$ in advance.
  • It assumes clusters are roughly round (spherical) and similar in size — because it assigns by distance to a centre. It fails on elongated, curved or nested shapes.
  • It's sensitive to feature scales (use standardisation) and to outliers (they pull centroids).
  • Every point is forced into some cluster, even outliers that belong nowhere.

7.Hierarchical clustering

Agglomerative clustering builds clusters bottom-up:

  1. Start with every point as its own cluster.
  2. Merge the two closest clusters.
  3. Repeat until one cluster remains.

The full history of merges forms a tree called a dendrogram. Cutting it at a chosen height gives any number of clusters — you don't have to pick $k$ before running it. "Closest clusters" needs a definition, called the linkage:

LinkageDistance between clustersTends to give
SingleClosest pair of pointsLong chains; follows curved shapes
CompleteFarthest pair of pointsCompact clusters
AverageAverage over all pairsA compromise
WardMerge that increases total within-cluster variance the leastSimilar-sized round clusters, like k-means (scikit-learn default)

The cost: it needs distances between all pairs of points, so it's slow for very large datasets.

8.DBSCAN: clusters as dense regions

DBSCAN (density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise) defines clusters as regions where points are packed closely together, separated by sparse regions. It has two settings:

  • eps ($\varepsilon$) — the radius of a point's neighbourhood;
  • min_samples — how many points must be within that radius for the region to count as dense.

Each point becomes one of three types:

  • Core point: has at least min_samples points (counting itself, in scikit-learn) within distance $\varepsilon$.
  • Border point: not a core point itself, but within $\varepsilon$ of one.
  • Noise: neither — an outlier, labelled $-1$.

Clusters grow by connecting core points that are within $\varepsilon$ of each other, then adding their border points.

Why DBSCAN is useful

It finds clusters of any shape, works out the number of clusters itself, and explicitly flags outliers instead of forcing them into a cluster. Its weakness: a single $\varepsilon$ struggles when clusters have very different densities. HDBSCAN extends it to handle varying densities.

9.How do you know a clustering is good?

Without labels, there's no accuracy to compute. Options:

  • Internal scores like silhouette — measure tightness and separation, but favour round clusters.
  • External scores when some true labels exist for checking, such as the adjusted Rand index (ARI): 1 = identical to the true groups, about 0 = no better than random.
  • Usefulness: do the clusters make sense to domain experts and help the actual task?

10.In Python

Choosing $k$ on data with 4 hidden groups:

from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs, make_moons
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN, AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, adjusted_rand_score
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y_true = make_blobs(n_samples=500, centers=4, cluster_std=1.0, random_state=42)
for k in range(2, 8):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(f"k={k}  inertia={km.inertia_:8.1f}  silhouette={silhouette_score(X, km.labels_):.3f}")

Inertia keeps falling, but its big drops stop after $k = 4$ (the elbow), and the silhouette score peaks at $k = 4$. Both point to the true number of groups.

Curved clusters — where k-means struggles:

Xm, ym = make_moons(n_samples=300, noise=0.06, random_state=0)
Xm = StandardScaler().fit_transform(Xm)

km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(Xm)
db = DBSCAN(eps=0.3, min_samples=5).fit(Xm)
ag = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=2, linkage="single").fit(Xm)

print("k-means ARI:", round(adjusted_rand_score(ym, km.labels_), 3))
print("DBSCAN  ARI:", round(adjusted_rand_score(ym, db.labels_), 3),
      " clusters:", len(set(db.labels_) - {-1}), " noise points:", list(db.labels_).count(-1))
print("single-linkage ARI:", round(adjusted_rand_score(ym, ag.labels_), 3))
k-means ARI: 0.47
DBSCAN  ARI: 0.993  clusters: 2  noise points: 1
single-linkage ARI: 1.0

k-means only half-recovers the two moons (ARI 0.47), because it cuts them with a straight line. DBSCAN finds both moons almost perfectly, works out by itself that there are 2 clusters, and flags 1 point as noise. Single-linkage hierarchical clustering follows the curves exactly.

11.Running example: Chennai Homes

k-means with $k = 3$ on size, distance and price, without seeing the area labels, finds: small central-ish homes (cluster 0), big pricey ones (cluster 1) and far, cheap ones (cluster 2). Cluster 2 is exactly the Outskirts houses 9 and 10 — the algorithm rediscovered geography from the numbers.

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
cols = ["size_m2", "dist_km", "price_lakh"]
Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[cols])
km = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(Z)
df["cluster"] = km.labels_
print(df.groupby("cluster")[cols].mean().round(1))
print(pd.crosstab(df["cluster"], df["area"]))

Where this is used
  • Customer segmentation for marketing
  • Grouping documents or images by similarity
  • Finding unusual activity (points that fit no cluster)

Choose it when: You want to discover structure without labels.

Think twice when: You actually have labels for the task — use supervised learning.

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythClustering finds the true groups.

RealityIt finds groups according to its own definition of similarity, and it will return clusters even for random data.

Mythk-means works for any cluster shape.

RealityIt assumes roughly round, similar-sized clusters; curved or nested shapes need DBSCAN or hierarchical clustering.

Responsible AI · Clusters are not natural categories

k-means will always return k groups, whether or not real groups exist. Giving clusters labels like "low-value customers" can become self-fulfilling if they decide who gets offers or attention. Treat clusters as a description of this dataset under these features, and be careful when they line up with sensitive attributes.

12.Questions beginners often ask

How do I know the clusters are meaningful?

Check silhouette scores, look at examples from each cluster, and ask whether they're useful for your goal. Clustering always returns clusters, even in random data.

k-means or DBSCAN?

k-means for roughly round clusters when you know (or can choose) k. DBSCAN for irregular shapes, unknown k and when you want outliers flagged.

Why do I get different results each run?

k-means depends on its random starting centroids. Use k-means++ and several restarts (n_init) and fix random_state for reproducibility.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Picking $k$ by minimising inertia (it always prefers more clusters).
  • Running k-means on unscaled features or on non-round cluster shapes.
  • Treating cluster labels as ground truth without checking they're meaningful.
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • Clustering vs classification — Known answer vs discovered groups.
  • Inertia vs silhouette score — Inertia can't choose k; silhouette can help.
  • k-means vs DBSCAN — Centres vs crowds.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

What shapes of cluster does k-means find well, and which does it fail on?

The trap: Thinking k-means finds any group.

It works best for compact, roughly spherical clusters of similar size, because it assigns each point to the nearest centre. It fails on elongated, ring-shaped or very unequal clusters — try DBSCAN, Gaussian mixtures or spectral clustering.

You run k-means twice and get different clusters. Is it broken?

The trap: Expecting a deterministic answer.

No. It converges to a local optimum that depends on the starting centroids. Use k-means++ initialisation, several restarts (n_init), and keep the run with the lowest inertia. Fix the random seed for reproducibility.

Why can't you choose $k$ by minimising inertia?

The trap: Picking the $k$ with the lowest inertia.

Inertia always falls as $k$ grows and reaches 0 when every point is its own cluster. Look for an "elbow", use the silhouette score, or — best — ask which grouping is useful for the purpose.

How do you evaluate clustering when there are no labels?

The trap: Saying "you can't".

Internal measures (silhouette, Davies–Bouldin), stability across resamples and random seeds, and above all usefulness: are the clusters interpretable and do they lead to different actions? If some labels exist, compare with them (adjusted Rand index).

13.Key takeaways

  • Clustering finds groups in unlabelled data.
  • k-means alternates "assign to nearest centroid" and "move centroid to mean", minimising inertia $\sum\|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c(i)}\|^2$.
  • Use k-means++ and several restarts; choose $k$ with the elbow method or the silhouette score $\frac{b-a}{\max(a,b)}$.
  • k-means assumes round clusters; hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN handle other shapes, and DBSCAN flags outliers.
  • Scale features first, and judge clusters by whether they're useful.

14.Check your understanding

1. In k-means, after assigning points, each centroid is moved to…

The update step sets each centroid to the average of its cluster, which minimises that cluster's sum of squared distances.

2. Why can't you choose $k$ by minimising inertia?

More clusters always fit at least as tightly, so inertia alone favours too many clusters. Look for the elbow or use silhouette.

3. A point has $a = 2$ and $b = 6$. What is its silhouette value?

$(6 - 2)/\max(2, 6) = 4/6 \approx 0.67$.

4. Your data has two interlocking crescent shapes and a few outliers. Which method fits best?

DBSCAN follows dense regions of any shape and labels outliers as noise.

5. In DBSCAN, what is a core point?

Core points sit in dense regions; clusters grow outwards from them.
