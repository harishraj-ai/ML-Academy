Beyond the course

What next: your learning plan

You've learned the foundations. Here's how to turn them into real skill: choose a direction, follow a 12-week plan, build projects and keep learning.

What you can do now

If you have worked through the course, you can already do a lot:

  • Read the maths behind most ML papers' method sections: vectors, gradients, probability, loss functions (Lessons 1–4).
  • Run an honest ML project: split data correctly, cross-validate, choose a metric that fits the real cost of mistakes, and avoid leakage (Lessons 5, 9, 20).
  • Choose and tune a model from linear and logistic regression to trees, forests, boosting, SVMs and kNN, and explain why it works (Lessons 6–15).
  • Find structure without labels with clustering and PCA (Lessons 16–17).
  • Understand neural networks down to backpropagation, and how CNNs, RNNs and Transformers are built (Lessons 18–19).
  • Audit a model for fairness and document it responsibly (Lesson 21).

The next step is to build things on real data. Reading more is useful, but projects are where the ideas stick.

A 12-week plan (about 6–8 hours a week)

  1. Weeks 1–2 · Consolidate. Finish the end-of-part tests and clear your review queue. Redo any lesson where you scored below 80%.
  2. Weeks 3–4 · First real project. Pick a tabular dataset (Kaggle, the UCI repository or open government data). Do the full Lesson 20 workflow: explore, baseline, pipeline, cross-validation, test set once, short write-up.
  3. Weeks 5–6 · Go deeper on one family. Gradient boosting for tabular work or PyTorch for deep learning. Reproduce one tutorial exactly, then change one thing and measure the effect.
  4. Weeks 7–8 · Second project in a new domain. Text (classify reviews), images (a small CNN with transfer learning) or time series (forecast demand). Include a per-group evaluation and a model card.
  5. Weeks 9–10 · Ship it. Save the pipeline, wrap it in a small web app or API, and write down how you'd monitor it.
  6. Weeks 11–12 · Share and read. Publish both projects (GitHub plus a short blog post), then read one classic paper or book chapter a week from the list below.
Habits that matter more than the plan
  • Always start with a simple baseline, and only keep complexity that beats it on validation data.
  • Write down what you tried and what happened — a lab notebook makes you faster and your work reproducible.
  • Explain your project to someone who isn't technical. If you can't, you don't understand it yet.

Free and well-regarded resources

Books

  • An Introduction to Statistical Learning (James, Witten, Hastie, Tibshirani, Taylor) — the classic next step for classical ML; free PDF with R and Python editions.
  • Mathematics for Machine Learning (Deisenroth, Faisal, Ong) — the maths of Lessons 2–4 in much more depth; free PDF.
  • Dive into Deep Learning (Zhang, Lipton, Li, Smola) — an interactive deep-learning book with runnable code.
  • Deep Learning (Goodfellow, Bengio, Courville) — the standard theoretical reference, free to read online.
  • Fairness and Machine Learning (Barocas, Hardt, Narayanan) — the follow-on to Lesson 21; free online.
  • Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn and PyTorch (Géron) — a practical, project-driven book (paid).

Courses and practice

  • Practical Deep Learning for Coders (fast.ai) — top-down, code-first deep learning; free.
  • Neural Networks: Zero to Hero (Andrej Karpathy) — build backpropagation and a small GPT from scratch, a natural sequel to Lessons 18–19.
  • Kaggle Learn — short, free hands-on courses, plus competitions and datasets for projects.
  • scikit-learn user guide — clear explanations and examples for every model in this course.

Keep the course useful

  • Use spaced review for a few minutes a week — it keeps the core ideas from fading.
  • Keep the cheat sheet and glossary to hand while you work on projects.
  • When you meet a new model, use Which model? and Compare to place it among the ones you know.
  • Check Recent developments for a dated overview of language models and generative AI.
