Before you start

This lesson builds on:

  • Lesson 1: What Is Machine Learning? — A model maps features $x$ to a prediction $\hat{y}$. Training adjusts its parameters to make a loss (such as MSE) small, and the real goal is doing well on new data.
  • Lesson 2: Vectors & Matrices — A vector is a list of numbers; a dataset $X$ is a matrix with one row per example. The dot product $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} = \sum w_ix_i$ is how linear models predict, and $\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|$ is the distance between two examples.

Where this fits: Calculus answers "which way should each parameter move?". It's the engine behind gradient descent (Lesson 7) and backpropagation (Lesson 18).

1.Why calculus?

In Lesson 1 you fitted a line by moving sliders and watching the loss go up or down. A computer can't "watch"; it needs a precise answer to one question:

"If I change this parameter a tiny bit, does the loss go up or down — and how fast?"

That question is exactly what a derivative answers. With derivatives, an algorithm always knows which way to adjust every parameter. This lesson teaches the small amount of calculus that ML needs: slopes, derivatives, a few rules, partial derivatives and the gradient.

2.Slope of a straight line

For a straight line, the slope is "rise over run": how much $y$ changes when $x$ increases by 1.

$$\text{slope} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

($\Delta$, "delta", means "change in".) For the line $y = 3x + 1$, going from $x=1$ to $x=2$ moves $y$ from 4 to 7, so the slope is $3/1 = 3$. A straight line has the same slope everywhere.

3.The derivative: slope of a curve at a point

Picture itYou're driving and glance at the speedometer: 60 km/h. That's not your average speed for the trip — it's how fast your position is changing right now. A derivative is the speedometer reading of any function.

A curve like $f(x) = x^2$ is steeper in some places than others. Its slope at a single point is the slope of the tangent line — the straight line that just touches the curve there.

To find it, take two points very close together, compute the slope between them, and let the gap $h$ shrink towards zero:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

$f'(x)$ (read "f prime of x"), also written $\frac{df}{dx}$, is the derivative of $f$.

Worked example — derivative of $x^2$ from scratch

$f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2$. So

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h$$

As $h \to 0$ this becomes $2x$. So $f'(x) = 2x$.

Check with numbers at $x = 3$: with $h = 0.001$, $\frac{(3.001)^2 - 9}{0.001} = \frac{0.006001}{0.001} = 6.001 \approx 6 = 2\cdot3$. ✓

Make sense of it

The situation: Suppose $x$ is how far a prediction is from the truth and $f(x) = x^2$ is its squared error. The derivative tells you how fast that error changes if you nudge the prediction.

What the result tells you: At $x = 3$ the slope is 6: move the prediction 0.01 further away and the squared error grows by about $6 \times 0.01 = 0.06$.

What if…?

  • At $x = -3$ the slope is −6: same steepness, opposite direction — the error falls if you increase $x$.
  • At $x = 0$ the slope is 0: you're at the bottom, nothing to improve.
  • Bigger $h$: the slope estimate is 7.0 with $h = 1$, 6.1 with $h = 0.1$ and 6.001 with $h = 0.001$ — the smaller the step, the closer to the true 6.

Why it matters in ML: The sign of the derivative tells a model which way to move a parameter, and its size tells how urgently. Far from the target (big $x$) the slope is steep; near the target it flattens — so learning naturally slows as it gets close.

What the derivative tells you
  • $f'(x) > 0$: the function is going up at $x$ — increasing $x$ increases $f$.
  • $f'(x) < 0$: the function is going down.
  • $f'(x) = 0$: flat — often a minimum or maximum.
  • The bigger $|f'(x)|$, the steeper the curve.

Imagine standing on a hillside in fog. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your feet. The derivative is that feeling.

Step-by-step derivationDerive the power rule for $x^3$ from the definition
  1. $$f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h}$$

    Start from the definition of the derivative.

  2. $$(x+h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3$$

    Expand the cube (multiply out $(x+h)(x+h)(x+h)$).

  3. $$\frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h} = \frac{3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3}{h}$$

    The $x^3$ terms cancel.

  4. $$= 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2$$

    Divide every term by $h$.

  5. $$f'(x) = 3x^2$$

    Let $h \to 0$: the terms containing $h$ vanish. This matches the power rule $nx^{n-1}$ with $n = 3$.

Read the formulaDefinition of the derivative
$$f'(x) = \htmlClass{fd-1}{\lim_{h\to0}}\frac{\htmlClass{fd-2}{f(x+h) - f(x)}}{\htmlClass{fd-3}{h}}$$
  • lim h→0 — make the step smaller and smaller
  • f(x+h) − f(x) — how much the output rises (the "rise")
  • h — how far we stepped (the "run")

4.Try it: the tangent and its slope

Move the point along the curve $f(x) = (x-1)^2 + 0.5$. The orange line is the tangent; its slope is the derivative $f'(x) = 2(x-1)$. Notice the slope is negative on the left, positive on the right, and zero exactly at the bottom.

5.The rules you actually need

You don't need to use limits every time. A few rules cover almost everything in ML:

RuleFormulaExample
Constant$\frac{d}{dx} c = 0$$\frac{d}{dx} 7 = 0$
Power$\frac{d}{dx} x^n = n x^{n-1}$$\frac{d}{dx} x^3 = 3x^2$
Constant multiple$\frac{d}{dx} [c\,f(x)] = c\,f'(x)$$\frac{d}{dx} 5x^2 = 10x$
Sum$\frac{d}{dx}[f + g] = f' + g'$$\frac{d}{dx}(x^2 + 3x) = 2x + 3$
Exponential$\frac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$—
Logarithm$\frac{d}{dx} \ln x = \frac{1}{x}$—
Worked example

$f(x) = 4x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x - 9$

Term by term: $4\cdot3x^2 - 2\cdot2x + 7 - 0 = 12x^2 - 4x + 7$.

Make sense of it

The situation: Picture $f$ as a cost curve. The derivative $12x^2 - 4x + 7$ gives its slope at any point, without drawing it.

What the result tells you: Each term is differentiated on its own and the results are added. The constant $-9$ disappears because a constant doesn't change.

What if…?

  • At $x = 0$ the slope is 7; at $x = 1$ it's 15; at $x = -1$ it's 23.
  • At $x = 10$ it's 1,167, and almost all of that comes from the $12x^2$ term. For large inputs the highest power dominates.

Why it matters in ML: Loss functions are built from sums of simple terms, so their gradients are too. This term-by-term rule is what automatic differentiation in PyTorch applies millions of times per second.

6.The chain rule — the engine of deep learning

Often one function is inside another: $f(g(x))$. The chain rule says: derivative of the outside (evaluated at the inside) × derivative of the inside.

$$\frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$$

Or, with $u = g(x)$: $\frac{df}{dx} = \frac{df}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$ — the "$du$"s look like they cancel, which is a handy memory aid.

Worked example

$f(x) = (3x + 1)^2$. Outside: $u^2$, inside: $u = 3x+1$.

$\frac{df}{du} = 2u = 2(3x+1)$, $\frac{du}{dx} = 3$. So $f'(x) = 2(3x+1)\cdot 3 = 6(3x+1)$.

Check at $x = 0$: $f'(0) = 6$. Numerically, $\frac{(3\cdot0.001+1)^2 - 1}{0.001} = \frac{1.006009 - 1}{0.001} \approx 6.009$. ✓

Make sense of it

The situation: A network is a chain: the input goes through one step, then another. Here the inside step multiplies by 3 and adds 1; the outside step squares.

What the result tells you: The chain rule multiplies the slopes of the steps: the outside slope $2(3x+1)$ times the inside slope 3. At $x = 0$ that's $2 \times 1 \times 3 = 6$.

What if…?

  • Make the inside steeper ($5x + 1$): the slope at 0 becomes $2 \times 1 \times 5 = $ 10. The inside factor is multiplied straight in.
  • At $x = 1$ the slope is $6 \times 4 = $ 24.

Why it matters in ML: Backpropagation is the chain rule through many layers, so gradients are products of many factors. If those factors are mostly below 1 the product shrinks towards zero (vanishing gradients); if above 1 it can blow up (exploding gradients) — Lesson 19.

A neural network is a long chain of functions inside functions. Backpropagation (Lesson 18) is just the chain rule applied over and over, from the output back to the first layer.

7.Applying it: the derivative of a loss

In plain wordsHere we use the derivative to fix a model. The derivative of the loss says whether increasing a weight makes the error bigger or smaller, so we always know which way to adjust it. Where the derivative is zero, the error is as small as it can be.

Now the payoff. Take the simplest model, $\hat{y} = w x$ (no bias), and one data point $(x, y) = (2, 6)$. The squared error is:

$$L(w) = (wx - y)^2 = (2w - 6)^2$$

Using the chain rule (outside: $u^2$; inside: $u = 2w - 6$, whose derivative is 2):

$$\frac{dL}{dw} = 2(2w - 6)\cdot 2 = 4(2w - 6)$$
  • At $w = 1$: $\frac{dL}{dw} = 4(2-6) = -16$. Negative → the loss decreases if we increase $w$. So increase $w$.
  • At $w = 5$: $\frac{dL}{dw} = 4(10-6) = 16$. Positive → decrease $w$.
  • At $w = 3$: $\frac{dL}{dw} = 0$. Flat — the minimum. And indeed $3 \cdot 2 = 6$, a perfect prediction.

The rule "move against the derivative" always takes us towards lower loss. That single idea is gradient descent.

8.Partial derivatives: more than one parameter

Real models have many parameters, e.g. $L(w, b)$. A partial derivative asks how $L$ changes when you nudge one parameter while holding all others fixed. It's written with a curly $\partial$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}, \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}$$

To compute one, treat every other variable as a constant and differentiate normally.

Worked example

$f(w, b) = w^2 + 3wb + b^2$

$\frac{\partial f}{\partial w} = 2w + 3b$ (treating $b$ as a constant)
$\frac{\partial f}{\partial b} = 3w + 2b$ (treating $w$ as a constant)

At $(w, b) = (1, 2)$: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial w} = 8$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial b} = 7$.

Make sense of it

The situation: Think of $f(w, b)$ as a model's loss with two knobs, $w$ and $b$. Each partial derivative says what happens if you turn one knob and leave the other alone.

What the result tells you: At $(1, 2)$ the loss is 11. Its slope is 8 in the $w$ direction and 7 in the $b$ direction, so the gradient is $(8, 7)$.

What if…?

  • Turn $w$ up by 0.1: the loss becomes 11.81 — up by about $8 \times 0.1 = 0.8$, as predicted.
  • Turn $b$ up by 0.1: the loss becomes 11.71 — up by about 0.7.
  • Step against the gradient with learning rate 0.1: $(1, 2) \to (0.2, 1.3)$ and the loss falls from 11 to 2.51.

Why it matters in ML: This is one step of gradient descent in miniature. Real models have millions of knobs, but the recipe is the same: compute every partial derivative, then move each parameter a little against its own slope.

9.The gradient: the direction of steepest ascent

Collect all the partial derivatives into a vector and you get the gradient, written $\nabla L$ ("nabla L" or "grad L"):

$$\nabla L = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} \\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} \end{bmatrix}$$

The gradient has a beautiful property: it points in the direction in which $L$ increases fastest. So $-\nabla L$ points downhill, towards lower loss. In the example above, $\nabla f(1, 2) = (8, 7)$.

Picture it

Think of the loss as a landscape: the ground's position is the parameters $(w, b)$, the height is the loss. At any spot, the gradient is an arrow pointing straight uphill. Training a model means walking downhill until you reach a valley — a minimum of the loss.

Advanced · optionalWhy the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent

The slope of $f$ in a direction given by a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ is the directional derivative $D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}$ (chain rule along the line $\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{u}$). By Cauchy–Schwarz,

$$\nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u} \le \|\nabla f\|\,\|\mathbf{u}\| = \|\nabla f\|,$$

with equality exactly when $\mathbf{u} = \nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$. So no direction climbs faster than the gradient, the steepest slope is $\|\nabla f\|$, and $-\nabla f$ is the steepest way down — the justification for gradient descent.

10.Preview: gradient descent in one line

Put it together and you get the learning rule used by almost every modern ML model:

$$\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta \, \nabla L(\mathbf{w})$$

"Take the current parameters, and step a little bit ($\eta$, the learning rate) in the downhill direction." Repeat until the loss stops falling. Use the Step button in the demo above to watch this happen on a curve. Lesson 7 covers it in depth.

11.Checking derivatives with code

You can always check a derivative numerically with a tiny $h$ — a habit professionals use to catch bugs:

def f(x):
    return (3 * x + 1) ** 2

def f_prime(x):          # our hand-derived answer
    return 6 * (3 * x + 1)

def numeric_derivative(f, x, h=1e-6):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)

for x in [0, 1, -2]:
    print(x, f_prime(x), round(numeric_derivative(f, x), 4))
# 0 6 6.0
# 1 24 24.0
# -2 -30 -30.0

And gradient descent on the loss $L(w) = (2w-6)^2$ from the example above:

w = 0.0
lr = 0.05                       # learning rate (eta)
for step in range(30):
    grad = 4 * (2 * w - 6)      # dL/dw
    w = w - lr * grad           # move downhill
print(round(w, 4))              # 3.0  -> the perfect weight

12.Running example: Chennai Homes

Take the simplest model, $\text{price} = w\times\text{size}$. The derivative of the loss tells us which way to move $w$: at $w = 0.4$ the slope is strongly negative (increase $w$), at $w = 0.9$ it's strongly positive (decrease $w$), and near $w = 0.65$ it's almost flat — close to the best value (about 0.6545).

Same 12 houses as Lesson 1. With so little data, treat the numbers as an illustration of the method, not as reliable estimates.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
df = pd.read_csv("houses.csv")   # download it from Lesson 1
x, y = df["size_m2"].to_numpy(), df["price_lakh"].to_numpy()
def loss(w):            # MSE for the model price = w * size
    return np.mean((w * x - y) ** 2)
def grad(w):            # dL/dw = (2/n) * sum((w*x - y) * x)
    return 2 * np.mean((w * x - y) * x)
for w in [0.4, 0.65, 0.9]:
    print(w, round(loss(w), 1), round(grad(w), 1))

Output:

0.4 1116.2 -6130.0
0.65 336.4 -109.2
0.9 1061.7 5911.7
Where this is used
  • Training every neural network and most other models
  • Sensitivity analysis: how much does an output change if an input changes?

Choose it when: Whenever a model is trained by minimising a loss.

Misconceptions to unlearn

MythA derivative of zero means you've found the minimum.

RealityIt could also be a maximum or a flat "saddle" point. For convex losses (one bowl) it is the minimum.

MythThe gradient points to the minimum.

RealityIt points in the direction of steepest increase at the current spot — not at any particular destination. That's why we take many small steps.

Responsible AI · You get what you optimise

Gradient-based learning makes the loss small — nothing else. If the measurable target is only a stand-in for what you care about, the model will optimise the stand-in (Goodhart's law: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"). Choosing the loss and the target is a value judgement, not just a technical detail.

13.Questions beginners often ask

Why do we need derivatives at all?

To know which direction to move each parameter to reduce the loss. Without them you'd have to guess, which becomes hopeless with thousands of parameters.

Will I have to differentiate everything by hand?

No. Libraries compute gradients automatically. Understanding what a gradient means is what lets you choose learning rates and diagnose training problems.

Why does gradient descent subtract the gradient?

The gradient points uphill (towards higher loss), so stepping in the opposite direction goes downhill.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Forgetting the inner derivative in the chain rule: $\frac{d}{dx}(3x+1)^2 \ne 2(3x+1)$; it's $6(3x+1)$.
  • Moving with the gradient instead of against it — that climbs uphill and increases the loss.
  • Treating the other variables as zero (instead of as constants) when taking a partial derivative.
Interview corner

Don't confuse

  • Derivative vs gradient — Gradient = all the slopes at once, as a vector.
  • Gradient vs gradient descent — One is a measurement, the other is a method.
  • Local minimum vs global minimum vs saddle point — Zero slope doesn't mean the bottom.

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

The gradient is zero. Have you found the minimum?

The trap: Assuming zero slope means the bottom.

Not necessarily: it could be a maximum, a local minimum or a saddle point. In high-dimensional neural networks, saddle points are far more common than poor local minima, and noise in SGD helps escape them.

ReLU isn't differentiable at 0. How can we train networks with it?

The trap: Thinking every function must be differentiable everywhere.

The kink is a single point that inputs almost never hit exactly. Frameworks simply use a derivative of 0 (or 1) there — a valid subgradient — and training works fine.

Why does the gradient point in the direction of steepest ascent, and why do we subtract it?

The trap: Thinking it points downhill.

The directional slope along a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ is $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$, which is largest when $\mathbf{u}$ points along $\nabla f$. To decrease the loss we move the opposite way: $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\nabla L$.

Why do frameworks compute gradients backwards (reverse mode) rather than forwards?

The trap: Not knowing there's a choice.

A network has one scalar loss and millions of parameters. Reverse mode gets all the partial derivatives of one output in a single backward pass, at about the cost of one forward pass. Forward mode would need one pass per parameter.

14.Key takeaways

  • The derivative $f'(x)$ is the slope of $f$ at $x$: how fast $f$ changes as $x$ changes.
  • Rules to remember: power rule $nx^{n-1}$, sums and constants, and the chain rule $f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$.
  • A partial derivative changes one variable while holding the others fixed.
  • The gradient $\nabla L$ is the vector of all partial derivatives; it points uphill.
  • Learning = moving parameters against the gradient: $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\nabla L$.

15.Check your understanding

1. What is the derivative of $f(x) = 5x^4$?

Power rule: $5 \cdot 4x^{3} = 20x^3$.

2. At some $w$, $\frac{dL}{dw} = -3$. To reduce the loss you should…

A negative derivative means the loss goes down as $w$ goes up. Gradient descent does $w - \eta(-3) = w + 3\eta$: it increases $w$.

3. Using the chain rule, what is $\frac{d}{dx}(2x - 5)^2$?

Outer derivative $2(2x-5)$ times inner derivative $2$: $4(2x-5)$.

4. For $f(w,b) = 3w + wb$, what is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial w}$?

Treat $b$ as a constant: $\frac{\partial}{\partial w}(3w) = 3$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial w}(wb) = b$.

5. The gradient of the loss points…

It points uphill. That's why gradient descent subtracts it.

16.Going deeper: calculus for optimisation

In plain wordsThe calculus that explains how well optimisation works: derivatives of whole layers (the Jacobian), curvature (the Hessian), why some losses have exactly one lowest point (convexity), a faster method that uses curvature (Newton's method), how to test a hand-written gradient, and how to optimise with a rule the answer must obey (Lagrange multipliers).

Intermediate · skip on a first read

The lesson above gives you derivatives, the chain rule and the gradient: enough to understand gradient descent. This section adds the tools that explain how well optimisation works: derivatives of whole vector functions (the Jacobian, used in backpropagation), curvature (the Hessian), why some losses have one clean minimum (convexity), a much faster method that uses curvature (Newton's method), how to test your own gradients, and how to optimise with constraints (Lagrange multipliers, used by SVMs and PCA).

You'll need: partial derivatives and the gradient from this lesson, and eigenvalues from Lesson 2's "Going deeper".

The Jacobian: derivatives of vector functions

The gradient handles a function that takes many numbers and returns one. A layer of a neural network takes many numbers and returns many. Its derivative is the Jacobian: a matrix with one row per output and one column per input, where entry $(i, j)$ is $\partial f_i / \partial x_j$.

For $\mathbf{f}(x, y) = \big(x^2 y,\ 5x + \sin y\big)$:

$$J = \begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y}\\[2mm] \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2xy & x^2\\ 5 & \cos y\end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(1, 2) = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 1\\ 5 & -0.4161\end{pmatrix}$$

import numpy as np

def f(v):                                  # a function from 2 numbers to 2 numbers
    x, y = v
    return np.array([x**2 * y, 5 * x + np.sin(y)])

def jacobian_formula(v):
    x, y = v
    return np.array([[2 * x * y, x**2],    # row 1: ∂f1/∂x, ∂f1/∂y
                     [5.0, np.cos(y)]])    # row 2: ∂f2/∂x, ∂f2/∂y

def jacobian_numeric(f, v, h=1e-6):
    cols = [(f(v + h * e) - f(v - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(len(v))]
    return np.column_stack(cols)

v = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
print(jacobian_formula(v).round(4))
print(jacobian_numeric(f, v).round(4))

Output:

[[ 4.      1.    ]
 [ 5.     -0.4161]]
[[ 4.      1.    ]
 [ 5.     -0.4161]]

The formula and the numerical estimate agree. Why it matters: the chain rule for vector functions is simply multiplying Jacobians. If $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}))$, then $J_{\mathbf{z}} = J_{\mathbf{g}}\,J_{\mathbf{h}}$, with shapes that line up like any matrix product. Backpropagation (Lesson 18) multiplies these Jacobians one layer at a time, starting from the loss, and it never builds the full matrices, only their products with vectors.

The Hessian: curvature in many directions

The first derivative is the slope; the second derivative is the curvature: how fast the slope changes. With many parameters, the second derivatives form the Hessian matrix $H_{ij} = \dfrac{\partial^2 L}{\partial w_i\,\partial w_j}$. It's symmetric, so (from Lesson 2's "Going deeper") it has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. Each eigenvector is a direction, and its eigenvalue is the curvature in that direction.

At a point where the gradient is zero, the eigenvalues tell you what kind of point it is:

  • all positive: a minimum (curves up in every direction);
  • all negative: a maximum;
  • some positive, some negative: a saddle point, like a mountain pass.
def hessian_numeric(g, v, h=1e-4):
    d = len(v); H = np.zeros((d, d))
    for i in range(d):
        for j in range(d):
            ei, ej = np.eye(d)[i] * h, np.eye(d)[j] * h
            H[i, j] = (g(v + ei + ej) - g(v + ei - ej) - g(v - ei + ej) + g(v - ei - ej)) / (4 * h * h)
    return H

bowl = lambda w: w[0]**2 + 10 * w[1]**2          # a long, narrow valley
saddle = lambda w: w[0]**2 - w[1]**2             # up in one direction, down in the other
for name, g in [("bowl", bowl), ("saddle", saddle)]:
    H = hessian_numeric(g, np.array([0.0, 0.0]))
    print(name, "Hessian eigenvalues:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(H).round(3))

Output:

bowl Hessian eigenvalues: [ 2. 20.]
saddle Hessian eigenvalues: [-2.  2.]

$L = w_1^2 + 10w_2^2$ has Hessian eigenvalues 2 and 20: a minimum, but in a valley that is 10 times steeper one way than the other. $w_1^2 - w_2^2$ has eigenvalues $-2$ and $2$: a saddle. In the huge loss surfaces of neural networks, most points with zero gradient are saddles rather than bad local minima, which is one reason gradient descent works better there than you might expect.

Convexity: when there's exactly one valley

A function is convex if the straight line between any two points on its graph never goes below the graph, like a bowl. Formally, for any $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}$ and any $t$ between 0 and 1:

$$L\big(t\mathbf{a} + (1-t)\mathbf{b}\big) \le t\,L(\mathbf{a}) + (1-t)\,L(\mathbf{b})$$

The practical test: a function is convex when its Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere. The big payoff is that every local minimum of a convex function is a global minimum, so gradient descent can't get stuck in the wrong valley.

  • Mean squared error for linear regression is convex. Its Hessian is $\frac{2}{n}X^\top X$, which Lesson 2's "Going deeper" showed is always PSD.
  • Log loss for logistic regression is convex (Lesson 8), and so is the SVM's hinge loss (Lesson 15).
  • Neural network losses are not convex: there are many minima and saddles, and the starting point matters (Lesson 19).

Taylor series and Newton's method

Near a point $\mathbf{w}$, any smooth function looks like a simple bowl. The second-order Taylor approximation says:

$$L(\mathbf{w} + \Delta) \approx L(\mathbf{w}) + \nabla L^\top \Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta^\top H\,\Delta$$

Gradient descent uses only the first two terms (the slope) and takes a cautious step $\Delta = -\eta\nabla L$. Newton's method uses the curvature too: it jumps straight to the bottom of the approximating bowl. Setting the gradient of the right-hand side to zero gives

$$\Delta = -H^{-1}\nabla L$$

Here are both on the long, narrow valley $L = w_1^2 + 10w_2^2$, starting from $(10, 1)$:

grad = lambda w: np.array([2 * w[0], 20 * w[1]])
H = np.array([[2.0, 0.0], [0.0, 20.0]])

for lr in [0.01, 0.05, 0.09, 0.11]:
    w = np.array([10.0, 1.0])
    for step in range(1, 10001):
        w = w - lr * grad(w)
        if np.linalg.norm(w) < 1e-6 or np.linalg.norm(w) > 1e6: break
    status = "diverged" if np.linalg.norm(w) > 1e6 else f"{step} steps"
    print(f"gradient descent, learning rate {lr}: {status}")

w = np.array([10.0, 1.0])
w = w - np.linalg.solve(H, grad(w))              # one Newton step
print("Newton's method: 1 step, lands at", w)

Output:

gradient descent, learning rate 0.01: 798 steps
gradient descent, learning rate 0.05: 153 steps
gradient descent, learning rate 0.09: 82 steps
gradient descent, learning rate 0.11: diverged
Newton's method: 1 step, lands at [0. 0.]

Gradient descent can't use a learning rate of 0.1 or more, because the steep direction (curvature 20) would overshoot and blow up. That is the stability limit $\eta < 2/20$ from Lesson 7. But with $\eta$ that small, progress along the gentle direction (curvature 2) is slow: 82 steps at best. Newton's method rescales each direction by its own curvature and, on a quadratic, lands exactly on the minimum in one step.

So why isn't Newton's method used everywhere? With $d$ parameters the Hessian has $d^2$ entries and solving with it costs about $d^3$ operations. For a model with a million weights that's impossible. Practical methods approximate the curvature cheaply instead: L-BFGS (scikit-learn's default solver for logistic regression) builds up an estimate from recent gradients, and Adam (Lesson 7) keeps a per-weight scaling.

Checking gradients like a professional

When you derive a gradient by hand, test it against a central difference, which is far more accurate than the one-sided version:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i} \approx \frac{L(\mathbf{w} + h\mathbf{e}_i) - L(\mathbf{w} - h\mathbf{e}_i)}{2h}$$

Its error shrinks like $h^2$ instead of $h$. Compare the two gradients with the relative error $\dfrac{\lVert \mathbf{g}_1 - \mathbf{g}_2\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{g}_1\rVert + \lVert\mathbf{g}_2\rVert}$, which doesn't depend on the gradient's scale. A common guide: below $10^{-7}$ is right, around $10^{-4}$ is suspicious, and above $10^{-2}$ is a bug.

# Gradient check for a tiny model: loss(w) = mean((tanh(Xw) − y)²)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(20, 3)); y = rng.normal(size=20); w = rng.normal(size=3)
loss = lambda w: np.mean((np.tanh(X @ w) - y) ** 2)

def grad_formula(w):
    p = np.tanh(X @ w)
    return X.T @ (2 * (p - y) * (1 - p**2)) / len(y)   # chain rule: tanh' = 1 − tanh²

def rel_error(a, b):
    return np.linalg.norm(a - b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) + np.linalg.norm(b))

h = 1e-5
numeric = np.array([(loss(w + h * e) - loss(w - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(3)])
print("relative error, correct formula:", f"{rel_error(grad_formula(w), numeric):.1e}")
buggy = lambda w: X.T @ (2 * (np.tanh(X @ w) - y)) / len(y)   # forgot the tanh' factor
print("relative error, buggy formula:  ", f"{rel_error(buggy(w), numeric):.1e}")

Output:

relative error, correct formula: 3.4e-11
relative error, buggy formula:   6.7e-02

The correct gradient matches to about $3\times 10^{-11}$. The buggy version, which forgot the $\tanh'$ factor from the chain rule, is off by $6.7\times 10^{-2}$, which flags it clearly. Two tips: use double precision (64-bit floats), and don't pick $h$ too small ($10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ is typical), or rounding errors take over.

Optimising with a constraint: Lagrange multipliers

Sometimes the parameters must obey a rule. PCA looks for the direction of greatest variance among vectors of length 1, and an SVM finds the widest margin while classifying every point correctly. The method of Lagrange multipliers handles an equality constraint $g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$: at the best point, the gradient of the objective must be parallel to the gradient of the constraint,

$$\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g$$

Otherwise you could slide along the constraint and still improve $f$. The number $\lambda$ is the Lagrange multiplier.

Example. Find the largest rectangle with perimeter 20: maximise $f = xy$ subject to $g = 2x + 2y - 20 = 0$. Then $\nabla f = (y, x)$ and $\nabla g = (2, 2)$, so $y = 2\lambda$ and $x = 2\lambda$, giving $x = y$. The constraint then says $4x = 20$, so $x = y = 5$ and the area is 25: the square wins.

from scipy.optimize import minimize

# Largest rectangle with perimeter 20: maximise x·y subject to 2x + 2y = 20
res = minimize(lambda v: -v[0] * v[1], x0=[1.0, 1.0],
               constraints=[{"type": "eq", "fun": lambda v: 2 * v[0] + 2 * v[1] - 20}])
print("numerical answer (x, y):", res.x.round(4), " area:", round(-res.fun, 4))

Output:

numerical answer (x, y): [5. 5.]  area: 25.0

A numerical optimiser with the constraint built in agrees. Using the same idea on PCA, maximising $\mathbf{v}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{v}$ with $\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{v} = 1$, gives $\Sigma\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$: the best direction is an eigenvector (Lesson 17). For inequality constraints such as the SVM's, the same idea extends to the KKT conditions (Lesson 15).

Hidden questions — answer in your head first, then open.

Why don't we train neural networks with Newton's method, if it converges so much faster?

The trap: "Because it doesn't work on non-convex functions."

Cost first: the Hessian has $d^2$ entries and solving with it is about $d^3$ work, which is impossible for millions of weights. It also needs the full dataset's curvature, whereas we train on noisy mini-batches. And on non-convex surfaces the Hessian isn't positive definite, so a raw Newton step can head towards a saddle or maximum. Quasi-Newton methods (L-BFGS) and adaptive methods (Adam) capture some curvature cheaply instead.

What makes a loss function convex, and which common ML losses are convex?

The trap: Saying "it has one minimum" without the test.

A twice-differentiable function is convex if and only if its Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere. Equivalently, the chord between any two points lies on or above the graph. Convex: MSE for linear models, log loss for logistic regression, hinge loss for SVMs, and these plus L1 or L2 penalties. Not convex: neural network losses, k-means' objective, mixture-model likelihoods.

What's the difference between the Jacobian and the Hessian?

The trap: Mixing up which one holds first derivatives.

The Jacobian holds the first derivatives of a vector-valued function (outputs × inputs). The Hessian holds the second derivatives of a scalar function (inputs × inputs), and it is symmetric. In fact, the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient. Backprop multiplies Jacobians; second-order optimisers use the Hessian.

In an SVM or in PCA, where do Lagrange multipliers come in?

The trap: Saying they're only a trick for textbook problems.

PCA maximises the variance $\mathbf{v}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{v}$ subject to $\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = 1$. The Lagrange condition gives $\Sigma\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$, so the components are eigenvectors and $\lambda$ is the variance. The SVM maximises the margin subject to inequality constraints. Its Lagrange multipliers (via the KKT conditions) are zero for every point except the support vectors, and its dual form is what makes the kernel trick possible.

Check your understanding: going deeper

1. A function takes 4 numbers and returns 3. What shape is its Jacobian?

One row per output (3) and one column per input (4).

2. At a point with zero gradient, the Hessian's eigenvalues are 5 and −1. What kind of point is it?

Curving up in one direction and down in another: a saddle.

3. Why is a convex loss good news for gradient descent?

There is only one valley, so you can't get stuck in a worse one. You still need a sensible learning rate.

4. On a quadratic loss, how many steps does Newton's method need to reach the minimum?

A quadratic is exactly its own second-order Taylor approximation, so jumping to the bottom of that bowl lands on the true minimum.

5. A gradient check gives a relative error of $0.05$. What should you conclude?

Correct gradients typically agree to about $10^{-7}$ or better. An error of $5\times 10^{-2}$ points to a mistake, such as a missing chain-rule factor.
