Lesson 0: Python, NumPy & pandas for Beginners

Interview

You compute MSE with np.mean((y_pred - y)**2) and get 2.33, but by hand it's 0.33. What went wrong?

Show answer

The trap: The formula is right, so people check the maths.

A shape bug. If y_pred has shape (3, 1) and y has shape (3,), broadcasting turns y_pred - y into a 3×3 matrix of every prediction minus every target. For predictions 1, 2, 3 and targets 1, 2, 4 that gives 2.33 instead of 0.33. Flatten with .ravel() and check .shape before computing losses.

Why does 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 return False, and what should you do instead?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming it's a Python bug.

Floats are stored in binary, where 0.1 can't be represented exactly; the sum is 0.30000000000000004. Compare with a tolerance: np.isclose(a, b) or abs(a - b) < 1e-9. This matters when testing model outputs or gradients.

You slice a NumPy array, edit the slice, and your original data changes. Why?

NumPy slices are views sharing the same memory. Use .copy() when you need an independent array. Python list slices, by contrast, are copies.

Lesson 1: What Is Machine Learning?

Basics

Is linear regression machine learning, or "just statistics"?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking ML means something complicated.

It's both. It learns its parameters from data to make predictions, which is exactly what ML is. Statistics and ML share many models; they differ more in goals (explaining vs predicting) than in methods.

Revisit Lesson 1 →

Basics

What's the difference between a parameter and a hyperparameter? Give two examples of each.

Show answer

The trap: Calling the learning rate a parameter.

Parameters are learned from data (linear-regression weights, a tree's split thresholds). Hyperparameters are set before training and tuned on validation data (learning rate, $k$ in kNN, maximum tree depth, regularisation strength $\lambda$).

Interview

Your model gets 100% accuracy on the training data. Is that good news?

Show answer

The trap: Celebrating.

Not by itself. It may have memorised the training data (overfitting), or a feature may leak the answer. Only a held-out validation/test score tells you how it generalises; also check the trivial baseline.

Revisit Lesson 1 →

Interview

Why do we train with one function (the loss) but report another (the metric)?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming they're the same thing.

The loss must be smooth for gradient-based optimisation (log loss, MSE). The metric should reflect the real goal (F1, recall at a fixed precision, profit) and is often not differentiable, so you can't optimise it directly.

Revisit Lesson 1 →

Interview

Why squared error rather than absolute error?

Show answer

The trap: "Because it's standard."

Squared error is differentiable everywhere and has a closed-form solution, and the best constant prediction under it is the mean. Absolute error is more robust to outliers (its best constant is the median) but has a kink at 0. Squared error also punishes big misses far more: an error of 3 costs 9 times an error of 1.

Revisit Lesson 1 →

Basics

Is k-means a classification algorithm?

Show answer

The trap: It assigns points to groups, so it sounds like classification.

No. It's unsupervised clustering: there are no labels, and the groups it finds have no names until a person interprets them. Classification learns from labelled examples.

When should you not use machine learning?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking ML is always the answer.

When simple rules work and are easier to audit; when there's little or unrepresentative data; when every decision must be explained exactly; when mistakes are unacceptable and can't be caught; or when the data will change faster than you can retrain.

Lesson 2: Vectors & Matrices

Interview

Is cosine similarity a distance? What does it ignore?

Show answer

The trap: Treating 1 − cosine as a proper distance.

It measures the angle between vectors and ignores their lengths, so (1, 1) and (100, 100) are identical to it. $1 - \cos$ is not a true metric: it can violate the triangle inequality. Use it when direction matters more than magnitude, as with text embeddings.

Revisit Lesson 2 →

Interview

Can the nearest neighbour change if you switch from Euclidean (L2) to Manhattan (L1) distance?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming distances always agree on order.

Yes. From (0, 0): the point (3, 0) is at L1 distance 3 and L2 distance 3; the point (2, 2) is at L1 4 but L2 2.83. L1 picks (3, 0); L2 picks (2, 2).

Basics

In NumPy, what's the difference between A * B and A @ B?

Show answer

The trap: Using * for matrix multiplication.

* multiplies element by element (and broadcasts). @ is matrix multiplication. A forward pass needs @; using * can silently give a wrong answer instead of an error if the shapes happen to broadcast.

Revisit Lesson 2 →

Interview

Why do we write predictions as $X\mathbf{w}$ with examples as rows?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking the order is arbitrary.

With $X$ of shape $(n, d)$ (one row per example) and $\mathbf{w}$ of shape $(d,)$, $X\mathbf{w}$ gives one prediction per row. It's a convention, but it fixes every other shape — in a neural layer, inputs $(n, d)$ times weights $(d, h)$ give outputs $(n, h)$.

Deep-dive

Two vectors have a dot product of 0. Are the features they represent unrelated?

Show answer

The trap: Equating perpendicular with independent.

Only in a limited sense. A zero dot product means they're perpendicular. If the vectors are centred (mean subtracted), it means zero correlation — still only no linear relationship, not independence.

Revisit Lesson 2 →

Deep-dive

Why does L1 regularization produce exactly-zero weights, while L2 only shrinks them?

Show answer

The trap: "Because L1 is stronger."

It's about shape, not strength. The L1 ball $|w_1| + |w_2| \le t$ is a diamond with corners on the axes. When the loss's contours first touch it, they usually touch at a corner, where some weights are exactly 0. The L2 ball is round, so the touching point is almost never on an axis. Equivalently, the L1 penalty's slope stays the same size right up to zero, so it keeps pushing until a weight reaches 0. The L2 penalty's slope $2\lambda w$ fades as $w$ shrinks.

Revisit Lesson 2 →

Interview

What is an eigenvector, and where do eigenvectors appear in ML?

Show answer

The trap: Giving only the equation, with no meaning.

A direction that a matrix only stretches, $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. In ML: PCA's components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, with eigenvalues equal to the variance in each direction. The eigenvalues of the Hessian describe a loss surface's curvature (and so how fast gradient descent converges). Spectral clustering and Google's PageRank are also eigenvector methods.

Revisit Lesson 2 →

Deep-dive

What's the relationship between PCA and the SVD?

Show answer

The trap: Treating them as unrelated algorithms.

Centre the data matrix $X$ and take its SVD, $X = U\Sigma V^\top$. The columns of $V$ are the principal components (the eigenvectors of $X^\top X$), and $\sigma_i^2/(n-1)$ is the variance along each one. Libraries compute PCA through the SVD because it's more accurate than forming $X^\top X$, which squares the condition number.

Revisit Lesson 2 →

Deep-dive

Your linear regression's weights change wildly when you add a few rows of data. What's going on, and how do you fix it?

Show answer

The trap: Blaming the optimiser or the random seed.

Probably ill-conditioning: some features are nearly linear combinations of others, so $X^\top X$ has a tiny eigenvalue and a huge condition number. Small data changes then cause big swings along that weak direction. Check the VIFs or the condition number. Fix it by removing or combining redundant features, using PCA, or adding ridge regularization, which adds $\lambda$ to every eigenvalue.

Revisit Lesson 2 →

Interview

Why is $X^\top X$ never negative definite, and when is it invertible?

Show answer

The trap: "It's always invertible because it's square."

$\mathbf{w}^\top X^\top X\mathbf{w} = \lVert X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 \ge 0$, so it is always positive semi-definite. It is invertible (positive definite) exactly when the columns of $X$ are linearly independent, which requires at least as many rows as columns. With more features than examples ($d > n$) it is always singular.

Lesson 3: Derivatives & Gradients

Interview

The gradient is zero. Have you found the minimum?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming zero slope means the bottom.

Not necessarily: it could be a maximum, a local minimum or a saddle point. In high-dimensional neural networks, saddle points are far more common than poor local minima, and noise in SGD helps escape them.

Revisit Lesson 3 →

Interview

ReLU isn't differentiable at 0. How can we train networks with it?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking every function must be differentiable everywhere.

The kink is a single point that inputs almost never hit exactly. Frameworks simply use a derivative of 0 (or 1) there — a valid subgradient — and training works fine.

Revisit Lesson 3 →

Interview

Why does the gradient point in the direction of steepest ascent, and why do we subtract it?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking it points downhill.

The directional slope along a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ is $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$, which is largest when $\mathbf{u}$ points along $\nabla f$. To decrease the loss we move the opposite way: $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\nabla L$.

Revisit Lesson 3 →

Deep-dive

Why do frameworks compute gradients backwards (reverse mode) rather than forwards?

Show answer

The trap: Not knowing there's a choice.

A network has one scalar loss and millions of parameters. Reverse mode gets all the partial derivatives of one output in a single backward pass, at about the cost of one forward pass. Forward mode would need one pass per parameter.

Revisit Lesson 3 →

Interview

What are numerical gradients good for, and why not train with them?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking they're as good as analytical ones.

They're excellent for checking a hand-written gradient (gradient checking). But they need one or two extra evaluations per parameter and suffer from rounding error, so they're far too slow and imprecise for training.

Revisit Lesson 3 →

Deep-dive

Why don't we train neural networks with Newton's method, if it converges so much faster?

Show answer

The trap: "Because it doesn't work on non-convex functions."

Cost first: the Hessian has $d^2$ entries and solving with it is about $d^3$ work, which is impossible for millions of weights. It also needs the full dataset's curvature, whereas we train on noisy mini-batches. And on non-convex surfaces the Hessian isn't positive definite, so a raw Newton step can head towards a saddle or maximum. Quasi-Newton methods (L-BFGS) and adaptive methods (Adam) capture some curvature cheaply instead.

Revisit Lesson 3 →

Interview

What makes a loss function convex, and which common ML losses are convex?

Show answer

The trap: Saying "it has one minimum" without the test.

A twice-differentiable function is convex if and only if its Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere. Equivalently, the chord between any two points lies on or above the graph. Convex: MSE for linear models, log loss for logistic regression, hinge loss for SVMs, and these plus L1 or L2 penalties. Not convex: neural network losses, k-means' objective, mixture-model likelihoods.

Revisit Lesson 3 →

Deep-dive

What's the difference between the Jacobian and the Hessian?

Show answer

The trap: Mixing up which one holds first derivatives.

The Jacobian holds the first derivatives of a vector-valued function (outputs × inputs). The Hessian holds the second derivatives of a scalar function (inputs × inputs), and it is symmetric. In fact, the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient. Backprop multiplies Jacobians; second-order optimisers use the Hessian.

Revisit Lesson 3 →

Deep-dive

In an SVM or in PCA, where do Lagrange multipliers come in?

Show answer

The trap: Saying they're only a trick for textbook problems.

PCA maximises the variance $\mathbf{v}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{v}$ subject to $\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = 1$. The Lagrange condition gives $\Sigma\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$, so the components are eigenvectors and $\lambda$ is the variance. The SVM maximises the margin subject to inequality constraints. Its Lagrange multipliers (via the KKT conditions) are zero for every point except the support vectors, and its dual form is what makes the kernel trick possible.

Lesson 4: Probability & Statistics

Interview

What's the difference between probability and likelihood?

Show answer

The trap: Using the words interchangeably.

Same formula, different viewpoint. Probability fixes the parameters and asks how probable different data are (it sums to 1 over data). Likelihood fixes the observed data and asks how well different parameters explain it (it needn't sum to 1). Training by maximum likelihood picks the parameters under which the data are most probable.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Interview

If two variables have zero correlation, are they independent?

Show answer

The trap: Yes, because nothing's going on.

No. Correlation only measures linear relationships. For $x = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2$ and $y = x^2$, the correlation is exactly 0, yet $y$ is completely determined by $x$.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Basics

Are mutually exclusive events independent?

Show answer

The trap: They seem to have nothing to do with each other.

The opposite: if two events can't both happen (and each has non-zero probability), learning that one happened tells you the other didn't. That's strong dependence.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Interview

Why divide by $n - 1$ instead of $n$ for a sample variance?

Show answer

The trap: "It's just the formula."

The sample mean is fitted to the same data, so deviations from it are slightly too small on average. Dividing by $n - 1$ (Bessel's correction) makes the estimate unbiased. For large $n$ the difference is tiny; NumPy's np.var divides by $n$ unless you set ddof=1.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Interview

A test is 90% sensitive and the disease affects 1% of people. You test positive. Are you 90% likely to be ill?

Show answer

The trap: Confusing $P(+\mid D)$ with $P(D\mid +)$.

No. With a 5% false-positive rate, $P(D \mid +) \approx 15\%$, because false alarms from the many healthy people outnumber the true positives. This is the base-rate fallacy, and it applies to fraud and anomaly detectors too.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Interview

What's the difference between standard deviation and standard error?

Show answer

The trap: Reporting the SD as the uncertainty of an average.

SD describes the spread of individual values. SE describes the uncertainty of an estimate such as the mean: $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. With $\sigma = 10$ and $n = 100$, individuals vary by about 10 but the mean is known to within about 1.

Deep-dive

A p-value is 0.03. Is there a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true?

Show answer

The trap: The most common statistics misconception.

No. It's the probability of seeing data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. It says nothing directly about how likely the hypothesis is, nor about the size of the effect.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Basics

When is the median a better summary than the mean?

Show answer

The trap: Always reporting the mean.

When data are skewed or contain outliers — incomes, house prices, response times. One extreme value can move the mean a lot (in Lesson 4, one typo moves it from 8 to 16) while the median stays put.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Interview

What's the difference between maximum likelihood (MLE) and maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation?

Show answer

The trap: Saying they always give the same answer.

MLE chooses the parameters that make the observed data most probable. MAP maximises likelihood × prior, so it also uses a belief about the parameters held before seeing the data. They agree with a flat prior or when there's lots of data. With little data, MAP is pulled towards the prior. MAP with a Gaussian prior is ridge regression, and with a Laplace prior it is lasso.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Deep-dive

What does the central limit theorem actually say, and what doesn't it say?

Show answer

The trap: "With enough data, everything becomes normally distributed."

It's about averages (and sums) of many independent values with finite variance: their distribution approaches a normal with standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. It does not make the data itself normal: 10,000 incomes are still skewed. It also needs independence and finite variance, and convergence is slow for very skewed or heavy-tailed data. It's why error bars on averages, test accuracy and A/B results work.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Interview

How does the bootstrap work, and when would you use it?

Show answer

The trap: Describing it as "making more data".

Resample your dataset with replacement to the same size, recompute the statistic, and repeat thousands of times. The spread of the results estimates the statistic's sampling variability. The middle 95% gives a confidence interval. Use it when no simple formula exists: medians, percentiles, ratios, AUC or F1 on a test set. It doesn't create information. With a tiny or unrepresentative sample, the intervals are unreliable too.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Deep-dive

Your A/B test reaches $p < 0.05$ on day 3 of a planned 14-day test. Can you stop and ship?

Show answer

The trap: "Yes, it's significant."

No. Checking repeatedly and stopping at the first significant result (peeking) inflates the false-positive rate far above 5%, because random fluctuations cross the threshold at some point quite often. Run for the planned sample size, which should come from a power calculation, or use a method designed for continuous monitoring (sequential tests or Bayesian approaches). Also watch for weekday/weekend effects, which a 3-day window can't average out.

Revisit Lesson 4 →

Deep-dive

You tried 50 model variants and the best one beats the baseline with $p = 0.03$. How much do you trust it?

Show answer

The trap: Trusting the p-value as if it were a single planned test.

Not much. With 50 tries, a few will look significant at the 5% level by chance alone (expected about 2.5). Correct for the number of comparisons (Bonferroni: $0.05/50 = 0.001$), or better, confirm on a fresh test set that wasn't used for choosing. Choosing a model on a validation set and reporting that same score is the ML version of this mistake.

Lesson 5: Data, Features & Generalization

Interview

Why do you need both a validation set and a test set?

Show answer

The trap: "The test set is enough."

Every time you use a set to choose something (model, hyperparameters, threshold), its score becomes optimistically biased. The validation set absorbs that tuning; the untouched test set gives the final honest estimate. Use it once.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Deep-dive

You select the 20 best features using all the data, then run 5-fold cross-validation. What's wrong?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking cross-validation protects you automatically.

Leakage: feature selection has already seen the validation folds' labels, so the CV score is optimistic — sometimes wildly (it can find "predictive" features in pure noise). Every learned step, including selection, scaling and imputation, must happen inside each fold — use a Pipeline.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Interview

Your training error is higher than your validation error. Is something broken?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming training error is always lower.

Not necessarily. Regularisation that's only active in training (dropout, data augmentation) makes training look worse; a small or easy validation set can score well by luck. It can also signal leakage or duplicates between the sets, so check.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Interview

Will collecting more data fix underfitting?

Show answer

The trap: "More data always helps."

Mostly no. More data reduces variance (overfitting). An underfitting model is too simple; it needs more features, a more flexible model or less regularisation.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Interview

Why shouldn't you randomly shuffle time-series data before splitting?

Show answer

The trap: Using the default train_test_split.

The model would train on the future and test on the past, so the score is optimistic. Split by time (train on earlier, validate on later) or use forward-chaining cross-validation (TimeSeriesSplit).

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Interview

What's stratified splitting, and when do you need it?

Show answer

The trap: Using random splits for rare classes.

Stratification keeps the class proportions the same in every split or fold. Without it, a fold of an imbalanced dataset might contain very few positives, making scores unstable.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Interview

The same customer appears several times in your data. How should you split?

Show answer

The trap: Splitting rows randomly.

Use group splitting (GroupKFold) so all rows of one customer go to the same side. Otherwise the model can recognise the customer instead of learning general patterns, and the validation score is inflated.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Deep-dive

What are the pros and cons of leave-one-out cross-validation?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking more folds is always better.

Each model trains on nearly all the data, so the estimate has low bias. But it needs $n$ fits, and because the training sets are almost identical, the estimate can have high variance. 5- or 10-fold is usually the better trade-off.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Deep-dive

What's nested cross-validation, and when do you need it?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking the best grid-search score is an unbiased estimate.

Two loops: an inner CV chooses hyperparameters using only the training part of each outer fold, and the outer CV scores the whole "tune then fit" procedure on data the tuning never saw. You need it when you want an honest performance estimate for a tuned model and don't have a separate test set. The best inner score is biased upwards, more so with many settings and little data.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Deep-dive

Your model scores R² = 0.95 in cross-validation but fails badly in production a month later. List likely causes.

Show answer

The trap: Blaming bad luck.

Leakage (a feature that won't exist at prediction time, or preprocessing fitted on all data). A split that doesn't match use, such as shuffled folds on time-ordered data or the same customer on both sides. Distribution shift, where production inputs differ from training (check with adversarial validation). Overfitting to the validation set through many tuning rounds. Fix: time-based or group splits, pipelines, a final untouched test set and drift monitoring.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Interview

How do you decide whether collecting more data is worth it?

Show answer

The trap: "More data always helps."

Plot a learning curve: train and validation scores against training-set size. If the validation score is still rising and there's a big gap to training (high variance), more data should help. If both are low and close together (high bias), more data won't, and you need better features or a more flexible model. You can extrapolate the curve's trend to estimate the gain.

Revisit Lesson 5 →

Deep-dive

What's the difference between covariate shift, label shift and concept drift?

Show answer

The trap: Using "data drift" for all three.

Covariate shift: the distribution of inputs $P(x)$ changes but $P(y\mid x)$ stays the same. Label shift: the class balance $P(y)$ changes but $P(x\mid y)$ stays. Concept drift: the relationship $P(y\mid x)$ itself changes. Covariate shift can be detected without labels (adversarial validation, statistical tests on features); concept drift needs fresh labels to detect, and usually retraining to fix.

Lesson 6: Linear Regression

Basics

Is $\hat{y} = w_1x + w_2x^2$ a linear regression?

Show answer

The trap: No, because it's a curve.

Yes. "Linear" means linear in the parameters $w_1, w_2$. You add $x^2$ as a feature and use the same least-squares solution.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Interview

You add a column of random noise to a linear regression. What happens to the training $R^2$?

Show answer

The trap: Expecting it to fall.

It can only stay the same or rise: least squares can always set the new weight to zero, and usually finds a tiny improvement by fitting noise (0.8476 → 0.8478 in a quick test). That's why adjusted $R^2$, or held-out $R^2$, is used to compare models with different numbers of features.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Interview

Can $R^2$ be negative?

Show answer

The trap: "It's a squared number."

On training data with an intercept, no. On test data, yes: $R^2 = 1 - \text{SS}_{\text{res}}/\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}$ goes below 0 whenever the model is worse than predicting the mean.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Deep-dive

A feature's coefficient is positive on its own but turns negative when you add another feature. Is that a bug?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming coefficients are fixed properties of a feature.

No. A coefficient is the effect holding the other features constant. With two highly correlated features, the credit is split between them and signs can flip: in a quick test, a feature's slope was +1.97 alone and −1.04 alongside a correlated partner. Interpret coefficients with care, and use regularisation or drop redundant features.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Deep-dive

Does standardising features change the predictions of ordinary linear regression?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming scaling always matters.

No. Plain least squares just rescales the coefficients to compensate, so predictions are identical (and verified so in Python). Scaling does change ridge and lasso, gradient-descent speed, and the comparability of coefficients.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Interview

Does linear regression need the features or target to be normally distributed?

Show answer

The trap: A very common myth.

No. Least squares works for any distributions. The normality assumption concerns the residuals, and only matters for exact confidence intervals and p-values — not for making predictions.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Interview

When would you use gradient descent instead of the closed-form least-squares solution?

Show answer

The trap: "The formula is always better."

The formula needs $X^\top X$ (size $d \times d$) and solving it costs about $d^3$. With very many features or too much data for memory, iterative methods (gradient descent, SGD) are cheaper and can stream the data.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Interview

What are the assumptions of linear regression, and which of them do you need just to get good predictions?

Show answer

The trap: Reciting the list without saying what each one is for.

Linearity, independent errors, normally distributed errors and equal error spread (LINE). For predictions checked on a test set, none is strictly required, although a badly wrong linearity assumption will show up as poor test error. The assumptions matter for inference: trusting standard errors, p-values and confidence intervals. Independence and equal spread affect the SEs most; normality matters only in small samples.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Deep-dive

A feature's p-value is 0.4. Should you remove it from the model?

Show answer

The trap: "Yes — it isn't significant."

Not automatically. A large p-value means the data can't pin the effect down, not that the effect is zero. It may overlap with another feature (check its VIF), the sample may be small, or domain knowledge may say it belongs. For prediction, decide with cross-validation; for explanation, report its confidence interval honestly instead of deleting it quietly.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Deep-dive

Two features are 95% correlated. What happens to the model's predictions, and to its weights?

Show answer

The trap: Saying the model becomes inaccurate.

Predictions are usually fine: the model only needs the shared information, and either feature carries it. The individual weights become unstable, with huge standard errors (high VIF), and can even flip sign between samples, so you can't interpret them. Fixes: drop or merge one feature, or use ridge regression, which splits the credit smoothly.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Deep-dive

Why do we minimise squared error rather than, say, absolute error?

Show answer

The trap: "Because squares are easier to differentiate."

That's part of it (it gives a closed-form solution), but the deeper reasons are statistical. If the noise is normal, least squares is the maximum-likelihood estimate. By the Gauss–Markov theorem, it is also the best linear unbiased estimator whenever the errors are uncorrelated with equal spread. Absolute error is the maximum-likelihood choice for heavier-tailed (Laplace) noise and is more robust to outliers; Huber loss is a compromise.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Deep-dive

What's the difference between an outlier, a high-leverage point and an influential point?

Show answer

The trap: Using the three words as if they meant the same thing.

An outlier has an unusual $y$ (large residual). A high-leverage point has unusual $x$ values (large diagonal entry of the hat matrix), so the line is forced to pass near it. An influential point actually changes the fit a lot when removed. That usually takes both: high leverage and off the line. Cook's distance measures influence directly.

Revisit Lesson 6 →

Interview

You one-hot encode a 3-level category, keep all 3 columns, and fit ordinary least squares with an intercept. What goes wrong?

Show answer

The trap: Expecting an error message every time.

Some solvers (including scikit-learn's) still return an answer, but the individual weights mean nothing. Drop one level (it becomes the baseline), or use regularization, which makes the solution unique.

Basics

What's the difference between an epoch, a batch and an iteration?

Show answer

The trap: Using epoch and iteration interchangeably.

A batch is the set of examples for one update; an iteration is one update; an epoch is one pass over all training data. 1,000 examples with batch size 100 = 10 iterations per epoch.

Interview

Does gradient descent always find the global minimum?

Show answer

The trap: "Yes, if you run it long enough."

Only for convex losses (linear and logistic regression), with a suitable learning rate. Neural-network losses are non-convex, so it finds a good-enough minimum depending on initialisation — which works surprisingly well in practice.

Your loss becomes NaN after a few hundred steps. Name three likely causes.

Show answer

Interview

Why does feature scaling speed up gradient descent?

Show answer

The trap: "Scaling is only for distance-based models."

Interview

Why is mini-batch noise sometimes helpful?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking full-batch gradients are always better.

Interview

Why shuffle the training data each epoch?

Show answer

Deep-dive

How does increasing the batch size affect training, and what's often done with the learning rate?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming bigger batches are always better.

Interview

Explain Adam in two sentences. Why is it so popular?

Show answer

The trap: Saying "it adapts the learning rate" without saying how.

Adam keeps two running averages per weight: of the gradient (momentum) and of the squared gradient. It steps along the first divided by the square root of the second, with a bias correction for the early steps. Because each weight's step is scaled by its own gradient size, it copes with badly scaled problems and needs little tuning, which suits deep networks with millions of very different weights.

Deep-dive

If Adam is so good, why do people still train some models with SGD and momentum?

Show answer

The trap: "SGD is just older."

SGD also uses less memory (Adam stores two extra numbers per weight) and has fewer moving parts. Adam's strengths are speed, robustness to scaling and less tuning, which is why it dominates for transformers, usually as AdamW.

Deep-dive

What's the difference between L2 regularization and weight decay, and why does it matter for Adam?

Show answer

The trap: "They're the same thing."

For plain SGD they are mathematically equivalent. For Adam they aren't: an L2 term in the loss gets divided by Adam's per-weight scaling, so weights with large gradients are barely regularized. Interview

Lesson 8: Logistic Regression

Basics

Why is it called logistic regression if it's used for classification?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking it's a misnomer.

It is a regression on the log-odds: $\ln\frac{p}{1-p} = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b$. The classification comes from thresholding the predicted probability.

Revisit Lesson 8 →

Interview

Why use log loss rather than MSE for logistic regression?

Show answer

The trap: "MSE works for everything."

MSE through a sigmoid gives a non-convex loss with tiny gradients when the model is confidently wrong, so learning stalls exactly when it should be fastest. Log loss is convex for logistic regression and punishes confident mistakes heavily.

Revisit Lesson 8 →

Interview

Is the decision boundary of logistic regression linear?

Show answer

The trap: "No, the sigmoid is curved."

Yes. The boundary is where $p = 0.5$, i.e. $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ — a straight line or flat plane. The sigmoid only bends the probabilities, not the boundary. Curved boundaries need extra features or another model.

Deep-dive

What happens to logistic regression on perfectly separable data without regularisation?

Show answer

The trap: Expecting a clean, finite answer.

The weights grow without limit: making them larger always makes the probabilities more confident and the log loss smaller, approaching but never reaching 0. Regularisation (sklearn's default C = 1.0) keeps them finite.

Interview

Your classifier outputs 0.9. Is the event 90% likely?

Show answer

The trap: Treating every score as a calibrated probability.

Only if the model is calibrated. Logistic regression is usually close; boosted trees, SVMs and neural networks often aren't. Check with a reliability plot and fix with Platt scaling or isotonic regression.

Interview

What's the relationship between softmax and sigmoid?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking they're unrelated.

Softmax over two classes with scores $(z_1, z_2)$ gives $\sigma(z_1 - z_2)$ for class 1. For scores (2, 0) both give 0.881.

Interview

Should the classification threshold always be 0.5?

Show answer

The trap: Using the default.

No. Choose it from the costs of each error and the class balance — a fraud system might flag at 0.1, a spam filter at 0.9 — using a validation set, never the test set.

Deep-dive

How does scikit-learn solve logistic regression, and why doesn't it use plain gradient descent?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming everything is trained with gradient descent.

The loss is smooth and convex, so second-order methods converge in a few dozen iterations. The default solver, L-BFGS, builds a low-memory approximation of the Hessian; Newton-CG and IRLS use the exact Hessian $X^\top S X$. Coordinate-descent solvers (liblinear, saga) handle L1 penalties and very large sparse data. Plain gradient descent would need careful learning rates and far more steps.

Deep-dive

What is calibration, how do you measure it, and how do you fix a badly calibrated model?

Show answer

The trap: Confusing calibration with accuracy or AUC.

A model is calibrated if predictions of $p$ come true about a fraction $p$ of the time. Measure it with a reliability diagram (binned predictions vs observed rates), the Brier score, or expected calibration error. Fix it on held-out data with Platt scaling (a sigmoid fitted to the scores) or isotonic regression. Random forests, boosted trees, SVM scores and naive Bayes often need it; logistic regression usually doesn't, unless it was class-weighted or heavily regularized.

Interview

You have 1% fraud. Would you rather resample the data, reweight the classes, or change the threshold?

Show answer

The trap: Saying resampling is always required.

All three shift the precision–recall trade-off. Reweighting or resampling changes training, which can help the model focus on rare cases but distorts its probabilities. Threshold moving keeps a calibrated model and picks the cut-off from the cost of each error type, which is usually the simplest and most transparent. Interview

Why does logistic regression need regularization on perfectly separable data?

Show answer

The trap: "To prevent overfitting" with no mechanism.

If a line separates the classes perfectly, making the weights larger always reduces log loss, because the sigmoid becomes steeper and every probability moves closer to 0 or 1. Basics

For cancer screening, would you optimise precision or recall? For a spam filter?

Show answer

The trap: Answering "accuracy".

Cancer screening: recall — missing a cancer is far worse than an extra follow-up test. Spam filter: precision — losing a real email in the spam folder is worse than letting some spam through.

Interview

Your fraud model has ROC-AUC 0.95 but the fraud team says most alerts are false. How can both be true?

A model has ROC-AUC 0.3. Is it useless?

The trap: Saying yes.

Interview

Does changing the threshold change ROC-AUC?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking AUC depends on the operating point.

What does F1 ignore, and when is that a problem?

Show answer

When would you report RMSE rather than MAE?

Show answer

How do you choose a classification threshold?

Lesson 10: Overfitting, Regularization & Bias–Variance

Interview

Why does L1 regularisation produce exactly-zero weights, but L2 doesn't?

Show answer

The trap: "L1 is stronger."

L1's penalty pulls each weight towards zero with a constant force, however small the weight, so small weights get pushed all the way to 0. L2's pull is proportional to the weight, so it fades as the weight shrinks and never quite reaches zero. Geometrically, the L1 constraint region has corners on the axes.

Revisit Lesson 10 →

Interview

Should you regularise the bias (intercept)?

Show answer

The trap: Penalising every parameter.

Usually not. The intercept just shifts predictions to the right average level; shrinking it towards 0 biases every prediction and makes results depend on where the target's zero happens to be. Most libraries leave it unpenalised.

Revisit Lesson 10 →

Interview

Why must you scale features before ridge or lasso?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking regularisation is scale-free.

The penalty acts on weight sizes, and a weight's size depends on its feature's units. Unscaled, a feature in small units needs a large weight and gets penalised more. In Lesson 10, measuring $x$ in units ten times larger changed how much of the weight survived $\lambda = 1$ from 93% to 12%.

Revisit Lesson 10 →

Interview

Does more training data reduce bias or variance?

Show answer

The trap: "Both."

Variance. More data makes a flexible model's fit more stable. Bias comes from the model being too simple, and data doesn't change that.

Revisit Lesson 10 →

Interview

Lasso keeps one of two highly correlated features and drops the other. Is that a problem?

Show answer

The trap: Treating the selected feature as "the important one".

Lasso picks between correlated features somewhat arbitrarily, and the choice can flip with small data changes. Elastic net (L1 + L2) tends to keep correlated features together. Don't read too much into which one survived.

Revisit Lesson 10 →

Interview

You choose $\lambda$ by trying values on the test set. What's wrong?

Show answer

The trap: "I'm only checking."

Choosing $\lambda$ with the test set leaks information into the model choice, so the test score is no longer an honest estimate. Use cross-validation on the training data (RidgeCV, LassoCV), then test once.

Deep-dive

Classical theory says test error rises once a model overfits. Why do huge neural networks sometimes generalise better as they grow?

Show answer

The trap: Applying the U-shaped curve everywhere.

This is "double descent": test error rises near the point where the model can just fit the training data, then falls again as models grow far larger, helped by the implicit regularisation of SGD and good architectures. The bias–variance picture is still useful but not the whole story for very large models.

The trap: Only knowing penalties.

Interview

Why is Naive Bayes called "naive", and why does it still work?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking the assumption must hold for it to work.

Can you trust Naive Bayes probabilities?

The trap: Reporting them as calibrated.

A word never appeared in any ham email during training. What happens without smoothing?

Why do implementations add log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities?

Is Naive Bayes a generative or a discriminative model? Why does it matter?

Do decision trees need feature scaling?

Lesson 14: Random Forests & Boosting

Interview

Does bagging reduce bias or variance? And boosting?

Show answer

Revisit Lesson 14 →

Interview

Will a random forest overfit if you add more trees?

Show answer

The trap: Treating trees like boosting rounds.

No. More trees make the average more stable; the test error levels off, it doesn't rise. Gradient boosting is different: more rounds can overfit, so use early stopping.

Deep-dive

Why does a random forest choose a random subset of features at each split?

Show answer

The trap: "For speed."

To decorrelate the trees. The variance of an average of $B$ trees is $\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$, where $\rho$ is their correlation. With $\rho = 0.5$ and 100 trees it's 0.505σ²; with $\rho = 0.1$ it's 0.109σ². Bootstrap samples alone leave trees too similar, because they all split on the strongest feature.

Interview

What is the out-of-bag score?

Show answer

The trap: Not knowing about it.

Each tree skips about 37% of the examples. Predicting each example using only the trees that didn't see it gives a free validation estimate, without a separate hold-out set.

Revisit Lesson 14 →

Interview

Why is boosting sensitive to label noise?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking all ensembles are robust.

Boosting keeps up-weighting the examples it gets wrong. A mislabelled example is always "wrong", so it attracts more and more attention. Bagging doesn't do this.

Interview

On tabular data, why do gradient-boosted trees often beat deep neural networks?

Trees handle these naturally and need little preprocessing; benchmarks repeatedly find tuned GBDTs (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) very hard to beat on typical tables.

Which training points determine an SVM's boundary?

Interview

What shapes of cluster does k-means find well, and which does it fail on?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking k-means finds any group.

Show answer

The trap: Picking the $k$ with the lowest inertia.

Should you standardise before PCA?

Almost always. PCA chases variance, so a feature in large units dominates. In Lesson 17, measuring one score ×10 made PC1 keep 99.8% of the variance and point almost entirely along that feature.

What's the difference between PCA and feature selection?

How are PCA and the SVD related?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking PCA requires the covariance matrix.

For centred data $X = U S V^\top$, the columns of $V$ are the principal directions and the variances are $s_i^2/(n-1)$. Libraries compute PCA via the SVD because it's more numerically stable than forming $X^\top X$.

Lesson 18: Neural Networks from Scratch

Basics

Why does a neural network need non-linear activation functions?

Show answer

The trap: "To make outputs between 0 and 1."

Without them, stacked layers collapse into a single linear layer: $W_2(W_1\mathbf{x}) = (W_2W_1)\mathbf{x}$. However deep, the network could only draw straight boundaries. The non-linearity is what lets depth build complex functions.

Revisit Lesson 18 →

Interview

What happens if you initialise all weights to zero?

Show answer

The trap: "Training just starts slowly."

Every neuron in a layer computes the same output and receives the same gradient, so they stay identical forever — the layer behaves like a single neuron. Random initialisation breaks this symmetry. (Biases can start at zero.)

Revisit Lesson 18 →

Interview

Why did sigmoid hidden layers make deep networks hard to train?

Show answer

The trap: Blaming the data.

The sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, and nearly 0 for large inputs. Backpropagation multiplies one such factor per layer, so gradients shrink exponentially in early layers: the vanishing-gradient problem. ReLU (slope 1 for positive inputs), careful initialisation, batch normalisation and residual connections fixed it.

Revisit Lesson 18 →

Interview

What is a "dying ReLU"?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking ReLU has no downsides.

If a neuron's input becomes negative for every example (often after a large update), it outputs 0 and gets zero gradient, so it never recovers. Lower learning rates, careful initialisation or Leaky ReLU help.

Revisit Lesson 18 →

Deep-dive

Why is softmax almost always paired with cross-entropy loss?

Show answer

The trap: "It's the convention."

Together their gradient with respect to the scores is just $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$ — simple, well-scaled and never stuck. For scores (2, 1, 0) with true class 2, it's (0.665, −0.755, 0.09). Frameworks combine them into one numerically stable function, so pass raw scores (logits) to it, not softmax outputs.

Revisit Lesson 18 →

Interview

Is dropout active when the model makes predictions?

Show answer

The trap: Forgetting to switch modes.

No. It's used only in training; at inference every neuron is kept (activations are rescaled so the expected values match). Forgetting model.eval() in PyTorch leaves dropout on and gives noisy, worse predictions.

Revisit Lesson 18 →

Interview

The universal approximation theorem says one hidden layer can approximate any function. So why go deep?

Show answer

The trap: Taking the theorem as practical advice.

The theorem says such a network exists, not that it's small or that training can find it. Deep networks represent many functions with far fewer neurons, by reusing features built in earlier layers.

Revisit Lesson 18 →

Interview

Your training loss doesn't decrease at all from the first step. What do you check?

Show answer

The trap: Immediately trying a bigger model.

Overfit one small batch first: a healthy network should drive its loss near zero on it. If it can't, look for bugs — wrong labels or shapes, a learning rate far too low or high, forgetting to zero gradients, applying softmax twice, or data that isn't normalised.

Lesson 19: Deep Learning Architectures

Interview

Why do CNNs need far fewer parameters than dense networks on images?

Show answer

The trap: "Convolutions are just efficient."

Weight sharing: one small kernel is reused at every position. A 3×3 convolution from 3 to 64 channels has 1,792 parameters whatever the image size; a dense layer from a 224×224×3 image to just 64 units needs 9,633,856.

Revisit Lesson 19 →

Interview

Why does attention divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$?

Show answer

The trap: "For normalisation", with no explanation.

Dot products of random $d_k$-dimensional vectors have a spread that grows like $\sqrt{d_k}$. Without scaling, large scores push softmax towards all-or-nothing, where its gradients vanish. Dividing keeps scores in a range where softmax is still sensitive.

Revisit Lesson 19 →

Interview

Why do Transformers need positional encodings?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming attention knows word order.

Attention treats its inputs as a set: shuffle the tokens and each output is just shuffled too. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would look the same. Positional encodings (fixed or learned) add order information.

Revisit Lesson 19 →

Interview

Why did LSTMs replace simple RNNs, and why did Transformers largely replace LSTMs?

Show answer

The trap: Naming them without the reasons.

Simple RNNs suffer vanishing gradients over long sequences; LSTMs' gates and cell state carry information further. Transformers then removed the step-by-step recurrence: attention connects any two positions directly and trains in parallel on GPUs.

Revisit Lesson 19 →

Interview

When fine-tuning a pretrained model on a small dataset, what should you freeze?

Show answer

The trap: Retraining everything with a high learning rate.

Start by freezing the early layers (general features like edges or word pieces), train the new head, then optionally unfreeze more layers with a small learning rate. Large updates can destroy what the pretrained weights learned.

Revisit Lesson 19 →

Interview

You augment images by flipping, then split into train and test. Problem?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking augmentation is harmless anywhere.

Flipped copies of the same image can land on both sides, so the test set contains near-duplicates of training images. Split first, then augment the training set only.

Deep-dive

What is the difference between pre-training and fine-tuning in large language models?

Show answer

The trap: Treating them as the same training.

Pre-training learns general language by next-token prediction on huge text corpora — by far the most expensive stage. Fine-tuning adapts the model with much less, targeted data (instructions, feedback, a specific domain).

Lesson 20: Feature Engineering, Pipelines & Final Project

Basics

Where should the mean and standard deviation for scaling come from?

Show answer

The trap: "From the whole dataset."

From the training data only, then reused unchanged for validation, test and production data. Computing them on everything leaks information about the test set.

Revisit Lesson 20 →

Interview

When you one-hot encode a category for linear regression, why drop one column?

Show answer

The trap: Keeping all columns by habit.

With an intercept, the one-hot columns always sum to 1, the same as the intercept column — perfect collinearity (the "dummy variable trap"). Dropping one column fixes it. Regularised models and trees don't need this.

Revisit Lesson 20 →

Deep-dive

Why is target encoding risky, and how is it done safely?

Show answer

The trap: Replacing each category with its average target on the full data.

Each row's own label contributes to its encoded value, so rare categories leak the answer and the model looks unrealistically good. Compute encodings out of fold (from other folds' data only) and add smoothing towards the overall mean.

Revisit Lesson 20 →

Interview

You oversample the minority class (e.g. SMOTE), then split into train and test. What's wrong?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking resampling is just preprocessing.

Synthetic or duplicated minority examples end up on both sides, inflating the test score. Resample the training data only, inside cross-validation, and evaluate on untouched, naturally imbalanced data.

Revisit Lesson 20 →

Interview

What's the difference between data drift and concept drift?

Show answer

The trap: Using the terms interchangeably.

Data drift: the inputs' distribution changes (younger customers, a new device type). Concept drift: the relationship between inputs and target changes (the same behaviour now means something different, e.g. after a policy change). Data drift is visible without labels; concept drift needs fresh labels to detect.

Revisit Lesson 20 →

Interview

You predict $\log(\text{price})$. Is $e^{\text{prediction}}$ the expected price?

Show answer

The trap: Simply exponentiating.

Not exactly. A model of log-price predicts the average of the logs; exponentiating gives roughly the median price, which is lower than the mean for skewed data. Correct for this if the mean matters (e.g. for total revenue).

Interview

A colleague sends you a pickled model. Any concern before loading it?

Show answer

The trap: Treating model files as plain data.

Unpickling can execute arbitrary code, so only load pickle or joblib files from sources you trust. Safer formats (such as ONNX or skops for scikit-learn models) exist for sharing.

Interview

Your model scores well offline but badly in production. What do you suspect first?

Show answer

The trap: Retraining immediately.

Training–serving skew (features computed differently online), leakage that inflated the offline score, data drift since training, or a different population or timing. Log the production features and compare them with the training data.

Lesson 21: Ethics & Fairness in Machine Learning

Basics

If you remove gender and ethnicity from the data, is the model fair?

Show answer

The trap: "Yes, it can't see them."

Not necessarily. Other features (postcode, school, first name) act as proxies, and without the attribute you can't even measure group differences. Keep it out of the inputs if appropriate, but use it for auditing.

Revisit Lesson 21 →

Interview

Can a model have equal precision, equal TPR and equal FPR across two groups?

Show answer

The trap: "Just tune the thresholds."

Not if the groups' base rates differ (unless the model is perfect). The identity $\text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot\text{TPR}$ forces at least one to differ. In Lesson 21's loan example, equalising FPR at 0.10 drops group B's precision to 0.67.

Revisit Lesson 21 →

Interview

Group B's accuracy is higher than group A's. Does that show the model treats B fairly?

Show answer

The trap: Using accuracy as the fairness measure.

No. In Lesson 21's example B had higher accuracy (0.88 vs 0.85) yet qualified B applicants were approved less often (TPR 0.60 vs 0.80). Compare the specific error rates that matter for the decision.

Interview

Is the four-fifths rule a law that defines fairness?

Show answer

The trap: Treating it as universal.

It's a US employment-selection rule of thumb for spotting possible adverse impact, not a definition of fairness and not a law everywhere. Rules differ by country and domain.

Deep-dive

How can a model's decisions make its future training data more biased?

Show answer

The trap: Thinking data is fixed.

Feedback loops: if a model sends more checks to one area, more problems are recorded there, which "confirms" the model next time. You only observe outcomes for people the model approved, too (selection bias). Monitor and occasionally explore.

General & production

Interview

How would you explain the bias–variance trade-off to a non-technical manager?

Show answer

The trap: Using formulas.

"A model that's too simple is consistently wrong in the same way, like always guessing the average (bias). A model that's too complex memorises the past and is wrong in unpredictable ways on new cases (variance). We tune complexity so it learns the real pattern but not the noise."

Interview

Your dataset has 1% positives. Walk through what you'd do.

Show answer

The trap: Jumping straight to oversampling.

Start with the right metric (PR-AUC, recall at a fixed precision, or cost), compare with the trivial baseline, use stratified splits, and try class weights. Tune the decision threshold for the real costs. Resample only if needed, and only inside the training folds.

Basics

What should you build before any sophisticated model?

Show answer

The trap: Starting with deep learning.

A baseline: predict the majority class or the mean, then a simple model such as logistic or linear regression. It tells you whether the complex model adds anything and catches pipeline bugs early.

Interview

Offline metrics improved but the A/B test shows no change. How can that happen?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming one of them is broken.

The offline metric may not track the business goal, the gain may be within noise, the test may be underpowered or too short, or users may behave differently with the new system. Offline evaluation is a filter; online experiments decide.

Interview

How do you know when to retrain a deployed model?

Show answer

The trap: "Every month", by default.

Monitor input distributions (data drift), prediction distributions, and live performance once labels arrive. Retrain on a schedule matched to how fast the data change, plus whenever monitoring flags a drop. Compare the new model against the current one before switching.

Interview

Batch or real-time predictions: how do you choose?

Show answer

The trap: Always choosing real-time.

Batch (scheduled, e.g. nightly) is simpler and cheaper when predictions can be computed in advance, such as daily recommendations. Real-time is needed when the input only exists at request time (fraud checks, search). It needs fast features and models.

Deep-dive

SHAP says feature X is the most important. Does changing X change the outcome?

Show answer

The trap: Reading explanations as causes.

SHAP explains how the model uses its inputs, not how the world works. A feature can matter to the model because it correlates with the true cause. Causal claims need experiments or causal analysis.

Interview

How do you handle noisy or wrong labels?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming labels are correct.

Measure label quality (inter-annotator agreement), inspect examples with high loss or that the model confidently "gets wrong", use robust losses or regularisation, and fix the labelling process. A few hours cleaning labels often beats weeks of model tuning.

Interview

What is training–serving skew?

Show answer

The trap: Not having heard of it.

Differences between how features are computed in training and in production — different code, different time windows, missing values handled differently. The model sees different inputs live than in training. Share one feature pipeline for both and log production inputs.

Interview

A language model gives a fluent, confident answer with a made-up reference. Why?

Show answer

The trap: "The model is lying."

It is trained to produce likely text, not to verify facts. A plausible-looking citation is likely text. Ground answers in retrieved sources (RAG), ask for citations you check, and evaluate factual accuracy explicitly.

Basics

What's the difference between a model's accuracy and its calibration?

Show answer

The trap: Assuming accurate models are calibrated.

Accuracy counts correct decisions. Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies (of cases given 70%, about 70% are positive). A model can be accurate but badly calibrated, and vice versa.

Deep-dive

How would you design an evaluation set for a model that will run for a year?

Show answer

The trap: Using a random split of historical data.

Hold out the most recent period (time-based split) so the test mimics deployment, include the edge cases and subgroups that matter, keep it fixed and versioned, and refresh it as the world changes. A random split overestimates future performance when data drift.
